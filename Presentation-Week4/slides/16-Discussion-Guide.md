# 16 Discussion guide: six anchor questions, pair cards, and the flow of the half hour

**Purpose:** the facilitator's plan for the 30-minute discussion. Six open anchor questions and one reserve, each moving between the paper's significance and its specific evidence, with several defensible positions, probes at both altitudes, a participation move, and the threads it leads to. Eight pair cards with roles. The flow of the half hour, and the moves for following what comes up instead of working down a list. **Count:** 7 anchors (3 on the spine, 3 branches, 1 reserve), 43 probes, 8 cards. **Use:** right after the talk. This is the "discussion plan" that `00-Overview.md` and slide 12.3 named as still to be written. **Written:** 2026-10-01 by the orchestrating session, from the paper, sections 01 to 15, and the course guidance on strong discussions. Sets 13 to 15 stay in use as probes and for grading; section 8 maps them. **Verification:** two independent Opus reviewers from Brief 17 (`../reports/opus-H-discussion-guide-check-1.md` and `-2.md`); their corrections are applied, see the log and open items.

The course guidance this file is built on: strong discussions move between high-level questions about significance and specific questions about method, design or evidence; ask questions with more than one defensible answer; use brief pair or small-group work to involve more people; follow interesting ideas rather than a fixed list; and spread participation across the class.

## 0. One page to run from

**The anchors, in the order of the spine (1, 3, 6), the branches (2, 4, 5) taken when the room raises them, and one reserve (7)**

1. **Whose gestures are these?** When this method asks users, whose habits is it recording, and should a designer build on them?
2. **What does an agreement score tell a designer?** Where would you stop trusting it?
3. **What would convince you the method works?** Did this paper deliver that?
4. **Was the monologue the right experiment?** What did removing feedback buy and cost?
5. **Who was not at the table, and how far does that reach?** Which parts of Figure 4 would survive a different room?
6. **Seventeen years on, what did this paper give us?** Did its influence run ahead of its evidence?
7. **Reserve: who bends, the recognizer or the user?** Did the paper's technology advice follow from its data?

**The half hour.** Warm-up elicitation, 5 min: move a little by hands in three with the grouping vote and the score computed twice, then undo tallied on the board. Pairs, 5 min, one card each with a question and two roles. Group, 20 min: harvest the pairs (2 min), Anchor 1 from the warm-up data (6 min), Anchor 3 or whichever branch is hot (6 min), Anchor 6 as the closer with a one-sentence round (5 min). About 4 minutes of buffer come from the talk.

**Moves.** Open each anchor on a concrete detail or on the room's own data, then widen. Hands in three options, never two. Harvest pairs by asking who heard something from their partner they disagreed with. Credit a classmate's commentary by name and let its author speak first. Park a tangent on the board and come back to it. Cold-call with a low-stakes question first (what did you do in the warm-up). If the room piles on the paper, appoint a defender. Close each anchor with a student's sentence, not yours.

**Numbers to have ready.** Agreement: square each group's share of 20, add (Eq. 1, p. 1087); floor 0.05, ceiling 1; select single 11, 3, 3, 3 gives 0.37 (Eq. 3, p. 1087); overall 0.32 one hand, 0.28 two hands (p. 1087). About 72% mouse-like (p. 1090). Designers: 43.5%, 60.9%, 19.1% (p. 1090). One hand preferred for 25 of 27 (p. 1088). 48 gestures for 22 referents, 57.0% coverage (pp. 1087, 1088). Planning about 8 s simple, 15 s complex; easy-rated gestures 3.4 s, difficult-rated 2.0 s (p. 1088). 216 later studies; 20 participants the most common choice, "likely" because of this paper (DIS 2020, p. 859). Patent application filed 2008-08-04, abandoned 2014-12-09, not in the paper.

## 1. What the guidance asks for, and how this file does it

| Guidance | How the guide does it |
|---|---|
| Move between high-level and specific questions | Every anchor has a "zoom in" list (a page, a figure, a number) and a "zoom out" list (significance, today's devices, the student's own research). The spine alternates: Anchor 1 is high-level and lands on the warm-up data; Anchor 3 is evidence; Anchor 6 is significance |
| More than one defensible answer | Each anchor lists three to five positions with the strongest evidence for each, and at least one position defends the paper. Stems ask "whose," "what would it take," "where does it stop," not "is it" |
| Brief pair or small-group work | The pair phase uses role cards: one question, two roles, so both partners have something to say and the disagreement is built in. Anchor 3 has a 90-second pair sentence completion and Anchor 4 a 60-second neighbour split inside the group phase |
| Follow ideas rather than a list | The spine is three anchors; the other three are branches with a three-minute version each. Section 7 is a thread map: what someone says, where it leads |
| Participation across the class | Role cards give quiet students a position to speak from. Three-way hands, a Figure 4 round where everyone speaks once, the closing one-sentence round, the "who heard a view they disagreed with" harvest, and a warm cold-call |

## 2. The warm-up, used as data

Slide 12.3 fixes the warm-up: show the effect of two commands, one from the top of Figure 3 (move a little) and one from the tail (undo or help), everyone performs the gesture they would use on the desk, tally the groups on the board, compute the agreement score together (Eq. 1, p. 1087). Three things to do during it so the anchors can draw on it.

- **Force one grouping decision in public, on move a little.** Nearly everyone will drag, some with one finger and some with two or more. Tally by hands in three (one finger, several fingers, something else) and ask the room to vote whether a one-finger drag and a two-finger drag are the same gesture. The paper never says how grouping treated finger count (p. 1087; pitfall 11). Two hints: in the taxonomy a touch with several fingers at one point is still a one-point touch (p. 1086), and in the final set a gesture drawn with one finger "could be performed with 1-3 fingers" (Figure 4 caption, p. 1089). Compute A both ways and leave both numbers on the board. For undo, tally the groups on the board and compute A once. Illustration with made-up numbers: 16 of 20 drag, 12 with one finger and 4 with two, and 4 others each do something different. All drags as one group: (16/20)² + 4 x (1/20)² = 0.65. Split by fingers: (12/20)² + (4/20)² + 4 x (1/20)² = 0.41. One decision moves the score from 0.65 to 0.41. Anchor 2 starts here.
- **Mark the smartphone-era gestures.** For undo, expect a shake, a three-finger swipe, a drawn arrow, a pantomimed keyboard shortcut. Circle them on the board. Anchor 1 starts here: they are this room's "I'm a child of the mouse" (p. 1090).
- **Note who did what.** Two or three people who made an unusual gesture are your first warm cold-calls: "You did the arrow. Where does that come from?" Nobody can get that wrong, and it brings in voices before the arguments start.

## 3. The six anchors

Format for each: **Ask** (the stem as spoken), **Why it matters**, **Positions people can defend** (the strongest evidence for each, page or section), **Zoom in**, **Zoom out**, **Move**, **Threads**, **Three-minute version**, **Traps**, **[classmate] hook**. "The paper says" marks the authors' claims; "we argue" marks ours.

### Anchor 1. Whose gestures are these?

- **Ask:** "Everyone in this study was screened for never having used an iPhone or a tabletop, and still about 72 percent of what they did was mouse-like. Just now, [number from the warm-up] of us produced a smartphone gesture for undo. So when this method asks users, whose habits is it recording, and is that what a designer should build on?"
- **Why it matters:** this is the paper's central promise, gestures "informed by user behavior" (p. 1083), set against the course readings' warning that evaluation converges on the familiar. It decides whether elicitation is a design method or a mirror.
- **Positions people can defend**
  1. **Build on it; that is the point.** The paper says the target is public tables "where the importance of immediate usability is high" (p. 1085). A first-time user has only the habits they bring. The paper reports the desktop pull as a finding, not an embarrassment ("desktop idioms strongly influence users' mental models," p. 1083; "It's a Windows World," p. 1090), and designs for it: a widget as well as a gesture where agreement is low, and widgets for commands where participants tapped imaginary ones (p. 1091). Design for the people who exist.
  2. **It locks in the past.** Greenberg and Buxton: we "converge on that which we can measure, which is almost always that which we are already familiar with" (CHI 2008, p. 115; section 10). Consensus among novices is a vote for yesterday. Pinch is in the set (Enlarge 3, Figure 4, p. 1089), but it was already shipping on the iPhone from June 2007, and we argue the market, not elicitation, made it universal; the study cannot tell the two apart (section 11). In 2014 Morris, Wobbrock and colleagues named the problem "legacy bias" and proposed fixes (section 11), so two of the three authors came to treat this as a limit of the method.
  3. **It measures the method as much as the users.** The 27 referents were "obtained from desktop and tabletop systems" (p. 1085) and include cut, paste, minimize, maximize and task switch (Table 1, p. 1086). Every participant had to produce a two-hand gesture for every referent (p. 1086). The screening named "an interactive tabletop, Apple iPhone, or similar" (p. 1085); no touch history was recorded, so trackpads, Palm Graffiti, ticket machines and the Nintendo DS went unasked (section 06). "Unrevised behavior" means unrevised by feedback (p. 1085), not habit-free. We argue the study measured the repertoire people brought in, and the paper calls that a fact about users (12.1).
  4. **It is a snapshot, and that is fine if you say so.** A 2008 sample gives 2008 habits; this room gave smartphone habits. The method is a thermometer, not a theory. The paper itself says "children, Eastern, or uneducated participants would behave differently" (p. 1091). Rerun it per population and per decade, and stop calling the output "the users' gestures."
- **Zoom in**
  - "The paper removed Windows and Macintosh elements 'to avoid bias' (p. 1085). Which bias did that remove, and which did it leave untouched?" (It removed the prompt cue Epps et al. had, p. 1084. It left the habits.)
  - "Replace cut, paste, minimize, maximize and task switch with referents native to a table. Can you? What does it mean if you cannot?"
  - "What would you have asked the twenty people about their past devices, and what would you have done with the answer?" (Section 11's first open question: record exposure as a covariate.)
  - "Some participants tapped an object to select it, then gestured on the same object, which the paper says negates 'a key benefit of gestures that couples selection and action' (p. 1090). Is that a habit to design around, or to design out?"
- **Zoom out**
  - "Should interaction design follow users or lead them? Where is the line between user-centred and user-captured?"
  - "If you ran this study today for an AR headset or a car, what legacy would you be recording? Is recording it a problem or the job?" (Production in-car gesture control began with the BMW 7 Series in 2015; progress.md.)
- **Move:** start with the circled smartphone gestures from the warm-up and the people who made them; that answers the first half of the stem descriptively. Then hands in three on the second half: "build on what people bring," "design against it," "rerun it per population and say so." The three corners are positions 1, 2 and 4, so the paper's defence has a corner from the first minute. One voice from each, then open.
- **Threads:** "it's the referents" leads to Anchor 4; "it's culture" to Anchor 5; "so what, it is still useful" to Anchor 6; "Apple just shipped pinch" to card H and Anchor 6.
- **Three-minute version:** the stem, the three-way hands, one voice per group, close with "so is elicitation a design method or a mirror?"
- **Traps:** do not say "unrevised behaviour means no prior habits" (pitfall 1). Say "72 percent of gestures," never "of participants." "I'm a child of the mouse" is one participant. The authors did not miss the desktop pull; they report it. "Users adapt within minutes" has no source in the paper; say it as common experience.
- **[classmate] hook:** a commentary on legacy bias, on the screening criterion, or on the desktop referents. Read one sentence of it, credit the author, ask them to take the first position.

### Anchor 2. What does an agreement score tell a designer?

- **Ask:** "To score our warm-up we had to decide whether two gestures were the same, and the number moved when we changed our minds. The paper had to make decisions like that across 1,074 gestures, sorted within 27 referents for one hand and for two, and tells us only that each group 'held identical gestures.' So what does an agreement score tell a designer, and at what point would you stop trusting it?"
- **Why it matters:** the score is the method's engine. Everything downstream, which gesture wins, which referent gets a widget, how later studies compare, rests on it. The paper says the process was "adopted from prior work" (p. 1087); the class should see where judgment enters a number.
- **Positions people can defend**
  1. **A sound, honest summary.** The paper prints the formula, two worked examples and the range (p. 1087). Squaring rewards one dominant group, which is what a designer wants to find. The pattern is robust: both move referents at 1.0, then a cliff, then a tail (Figure 3, p. 1088); complexity correlates inversely with agreement, r = -0.52 (p. 1087). The field refined the score rather than discarding it (Vatavu and Wobbrock 2015, section 04).
  2. **The hidden step is grouping.** "Identical" is never defined (p. 1087). The authors grouped and coded the gestures themselves, and the paper reports no second coder and no reliability check (pp. 1086 to 1087; section 09). The taxonomy counts a touch with several fingers at one point as one-point "as our participants often did" (p. 1086), and Figure 4 lets one-finger gestures be made with 1 to 3 fingers (p. 1089). We argue finger count was merged in grouping, which raises A; the paper does not say (pitfall 11). The room's two numbers show how much one such decision moves.
  3. **It measures consensus, not guessability.** A says how many people invented the same thing. It does not say whether a new person would guess the chosen gesture. Wobbrock's 2005 EdgeWrite study measured guessability directly (section 04); this paper borrowed the elicitation and the score and left guessability as "an important next step" (p. 1091). Coverage, 57.0% of all proposals (p. 1087), is a third number again.
  4. **The low end is the most useful output.** The paper's design advice came from where agreement fell: a widget as well as a gesture beyond the first seven referents, and widgets for delete, zoom in, zoom out, accept, reject, menu access and help (p. 1091). "Our user-defined set is useful, therefore, not just for what it contains, but also for what it omits" (p. 1088). Disagreement tells you where not to use a gesture.
- **Zoom in**
  - "Select single: the largest group was eleven of twenty, a tap by Figure 4 (our reading); nine did something else in three groups of three; A = 0.37 (Eq. 3, p. 1087). Figure 4 keeps tap and lasso (p. 1089). Is 0.37 'agreement'? Where would you set the line between 'gesture' and 'widget'?"
  - "With twenty people the floor is 0.05. The tail of Figure 3 runs from about 0.21 for task switch down to about 0.11 for cut (p. 1088, chart readings). How many people changing their minds would reorder cut, next and reject?"
  - "Where two referents claimed the same gesture, 'the referent with the largest group won the gesture' (p. 1087). The paper does not say what the loser got, and the set is 'conflict-free' partly because one gesture means enlarge on an object, zoom in on the background and open on a container (p. 1088), which needs hit-testing (p. 1091). Did users agree, or did the authors overload gestures by target?"
  - "Every correlation in the paper runs against the three authors' own complexity ratings, made before the study (Table 1, p. 1086): r = -0.52 with agreement (p. 1087), 0.71 with planning time and -0.59 with goodness (p. 1088). The paper concedes that participants and the authors did not always regard the same referents as complex (p. 1090). Where does designer judgment re-enter a user-defined method?"
  - "The chi-square tests count 1,074 gestures as independent observations; twenty people made them (pp. 1087 to 1088). Which claims does that weaken most?"
- **Zoom out**
  - "Is consensus among twenty strangers the right criterion for a gesture? What else could you optimise: learnability and memorability (p. 1091, citing Wu et al.), separability for the recognizer (Long et al., p. 1084), speed, comfort?"
  - "Vatavu and Wobbrock's corrected rate turns this paper's 0.32 into 0.284 (section 04). If the numbers change and the ranking does not, did anything change?"
  - "Think of a number in your own research that hides a judgment call like this grouping. What would you have to report next to it for a reader to trust it?"
- **Move:** the live grouping vote from the warm-up is the opener; point at the two numbers on the board. Later, fingers: "hold up one to five fingers for how much you would trust A to pick a gesture for a public kiosk," then ask a one and a five to explain. (The paper says "the number of fingers rarely matters," p. 1088; expect a laugh.) If anyone arrived late, ask them to guess which command the room's winning undo gesture was for: guessability in one try, a different measurement from agreement.
- **Threads:** "then what should they have measured" leads to Anchor 3; "the designers could not have guessed this" to Anchor 3's designer comparison; "widgets, then" to Anchor 4's probe on imaginary widgets.
- **Three-minute version:** the two numbers on the board, the stem, one probe (0.37, gesture or widget), close with "what would you want reported next to every A?"
- **Traps:** A is not a percentage (pitfall 2); "0.32 agreed" is wrong. The 0.42 example is move a little with two hands; one-hand move is 1.0 (pitfall 5). The finger-merging claim is our inference. Do not call the goodness ratings circular; participants did not know group sizes (12.2, Watch out).
- **[classmate] hook:** a commentary that questioned the score, the grouping, or the statistics.

### Anchor 3. What would convince you that the method works?

- **Ask:** "The paper says popularity 'goes a long way to validating' its approach, based on ratings people gave their own gestures. Each author, designing alone, came up with only 43.5 percent of the users' final set; all three together, 60.9 percent. The guessability test the authors proposed was not run in this paper. What would it take to convince you that a design method works, and did this paper deliver it?"
- **Why it matters:** this is the thesis of slide 12.1 put to the room as a question instead of a verdict, and it is where the course readings on evaluation (Greenberg and Buxton, Wobbrock and Kientz, Olsen) earn their place.
- **Positions people can defend**
  1. **Yes, for what it claimed.** The paper lists four contributions: a characterization with a taxonomy, a gesture set, insight into mental models, implications for technology and design (p. 1084). It says validation of the set is "an important next step" (p. 1091). Greenberg and Buxton: early evaluation "can mute creative ideas that do not conform to current interface norms" (CHI 2008, p. 111). Wobbrock and Kientz: artifacts need not always come with studies (Interactions 2016, p. 40). The designer comparison shows that experts do not predict users: each author matched 43.5% of the users' set, all three 60.9% (p. 1090). The 2010 follow-up added blind judges, 22 of them over 81 gestures, who preferred gestures authored by larger groups, partly because the researchers' gestures were more complex (sections 04, 11).
  2. **No; the claims run ahead of the evidence.** The Conclusion calls the set "a good candidate for deployment" with "ease of recognition" (p. 1091) though no recognizer was built. The validation offered is self-report: the people who invented a gesture rated it (p. 1088), and the test treats 1,074 gestures from twenty people as independent (p. 1088). The designer comparison measures overlap with the users' set, not quality; the yardstick is the users' set, so designers cannot win, and the paper's own narrower conclusion is that "three experts cannot generate the scope of gestures that 20 participants can" (p. 1090; section 05). The 2010 study tested preference, not guessability, and the preference was partly explained by the researchers' gestures being more complex (pitfall 6). Wobbrock and Kientz: methods need "repeated validation" (p. 41).
  3. **The field validated it by adoption, which is a different thing.** By 2020 a review counted 216 elicitation studies and called this paper the first application of the guessability method to hand gestures and the most cited (DIS 2020; section 04). Adoption shows utility, not validity. We argue the 20-participant norm, which the review says is "likely explained" by this paper's choice of 20 (p. 859), is the field inheriting a habit, a legacy bias of its own.
  4. **The right test was named, and it is still the right test.** Unlabeled clips of the users' gestures and of the designers' gestures shown to 20 new people, who guess the command, "from signs to referents" (p. 1091). Design it in the room: what result makes each position concede? Whether it was later run for this set was not checked (section 11).
- **Zoom in**
  - "The hypothesis was that the 'wisdom of crowds' would generate a better set than the authors (p. 1090). What does 'better' mean in that sentence, and against what was it measured?"
  - "19.1% of each author's gestures were never tried by any participant: the authors are 'either thinking creatively or are hopelessly lost!' (p. 1090). Which? How would you find out?" (Elicitation as floor or ceiling, 12.2.)
  - "Ratings were given right after each gesture, before anyone knew group sizes (p. 1088). So the validation is not circular. What exactly is the weakness?" (No independent judge; the inventor rates; the independence of the 1,074.)
  - "Olsen's three steps for 'empowering new design participants': describe the population, show existing tools fail it, show the new tool is more effective. Which steps does the paper complete?" (Section 10: step 1 yes, step 2 arguably, step 3 only self-ratings.)
- **Zoom out**
  - "How does any HCI method become standard? Think-aloud, heuristic evaluation, the System Usability Scale. Does a method need a validation study, or a thousand users?"
  - "If the reverse test found the designers' gestures guessed just as well, would the method be wrong, or just unnecessary?"
- **Move:** pairs get 90 seconds to finish the sentence "This method is validated when ___." Collect five answers aloud and sort them on the board: self-report, independent judges, guessability, recognizer performance, adoption. Then ask which column the paper filled.
- **Threads:** "peer review let this through" leads to Anchor 6 (what CHI rewarded in 2009); "Microsoft" to Anchor 6, position 5, with the facts-only rule; "the 2010 study" to the preference-versus-guessability probe.
- **Three-minute version:** the stem, a 60-second sentence completion, three sentences aloud and no board sort, close with "which column would you demand before you shipped?"
- **Traps:** GI 2010 is not "users preferred user-authored gestures" (pitfall 6). The designer comparison is overlap, not quality (pitfall 14). "Never validated" means not validated for guessability in this paper; do not claim later work never did it. "Circular" is wrong.
- **[classmate] hook:** a commentary on validation, on the designer comparison, or on the self-ratings.

### Anchor 4. Was the monologue the right experiment?

- **Ask:** "The authors turned the user-computer dialogue into a monologue on purpose: nothing responded, nothing failed, nobody could go back. They say this captured 'unrevised behavior.' What did that choice buy, what did it cost, and how would you have run it?"
- **Why it matters:** the three design choices (ask users, show the effect first, no recognizer) are what later studies copied. The class should be able to say which of the paper's findings are facts about people and which are facts about a room with no feedback.
- **Positions people can defend**
  1. **Right call: first instincts are the product.** The paper says a recognizer would have let "the vicissitudes of gesture recognition" shape behaviour, so it removed the gulf of execution and created "a monologue in which the user's behavior is always acceptable" (p. 1085). Epps et al. conceded that their Windows prompts "may have biased" people toward the mouse (p. 1084). Public tables need "immediate usability" (p. 1085); we argue the first attempt is the one that counts there.
  2. **It cost the most interesting data.** The paper concedes that participants "could not change previous gestures," that seeing all referents first might have changed choices (p. 1091), and that "interactive systems that provide feedback will implicitly remind users to remain in contact with the table" (p. 1091). One participant used more fingers "to ensure that I was pressing," which the authors say "may be, at least in part, due to the lack of feedback from the table" (p. 1090). We argue part of the finger finding, and perhaps the above-table and off-screen behaviour, is an artefact of the setup. People adapt (common experience, not a finding of the paper); the data say nothing about the second use.
  3. **The forced structure shaped the set.** Everyone made a two-hand gesture for every referent (p. 1086) though one hand was preferred for 25 of 27 (p. 1088); 17 of the 48 set gestures are two-handed (p. 1088). Referents came one at a time in random order (p. 1086). The set's "consistency and symmetry" (p. 1088) came partly from the authors' unification step: the paper says of participants' simplified mental models, "This allows us to unify the gesture set" (p. 1090). We argue the method and the authors' hands are both in the output.
  4. **The hybrid, which is what came next.** Morris and colleagues in 2014 proposed production (several proposals per referent), priming (show what the device can do) and partners (work in pairs) (section 11). Each puts something back into the monologue. A two-phase study would run the monologue first, then let people revise with feedback, and measure what changes.
- **Zoom in**
  - "Close and task switch got no gesture because people tapped imaginary widgets; 'a common gesture was not feasible' (p. 1087). Is that a failure of the referents, of the monologue, which offered no widget to tap, or a real finding that some commands want buttons?"
  - "Participants were told to touch the table, and one still crossed her hands with the top hand about 3 inches above it (p. 1090). With feedback that would vanish. Does the recommendation to sense above the table (p. 1091) rest on an artefact?"
  - "Opposite referents got reversible gestures although they were never shown as pairs (p. 1090). Is that evidence that the monologue still captured something systematic?"
  - "Insert and maximize had the highest two-hand agreement, about 0.58 each (Figure 3, p. 1088), and tied on hand preference, yet got no dedicated gesture because people reused dragging and enlarging (pp. 1087 to 1088). Did the method find them redundant, or did the authors decide it?"
- **Zoom out**
  - "What is 'natural' interaction if people's first instinct is the mouse? Weiser: 'The most profound technologies are those that disappear,' and they disappear because 'whenever people learn something sufficiently well, they cease to be aware of it' (Weiser 1991, opening paragraphs). Does a gesture you have to guess count as disappearing, or only one you have learned?"
  - "Where else do we study first behaviour: first-click tests, onboarding analytics? When is the first attempt the right target, and when is the learned one?"
- **Move:** split the room by seat in three. Left third argues "first instinct is the product," right third "learned behaviour is the product," middle third designs the two-phase study; 60 seconds with a neighbour, one or two voices from each third, the hybrid last and from someone who has not spoken.
- **Threads:** "fingers" leads to section 05's evidence (13 of 20 varied finger count, p. 1090) and the "ensure that I was pressing" probe; "widgets" to Anchor 2, position 4, and Weiser's doubt about a menu bar on a board (section 10); "what would you run instead" to the closer in Anchor 6.
- **Three-minute version:** the stem, the three-way seat split, one voice from each third, the hybrid last.
- **Traps:** "unrevised" means unrevised by feedback (pitfall 1). The touch instruction is reported under Results, not Procedure (p. 1090). The paper does not say whether the one-hand or the two-hand gesture came first; claim no order. "Adapt within minutes" is common experience, not a finding.
- **[classmate] hook:** a commentary on the no-recognizer choice, on feedback, or on the two-hand requirement.

### Anchor 5. Who was not at the table, and how far does that reach?

- **Ask:** "Twenty right-handed, literate American adults, tested one at a time, on a table that groups can share. The authors say children, Eastern or uneducated participants 'would behave differently.' Where does that limitation stop: with Figure 4 on the screen, which of these gestures would you not ship in a different room, and why?"
- **Why it matters:** the set is offered as "a good candidate for deployment" (p. 1091) on public tables. Public means everyone. This anchor asks the class to size a limitation the authors state but do not measure.
- **Positions people can defend**
  1. **It is local to the symbols, and already hedged.** Symbolic gestures were .100 of all proposals (Figure 2, p. 1087). The culture-bound items are identifiable: accept "draw check," reject "draw 'X'," help "draw '?'" (Figure 4, p. 1089). The paper already suggests widgets for accept, reject and help (p. 1091), and the set already uses aliasing (p. 1088), so we argue a locale could add its own mark. Physical gestures were the largest nature category, 43.9% (p. 1091); a drag is a drag anywhere. Swap the marks and the set travels.
  2. **Culture reaches into the "neutral" gestures and into consensus itself.** Next is drawn left to right (Figure 4, p. 1089); we read that as reading direction, an inference. The imaginary close button at the top right and taskbar at the bottom (p. 1090) are software culture. We argue "Oh, that's the same as enlarge" (p. 1090) may be a desktop education speaking; the paper reads it as a simplifying assumption. And the agreement score was computed inside one culture, so, we argue, what counted as consensus is cultural too. In Japan a circle marks correct and a check can mark wrong; in Bulgaria a nod means no (section 06).
  3. **The bigger omission is the group.** The paper says Morris et al.'s cooperative gestures "address groups of users and omit issues relevant to single-user gestures, which we cover here" (p. 1084). Minimize 2 moves the object "to the bottom of the surface (as defined by user's seating position)" (Figure 4, p. 1089): four people, four directions. Pan and zoom change the whole surface for everyone. What is "agreement" when the effect of a referent differs by seat?
  4. **Body and ability.** All participants were right-handed (p. 1085; Figure 2 caption, p. 1087), and no result is reported by handedness or age. Figure 2 splits form by hand for two-hand gestures (caption, p. 1087): the left hand shows more one-point touches and fewer one-point paths than the right (.343 against .274 and .379 against .450, printed in the figure). We read that as the non-dominant hand holding while the other acts, a difference of role as much as of hand; how one-hand gestures enter the two columns is not stated, and what left-handed users would do is unknown. The set includes drawn marks and a corner drag for rotate (Figure 4), while the paper itself warns that "demands for precise positioning should be avoided" (p. 1091) and found that gestures rated easy took longer, "perhaps because they were smoother or less hasty" (p. 1088; the precision reading is ours, 07.2). An older, left-handed or motor-impaired person at a public table is exactly the walk-up case.
- **Zoom in**
  - "Take Figure 4 panel by panel. Which gestures survive in Tokyo, which in a class of eight-year-olds, which for a left-handed user? One reason each." (Also card E.)
  - "Mean age 43.2 with a standard deviation of 15.6 (p. 1085) means at least one person under about 28 or over about 58 (our arithmetic, section 06), but no result is broken down by age. What would you expect age to change?"
  - "These were adults who learned computing on the mouse. Would twenty 20-year-olds in 2008 have behaved differently? Would twenty 20-year-olds today?"

### Anchor 6. Seventeen years on, what did this paper give us?

- **Ask:** "By 2019 this was the most cited gesture elicitation paper, and a 2020 review that counted 216 such studies calls it the first to apply the guessability method to hand gestures. On the other side: no product we could find shipped its gesture set; pinch was already shipping on the iPhone; and twenty participants became the field's most common sample, likely because this paper used twenty. Seventeen years on, what should we credit this paper with, and did its influence run ahead of its evidence?"
- **Why it matters:** this is the course's third goal, broader implications, and the natural closer. It also turns the presenters' thesis (12.1) into a question the room answers.
- **Positions people can defend**
  1. **The method, and that is a lot.** The abstract presents "an approach to designing tabletop gestures" (p. 1083). Wobbrock's 2012 whitepaper lists this paper as an example of a methodological contribution (section 10). A review of 216 studies calls it the first application of the guessability method to hand gestures and the most cited (DIS 2020; section 04). A method hundreds of people used is a contribution whatever this one paper's validation looked like.
  2. **The concrete findings mattered more.** "Gestures should not be distinguished by number of fingers" and the four-finger boundary (p. 1091); one hand preferred for 25 of 27 (p. 1088); "demands for precise positioning should be avoided" (p. 1091); widgets for low-agreement commands (p. 1091). A practitioner could use these the next morning. The method is for researchers.
  3. **A shared language.** The four-dimension taxonomy (Table 2, p. 1086) and the agreement number gave later studies a way to code and compare. The score became a unit, corrected in 2015 (section 04). The lasting gift is comparability.
  4. **Influence ran ahead of evidence, and the field inherited habits.** The set went nowhere as published (our judgment, section 11). The n = 20 norm came from this paper "likely" (DIS 2020, p. 859). A 2014 article by Morris, Wobbrock and colleagues, two of this paper's authors, named legacy bias as a limit of the method (section 11). The Conclusion's "good candidate for deployment" with "ease of recognition" (p. 1091) was not tested in the paper; no recognizer was built. A paper can be important and under-evidenced at once; the question is whether the field read it as a method paper or as a result.
  5. **Context: industry research.** Two of three authors were at Microsoft Research (p. 1083); the study ran on a Microsoft Surface prototype (p. 1085); Microsoft filed a patent application on the procedure on 2008-08-04, naming the three authors as inventors, published 2010, abandoned 2014-12-09; the paper does not mention it (section 03). These are documented facts; motive is not. Does knowing them change how you weigh "the first to employ users, rather than principles" (p. 1084) or "goes a long way to validating" (p. 1088)? Group only, never pairs, and restate the rule first (Q15.7).
- **Zoom in**
  - "Read the four contributions on p. 1084 against Wobbrock and Kientz's seven types. Which type is each? Where is the method?" (Section 10: the numbered list omits it; the abstract carries it.)
  - "The Conclusion claims 'ease of recognition, consistency, reversibility, and versatility through aliasing' (p. 1091). Which of the four was shown in the paper and which asserted?" (Consistency and reversibility are in Figure 4 and p. 1088; aliasing is in the set; recognition was not tested in the paper.)
  - "The paper says the gestures are 'not, in fact, "obvious"' because the authors matched only 43.5% (p. 1091). Is non-obviousness a virtue in a gesture meant to be guessed?"
  - "The taxonomy's nature dimension depends on intent: the paper says the gesture itself 'usually is not enough to reveal its metaphorical nature; the answer lies in the user's mental model' (p. 1086), and the authors coded it by hand, settling finger cases in debriefing (p. 1086). Can another lab apply it without think-aloud data? If not, is it a shared language or a local one?"
- **Zoom out and the closer**
  - "Design the 2026 version. Twenty participants, a device of your choice: AR headset, car, smart glasses, a public table. What do you keep from this method, what do you drop, what do you add? One change per person."
  - "You are a CHI reviewer in 2009. Accept? You are a CHI reviewer today. Accept? What changed, the standards or the paper?"
- **Move:** the closing round. Eight to ten people aloud, one sentence each: "the one thing I would change if I ran this study today." No repeats. Everyone else writes their sentence on the back of their pair card, collected at the door and counted for grading. Tally on the board as method, population or measurement changes. End by rereading slide 12.1's sentence as a question: "So: is the evidence thinner than the influence, or is that what a method paper looks like at birth?"
- **Threads:** "patent" stays under the facts-only rule; "pinch" leads to Anchor 1, position 2, and card H; "what would you measure" to Anchor 3, position 4.
- **Three-minute version:** the stem and five volunteers' sentences; the rest written on the cards.
- **Traps:** the 2016 Interactions article does not name this paper; the 2012 whitepaper does (section 10). "No product adopted the set" is our judgment. The patent application was never granted; say "application," attribute no motive (pitfalls 15; 03.1). GI 2010 wording (pitfall 6). Do not say "weaker than its reputation" (12.1, Watch out).
- **[classmate] hook:** a commentary that judged the contribution, cited later work, or raised Microsoft.

### Anchor 7 (reserve). Who bends, the recognizer or the user?

- **Ask:** "Designers used to pick gestures the recognizer could tell apart; this paper picked the gestures users agreed on and left the recognizer for later. The result is 48 gestures for 22 commands, some meaning different things depending on where they land, on a 2008 table that watched hands from below at about 43 pixels per inch. So who should bend, the recognizer or the user, and did the paper's technology advice follow from its data?"
- **Why it matters:** this is the paper's fourth contribution, "implications for surface computing technology" (p. 1084), and the one anchor that brings section 08 into the discussion. It is also where "ease of recognition" (p. 1091), claimed with no recognizer built, gets tested by the room. Added after both cross-checks named recognition as the gap in the guide.
- **Positions people can defend**
  1. **The user, and the set already helps the recognizer.** The paper says designer gestures "chosen out of concern for reliable recognition" produced "somewhat arbitrary gesture sets" (p. 1083). Its findings simplify recognition: 1 to 3 fingers read as a "single point" (p. 1090), so finger count need not be sensed; gestures with off-screen parts "have alternatives in the user-defined set for tables without a sensing boundary" (p. 1091); reuse by target shrinks the vocabulary (p. 1088).
  2. **The recognizer needs a veto.** Long et al. found "users are sometimes poor at picking easily differentiable gestures" (p. 1084); the paper's answer, "implicit agreement among users" (p. 1084), settles which referent gets a gesture (p. 1087), not whether two shapes can be told apart. A lasso, a scratch-out, a check, a slash and a question mark (Figure 4, p. 1089) all arrive as one-point paths, and "hit-testing within objects will be necessary" (p. 1091). "Ease of recognition" (p. 1091) was asserted; the recognizer was the next step (p. 1091). We argue a confusability check belongs inside the method.
  3. **The physics engine as a third way.** 43.9% of gestures were physical; seven participants expected intervening objects to move out of the way and four "threw" an object off-screen, so "the idea of using a physics engine [32] rather than a traditional recognizer has support," though "a physics engine alone will probably not suffice" for the symbolic, abstract and metaphorical rest (p. 1091). Ref. 32 is Wilson's own UIST 2008 work (p. 1092); that is a fact about lineage, not a motive.
  4. **Sensing should follow users, if the behaviour is real.** The paper asks for cameras that look "down or sideways at users' hands" and for a low-resolution sensing boundary beyond the display (p. 1091), because of off-screen drags and above-table gestures (p. 1090). It also concedes that feedback "will implicitly remind users to remain in contact with the table" (p. 1091). We argue the hardware ask may rest on behaviour that feedback would remove (Anchor 4).
- **Zoom in**
  - "Seven and four of twenty participants (p. 1091). Is that enough to steer a system architecture? What would be?"
  - "Pick two gestures in Figure 4 that a 2008 camera under the table would confuse. Which would you cut, and what do users lose?" (Section 08: 1024 x 768 over 24 by 18 inches is about 43 pixels per inch, our arithmetic.)
  - "The Conclusion claims 'ease of recognition' (p. 1091). From which evidence in the paper?"
- **Zoom out**
  - "Who should bend, the recognizer or the user? Does the answer change between 2008 and now that recognizers are learned from data?"
  - "What should a designer hand an engineer from an elicitation study: the set, the agreement scores, or the disagreements?"
- **Move:** the engineer's veto. Put four Figure 4 panels on the screen (lasso, scratch out, check, slash). Hands: which one would you cut for the recognizer's sake? Then one person who voted to cut and one who refused.
- **Threads:** "the behaviour would vanish with feedback" leads to Anchor 4; "widgets instead" to Anchor 2, position 4; "Wilson's physics paper" stays under the facts-only rule.
- **Three-minute version:** the stem, the engineer's veto, close with "who bends?"
- **Traps:** the 43 pixels per inch is our arithmetic (section 08). "Ease of recognition" was not tested in the paper; do not say the set failed recognition. Ref. 32's authorship is a fact; attribute no motive. Long et al. is about users, not designers (pitfall 8).
- **[classmate] hook:** a commentary on recognition, sensing, or the physics engine.

## 6. Moves for the room

- **Concrete first, then wide.** Every anchor opens on a page, a figure, a number or the room's own tally. Abstract questions get silence; a detail gets an answer, and the view follows.
- **Hands in three, never two.** A two-way show of hands produces a majority and a silence. A third option ("the question is badly posed," "it depends on the device") gives the undecided a place and a reason to speak.
- **Harvest the pairs.** "Who heard a view from their partner that they disagreed with?" People will report a partner's view more readily than their own, which brings in the quiet partner by proxy. Then turn to the partner: "Is that what you said?"
- **Warm cold-call.** Ask someone who has not spoken a question they cannot get wrong (what they did in the warm-up, which card they had, which Figure 4 panel they picked) before asking them for a view.
- **Credit the commentaries.** Read one sentence from a commentary, name its author, and ask them to take the first position on that anchor. This covers the integration rubric and starts the anchor with a voice that is not Oliver's.
- **Park and return.** When a tangent appears, write it on the board with the student's name and say when you will come back. Then come back. A parked thread that is never picked up teaches the room not to offer tangents.
- **Hold the dominant voice.** "Hold that, I want two other views before you come back to it." Then come back to them.
- **Appoint a defender.** If the room is only attacking the paper, ask someone to make the authors' case for two minutes; the positions marked 1 in each anchor are their brief. If the room is only defending, position 2 or 3 is the brief.
- **Let students answer students.** When a student makes a claim, ask "who sees it differently?" before answering yourself.
- **Close each anchor with a student's sentence.** "Give me the one-line version of where we got to." Not Oliver's summary.
- **Keep the arithmetic ready.** Square each group's share, add. With 20 people the floor is 0.05. If the class is not 20, the floor is 1 over the class size.
