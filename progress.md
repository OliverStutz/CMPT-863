## The project in short

A CISO presents a cybersecurity briefing to a board. A board member uses a simulated vision-LLM content checker that reads the slides and raises flags. We study how this changes the board member's trust in the CISO. The board member holds the formal power, the CISO holds the expertise.

## What we learned about the sources

| Paper | Finding |
|---|---|
| Aamir et al. (2026) | 13 Australian directors and senior executives. It did not study trust or power between board and CISO, and it proposes no AI tool. Only the abstract could be checked |
| Chen et al. (2026) | The doctor offered the AI consultation. Effects held only when the doctor was seen as human-like |
| Prike et al. (2025) | 406 participants, not about 4,000. Eight false claims were fact-checked. No trust measure |
| Strickland (1958) | Checked through a secondary source only. The two subordinates did "similar" work. The explanation by attribution comes from later authors |
| Reif et al. (2025) | The penalty for using AI shrank or vanished when the evaluator used AI often |
| Mayer and Davis (1999) | The scale is 5-point, not 7-point, and the items refer to "top management" |

**On H3:** no study was found in which a person ends with more trust after clearing a doubt than without the doubt. The closest study found recovery to the earlier level (Greitemeyer & Sagioglou, 2015). Another found a lasting deficit (Thorson, 2016). No study from 2023 to 2026 looked at a professional who rebuts an AI flag, which is the gap this project fills.
