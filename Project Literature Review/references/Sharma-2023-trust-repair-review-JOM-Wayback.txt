Abstract
We review research on the topic of trust repair, which has proceeded over the past four decades using three different philosophical mechanisms that provide the bases through which trust is restored by multiple repair tactics. We base our definition of trust repair on the view that trust is “a psychological state comprising the intention to accept vulnerability based on the positive expectations of the intentions or behaviors of another.” We define trust repair as any increase in trust above the post-transgression level and complete repair as an increase in trust to the pre-transgression level. We provide an overview of the research designs incorporated in the trust-repair literature and make recommendations and cautions in measuring trust repair. We then summarize the emergence of the different mechanisms behind trust repair, namely, attributional, social equilibrium, and structural mechanisms, and review literature on the related verbal and behavioral repair tactics that can be employed after a transgression. We also provide a review on the process of trust repair, that is, the different stages that unfold to repair trust. We conclude by suggesting that future research should explore trust repair not only from an isolated tactics perspective but also from a broader pathway perspective. Finally, as trust repair occurs over time, future research should also explore the role of differing perceptions of time in the trust-repair process.

Trust is an integral part of a relationship and provides both interpersonal and organizational benefits (Davis, Schoorman, Mayer, & Tan, 2000; Dirks & Ferrin, 2002). Trust is generally defined as involving risk and vulnerability (Mayer, Davis, & Schoorman, 1995). The presence of risk in such relationships increases the possibility of betrayal for the trustor. A betrayal invalidates the “trustworthy until proven otherwise” assumption of initial trust development (Kim, Ferrin, Cooper, & Dirks, 2004: 104) and induces concern about additional harm in the trustor (Williams, 2012). Thus, trust repair is different from general trust development. Many scholars have examined the consequences of a betrayal and the process by which broken trust may be repaired (e.g., Kim et al., 2004; Morrison & Robinson, 1997; Sitkin & Roth, 1993). The intent of this paper is to review and integrate this research and suggest avenues to bolster the literature further.
Relationships are ties between two or more individuals or organizations that incorporate affective, cognitive, and behavioral connections and interactions between the parties. Active relationships are inherently risky in nature (Lawler, 2001) as they involve psychological and emotional investment, mutual dependence, and implicit or explicit expectations (Molm, 1994, 2010), which when broken can distort the accepted dynamics of the relationship (Lewicki & Bunker, 1996) and slip it into a disequilibrium (Goffman, 1967; Ren & Gray, 2009). This disequilibrium negatively affects some or all aspects and in certain situations challenges the very existence of the relationship (Lewicki & Bunker, 1996). Thus, whenever relationships are breached, repair becomes essential for the trust in the relationship to remain intact. Repair would also be needed if violations are made by the trustee to a third party, as knowledge of these may also diminish perceived trust of the trustor in the trustee (Gillespie, Lockey, Hornsey, & Okimoto, 2021; Kim et al., 2004).
Repair of relationship “occurs when a transgression causes the positive state(s) that constitute(s) the relationship to disappear and/or negative states to arise, as perceived by one or both parties, and activities by one or both parties substantively return the relationship to a positive state” (Dirks, Lewicki, & Zaheer, 2009: 69). Scholars from multiple fields have studied repair of relationship for many decades. For example, in the Cold War era, political scientists were interested in developing mechanisms in order to resolve conflict between the two blocs and came up with the development of the “graduated and reciprocated initiatives in tension reduction” (GRIT) strategy (Lindskold & Collins, 1978; Osgood, 1962). Sociology scholars studied relationship repair in the context of understanding the attitudes between interethnic and interracial groups, not only in American society (e.g. Deutsch & Gerard, 1955; Sherif & Sherif, 1969) but also in other societies, such as South Africa during the time of apartheid (Hatch, 1955; Langely, Okolo, & Langley, 1974). Repair of relationship was not only explored in macro fields but was also studied in microlevel disciplines, such as psychology. Specifically, marital relations scholars explored ways in which marital conflict could be resolved (Beck, 1966; Worthington & DiBlasio, 1990) and marriage rebuilt after extramarital affairs (Schneider, 1989). In the field of management, Sitkin and Roth (1993) and Elsbach (1994) were among the first to conduct major research to understand the notion of repair of relationship after a violation. Instead of considering only repair of relationship as a whole, since then scholars have also started to become more precise in exploring repair of specific aspects of relationships.
Repair of trust is a subset of repair of relationship wherein the trustor again possesses confident positive expectations of the trustee (Lewicki, McAllister, & Bies, 1998; McAllister, 1995) or is willing to be vulnerable to the trustee in the presence of risk due to the restoration of trustee's perceived trustworthiness (Mayer et al., 1995). Repair of other forms of relationship, such as cooperation and legitimacy, may reestablish interaction between the parties without restoring trusting intentions in the relationship. Certain relational repair constructs, such as reconciliation and forgiveness, are seen as a proxy to repair of trust but are in fact different. For example, trust has been used as part of a forgiveness measure (Fehr & Gelfand, 2010). However, forgiveness is the relinquishing of negative feeling toward the trustee (Bies, Barclay, Tripp, & Aquino, 2016) and is not an equivalent of trust repair because the trustor can forgive without repairing trust at all (Kim, 2018). Moreover, even though forgiveness can facilitate trust repair (Grover, Abid-Dupont, Manville, & Hasel, 2019), it is neither a mandatory condition (because trust can also be repaired by proving innocence; Kim et al., 2004) nor a sufficient condition. As this review is directed specifically toward repair of trust, we will incorporate other forms of relationship repair only to the extent they influence the repair of trust.
Much of the trust-repair literature is based on functionalist assumptions that it can be repaired by incorporating a set of relatively simple prescriptions or tactics (Mollering, 2006). These tactics have taken center stage in the literature, and studies have utilized a variance theory approach (Langley, 1999; Van de Ven, 2007) to discern their effectiveness to repair trust. Even though repair of trust is a process in itself, the use of process approaches to investigate trust repair is surprisingly underdeveloped (cf. Gillespie & Siebert, 2018), and most process-based studies either are theoretical in nature or utilize case study methodology. A process theory approach is important not only to understand the repair over time but also to recognize when and how does complete trust repair occur, as most of the studies have shown that repair tactics do not return the trust levels to the pre-transgression levels (Kim, 2018). In this review, in addition to providing a definition for trust repair, we will elaborate on all the major trust-repair tactics studied in the literature and the mechanisms they use to repair trust. We will also explain the process of trust repair and provide suggestions for how the trust-repair literature can be further developed in the Future Research Agenda section.
Review Process
In developing our review article, we conducted a literature search for articles on repair of trust that followed the classic paper on this subject published by Sitkin and Roth (1993). We used multiple combinations of the terms “repair,” “reconcile,” “restore,” “trust,” and “relationship” on Google Scholar, ABI Informs, Web of Science, and PsycINFO databases. We focused our search primarily on peer-reviewed articles in journals and prominent book chapters in the field. We included only those articles that explored repair of trust after a transgression (implicitly or explicitly mentioned in the article) in the organizational or professional context and at all levels of analysis. Both empirical and theoretical articles, and prominent book chapters, were part of the review. Our search found a total of 121 relevant articles and book chapters in multiple fields, such as organizational behavior, psychology, strategy, economics, marketing, and accounting. A total of 97 of these were empirical articles. We then conducted a thorough literature review wherein we extracted information about the repair tactics, research design, level of analysis, sample of study, dependent variable, and findings of each empirical article.1 We also read all the shortlisted theoretical articles and book chapters to form a solid foundational base for the review and strengthen its content.
Our search found two articles that reviewed trust repair at the individual and organizational level (Kramer & Lewicki, 2010; Lewicki & Brinsfield, 2017). Kramer and Lewicki (2010) took a tactic-focused perspective in that they elaborated on the characteristics of the tactics an individual or organization can use to repair trust. Lewicki and Brinsfield (2017) updated the Kramer and Lewicki (2010) review on repair tactics that are available to the trustee and provided a review of the moderation effects of different trust violation characteristics. We also considered a review in the related field of relationship resilience (Olekalns, Caza, & Vogus, 2020), which utilized a process-focused perspective to elaborate on relationship resilience after a relational fracture. Finally, we analyzed some reviews on trust repair that were recently published as book chapters (e.g., Gillespie et al., 2021; Gillespie & Siebert, 2018; Kim, 2018).
We found significant gaps in our search of the reviews on trust repair in the literature. First, none of the reviews provided an explanation of all the tactics studied in the trust-repair literature at different levels of analysis. Whereas Lewicki and Brinsfield (2017) focused primarily on the tactics used at the individual level (Gillespie et al., 2021), they did not substantially explain many repair tactics used at the organizational level, such as leadership replacement, trust transference, and transparency maintenance. Second, we did not find a single article that reviewed the process of trust repair. Given the gaps, our review adds value to the trust-repair literature on three main aspects. First, our review elaborates on the definition of trust repair and provides a new definition that is more comprehensive in nature. Second, our review takes a “one-stop shop” approach and provides an explanation on different tactics used after a transgression at multiple levels of analysis. Finally, this article reviews the stages through which trust is repaired and identifies the complementarities of different mechanisms within the trust-repair process.

Definition of Trust Repair
Our review of the literature suggests that most scholars who study trust repair do not define the construct as its own entity. In their research on trust repair, these scholars have often taken the approach of defining trust and using that definition as a baseline to study its repair (e.g. Brühl, Basel, & Kury, 2018; Cianci, Clor-Proell, & Kaplan, 2019; Haesevoets, Van Hiel, Folmer, & De Cremer, 2014; Schweitzer, Hershey, & Bradlow, 2006). Some scholars, however, have defined repair of trust as its own entity. Table 1 displays the list of definitions of trust repair that have been used in the literature.

Even though the current definitions of trust repair in the literature provide a general understanding of the concept, these definitions lack clarity on two accounts. First, as trust is the central construct in the definition, a brief discussion on the definition of trust is in order. Second, most of the definitions of trust repair are ambiguous on the specification of how much repair needs to occur for trust to be completely repaired. In other words, there is clarity needed in the literature about what forms the limits of trust repair beyond which improvement in trust no longer remains part of the “repair” process. Incorporating the limits of trust repair within the definition would increase both clarity and comprehensiveness of the definition.
What Is Being Repaired?
In the decade of the 1990s, several scholars proposed definitions of trust (see Fulmer & Gelfand, 2012; Lockey, 2017). After research and review of these definitions, the field reached a consensus that two components form an integral part of the concept of trust: trusting beliefs, that is, positive expectations of future behavior by the trustee, and trusting intentions, that is, willingness of the trustor to be vulnerable to the trustee (Fulmer & Gelfand, 2012; Gillespie et al., 2021; McKnight, Cummings, & Chervany, 1998).
According to McAllister (1995: 25), trust is defined as “the extent to which a person is confident in, and willing to act on the basis of, the words, actions, and decisions of another.” Lewicki et al. (1998: 439) define trust as “confident positive expectations regarding another's conduct” and distrust as “confident negative expectations regarding another's conduct.” These definitions form the basis of the argument that trusting beliefs are at the center of the concept of trust, with trusting intentions as a natural consequence (Kramer & Lewicki, 2010).
On the other hand, definitions by Mayer et al. (1995) form the basis of the perspective that considers trusting intentions at the center of the trust construct and trusting beliefs as a natural antecedent (Kim, 2018). They define trust as “the willingness of a party to be vulnerable to the actions of another party based on the expectation that the other will perform a particular action important to the trustor, irrespective of the ability to monitor or control that other party” (Mayer et al., 1995: 712). According to scholars from this perspective, a breach in trust is likely to discourage the trustor to engage in risky interactions with the trustee, and trust is repaired when the trustor is willing to be vulnerable to the trustee again (Tomlinson & Mayer, 2009).
What started as two approaches to a definition of trust evolved into an integrated definition that included both perspectives. The early versions of this are seen in Rousseau et al.’s (1998: 395) definition as “a psychological state comprising the intention to accept vulnerability based upon positive expectations of the intentions or behaviors of another” and culminating in the review by Lewicki and Brinsfield (2017) that incorporates both aspects in the definition of trust.

What Are the Limits of Trust Repair?
Most of the definitions of trust repair in use fail to explicitly and objectively suggest the limits of trust repair. In other words, trust-repair scholars do not explicitly clarify in their definition the extent to which repair process is assumed to continue and when the repair of trust is deemed complete. Even though scholars mention the limits of trust repair in their explanations (e.g., Dirks et al., 2009; Lewicki & Brinsfield, 2017) and research design (e.g., Ferrin, Kim, Cooper, & Dirks, 2007; Kim et al., 2004), the understanding of the limits of trust repair within its current definitions are implicit at best.
According to Merriam-Webster Dictionary, repair means “putting together what is torn or broken.” In the trust-repair context, the transgression “tears” the trust of the trustor to a lower level. Any actions to increase the level of trust above the post-transgressional levels would be considered trust repair. The degree of trust repair would be measured as the percentage of lost trust that is repaired following the transgression (explained in detail in the Future Research Agenda section). Trust would be considered fully repaired, that is, not broken anymore, when trust is restored to the pre-transgression level, irrespective of how its antecedents, that is, factors of trustworthiness, are readjusted. Thus, trust repair occurs within the limits of the effects of the transgression on trust. Improvement of trust beyond the pre-transgressional levels falls outside the limits of the effect that the transgression had on trust and, hence, should be considered not as “repair” but as further development of trust. It must be noted that when the trust is repaired completely, the combination of antecedents, that is, perceived trustworthiness, may not remain the same as the source of trust may shift. For example, a trustor may be equally willing to be vulnerable to the trustee or have similar positive expectations about the trustee after the intervention, but the main source of those intentions or beliefs may shift from cognition-based to affect-based factors or vice versa (Lewicki & Bunker, 1996; McAllister, 1995). This provides an explanation for the “Humpty Dumpty” problem (Lewicki & Brinsfield, 2017) by suggesting that even though the composition of trustworthiness may never be the same again, as the antecedents may have shifted, trust can return to its pre-transgressional level, or even exceed it, after a violation. Hence, we define trust repair as any improvement in trust level following a transgression. Complete repair is achieved when the trust level reaches the pre-transgression level.

Mechanisms That Influence Trust Repair
Even though some research has been conducted in the repair-of-trust literature at the organizational level (cf. Bachmann, Gillespie, & Priem, 2015), research on trust repair has primarily focused on generating and testing hypotheses about what interpersonal tactics, actions, and reforms are most likely to be effective (cf. Gillespie et al., 2021). In the literature, we found two relevant schemes for classification of the repair tactics. According to Dirks et al. (2009), scholars have studied multiple repair tactics and classified them under three main mechanisms: attributional (e.g., Kim, Dirks, & Cooper, 2009; Tomlinson & Mayer, 2009), social-equilibrium (e.g., Reb, Goldman, Kray, & Cropanzano, 2006; Ren & Gray, 2009), and structural (e.g., Nakayachi & Watabe, 2005; Sitkin & Roth, 1993). Bachmann et al. (2015) provided a different classification for repair tactics. These authors classified the repair tactics in the organizational repair literature into six categories: sensemaking, relational, regulations and controls, ethical culture, transparency, and transference. We will use the former classification of repair tactics for two reasons: First, Bachmann et al. built the six-category classification primarily for organizational-level trust repair. As some categories are not valid for repair tactics at other levels (e.g., ethical culture), they would not be the best form of classification for a multilevel clustering. Second, these six categories fit well within the broader, three-mechanism model (Gillespie et al., 2021). Specifically, sensemaking is similar to the attribution mechanism; relational tactics use similar assumptions to repair as the social-equilibrium mechanism; and ethical culture, regulations and control, and transparency repair trust using assumptions mostly based on the structural mechanism. Transference can be part of any of the three mechanisms, depending on the way third-party trustworthiness and/or control is used to repair trust.
At a broader level, trust-repair tactics affect subsequent trust levels by addressing issues related to at least one of the three aspects of the repair process: transgression, relationship, and environment. Trust repairing in the relationship between the trustee and the trustor would improve if the responsibility of the transgression is at best removed or at least reduced from the trustee (i.e., the individual characteristics of the trustees are improved), the dent in the relationship due to the transgression is restored (i.e., the social order between the parties is reestablished), and an environment is created that reduces the likelihood of trust to be violated again (i.e., the external forces are influenced to reduce the likelihood the transgression will happen again). Each mechanism predominantly (though not exclusively) addresses one of these aspects of the repair process. Specifically, tactics following the attribution mechanism focus on the individual characteristics of the trustee, tactics following the social-equilibrium mechanism focus on the social order between the parties, and tactics that follow the structural mechanism focus on the external environment within which the parties exist. Whereas the attribution and social-equilibrium mechanisms have been the dominant mechanisms to study trust repair at the individual level, the structural mechanism has been primarily used to understand collective-level trust repair (Gillespie et al., 2021). In this section, we elaborate on each of the three mechanisms. Table 2 provides a summary of the three mechanisms used to study trust repair.

Attributional Mechanism
The attributional mechanism focuses on shaping the cognitive evaluations of the trustor to repair the trust (Gillespie et al., 2021). This mechanism operates on the assumption that the degree and nature of responsibility of the actor with the transgression determine the actor’s trustworthiness in the relationship with the trustor. After the transgression occurs, the trustors “are motivated to draw attributions and the targets are motivated to shape those attributions” (Dirks et al., 2009: 72). Blame attributions are made based on prior beliefs of the victim, the information received from the environment, and expected behavior in the situation by the trustee (Kelley & Michela, 1980). Locus, stability, and controllability of the cause of transgression influence the degree and nature of blame placed on the trustee, which in turn impacts the affect felt by the trustor and the expectancy of future transgression by the trustee (Weiner, 1985, 1988). As affect and expectancy are the main determinants of action (Weiner, 1988), internal, stable, and/or controllable causes of transgression reduces the likelihood that forgiveness is offered and the relationship is reconciled (Andiappan & Treviño, 2011). Thus, the main premise of this mechanism is that trust can be repaired if the greater understanding of the transgression received by the trustor reduces the perceived responsibility of the trustee, or at least the perceived controllability and stability of the cause of transgression. It must be noted that an attempt to paint an innocent image in the face of guilt by the trustee is not advisable for both moral and pragmatic reasons, because misrepresentation would be an insult to the very notion of trust and stand contrary to the ethics of relationships. It would also lead to a greater deterioration of trust if it is exposed in the future (Ferrin et al., 2007; Kim et al., 2004).
Borrowing from Weiner's (1985) attributional theory, Kim et al. (2009) and Tomlinson and Mayer (2009) have provided the foundational understanding of how the attributional mechanism operates within the trust-repair process (Gillespie & Siebert, 2018). In their bilateral model of trust repair, Kim et al. (2009) posit that the attribution of guilt for the transgression is a three-stage process where the trustor attempts to find answers to three sequential questions. The answers received or perceived by the trustor impact the need for the subsequent questions. In the first stage, the trustor tries to find answer to the question, Is the trustee innocent or guilty of committing the transgression? If the trustee is blamed with at least some guilt for the transgression, then the attribution process enters the second stage, wherein the trustor seeks an answer for the following question: Should the guilt be attributed to the situation or to the trustee? The need for the final query arises only if at least some guilt is attributed to controllable and/or internal causes. Finally, in the third stage, the trustor explores whether the transgressing shortcoming in the trustee is changeable or not. Repair of trust is extremely difficult if the trustor perceives the internal causes of the transgression to be not changeable. Many factors, such as the frequency and nature of the violation and stage of the relationship, can influence the perceived changeability of the transgressional trait (see Lewicki & Brinsfield, 2017, for details). As is discussed later in this review, different tactics are most effective to direct the trustor toward positive perceptions in different attributional stages. For example, denial is effective to establish innocence of the trustee, that is, in the first stage of the attribution process, whereas explanation and apology are particularly effective in the second and third stage of the attribution process, respectively.
Tomlinson and Mayer (2009) also provide a similar explanation to how an attribution process plays a role in trust repair. Specifically, their explanation is based on two concepts: causal ascription, that is, whether the trustor ascribes the cause of the transgression on the trustee's lack of ability, benevolence, integrity, or luck (termed as “other” in Tomlinson & Mayer, 2009), and causal attribution, that is whether the negative outcomes from the transgression are due to external or internal factors, controllable or uncontrollable situations, and unstable or enduring characteristics of the trustee. Causal ascription and causal attribution together influence the likelihood of trust repair and which repair tactics would be effective. Even though these two models of the attributional mechanism of trust repair were explored in the interpersonal trust-repair perspective, research has shown that the attributional mechanism is often utilized to study repair after organizational-level trust breaches (Dietz & Gillespie, 2012; Elsbach, 1994; Gillespie & Siebert, 2018).

Social-Equilibrium Mechanism
The social-equilibrium mechanism focuses on the social interplay between members in a relationship to repair trust and thus is socio-psychological in nature. It is based on the interaction ritual model proposed by Goffman (1967), which states that transgressions are disruptions in the normative assumptions shared by the trustor and the trustee as embedded members within their social network (Goffman, 1967: 19). These violations in turn disrupt the social order of the relationship and require reconsiderations of social rituals and execution of symbolic or restorative acts to reestablish the social order of the relationship (Gillespie & Siebert, 2018). Thus, the main aim of the social-equilibrium mechanism is to reestablish the social order in the relationship between the trusting parties.
According to the social-equilibrium mechanism, individuals are bound within the relational and cultural social web, which creates an implicit understanding between the parties about the expectations of resource exchange. Thus, such a social structure facilitates reciprocal transactions wherein “actors initiate exchanges without knowing whether, when, or to what degree others will reciprocate” (Molm, 1994: 168). However, as all individuals are either not aware, not equipped, or not willing to follow the social code, they intentionally or unintentionally break the norms from time to time. A transgression leads to “ritual disequilibrium or disgrace” in the social balance of the relationship (Goffman, 1967: 19) and disrupts the interpersonal system between the parties (Lewicki & Bunker, 1996). According to the affect theory of social exchange, this disruption leads to involuntary negative emotions (Lawler, 2001) that, once activated, “become the driving force in subsequent thought and action” (Izard, 1991: 43) and prohibit the parties in the relationship from working together until norms are reinstated and equilibrium is restored. Appropriate consequences of the transgression for the trustee become an important part of the social justice restoration process (Reb et al., 2006). Thus, complete repair of trust occurs when the restorative behavior reaffirms the social norms of trust in the relationship and reestablishes the pre-transgressional social order. This restoration process can be initiated by either the trustor, the trustee, or a third party, but both the trustor and the trustee must be willing to repair the relationship (Kim et al., 2009; Ren & Gray, 2009).
The social-equilibrium mechanism considers trust-repair efforts to be effective when two conditions are met: (a) the negative affect that the trustor feels toward the trustee due to the transgression is eliminated, and (b) the trustor is satisfied with the extent and sincerity of the restorative efforts by the trustee (Ren & Gray, 2009). Negative affect influences the repair of trust negatively as it colors the cognitive processing of social information about the relationship (G. Jones & George, 1998; Williams, 2001). Thus, a prerequisite for a trust relationship to be repaired is that the negative affect of the trustor toward the trustee should be diminished at the least and replaced with positive affect at best. The reversal of physical, emotional, and/or monetary loss incurred by the trustor due to the transgression, or at least a sincere acknowledgment and concern for it, assists in diminishing the negative affect felt by the trustor due to the transgression. For satisfaction with the extent and sincerity of the restorative efforts to be achieved, expected repair actions by the trustee in terms of justice restoration and trustworthy replenishment must not be perceived as “cheap talk” and instead be put into effect (Bottom, Gibson, Daniels, & Murnighan, 2002).

Structural Mechanism
The structural mechanism generally takes an agentic perspective to trust repair (Long & Sitkin, 2018) and assumes that individuals are utility maximizers and are driven by self-interest and profitability (cf. Dirks et al., 2009). According to scholars of structural mechanisms in trust repair, a breach in trust can either decrease the positive expectations or increase the negative expectations in the trustee, in other words, negatively influence trusting beliefs. It reduces cooperation and increases the likelihood of negative or short-term transactional behavior taking its place (Dirks et al., 2009; Lewicki & Bunker, 1996). Repair of such expectations occurs when changes are made to formal and/or informal structures, procedures, and social norms that either decrease negative expectations or increase positive expectations of future behaviors (Gillespie & Dietz, 2009; Gillespie & Siebert, 2018). In other words, structural scholars suggest that trust can be repaired if external conditions are set that either promote trustworthy behavior or diminish the opportunity of untrustworthy behavior. For example, structural scholars recommend that trust can be repaired by incorporating control mechanisms, such as detailed legal contracts, in the relationship. These control mechanisms minimize the “implementation-related problem” (Sitkin & Roth, 1993: 373) as they explicitly define the expected and acceptable behavior, bind the parties to perform their agreed-upon responsibilities with integrity, and extend rewards or sanctions based on the performance (Cardinal, Kreutzer, & Miller, 2017; Ouchi, 1979; Weibel et al., 2016). They reduce psychological, temporal, and monetary risks for both the trustor and the trustee as they ensure that the rights of the trustor would be protected and the trustor would be treated fairly. This helps in reestablishing reliability between the parties (Sitkin & Roth, 1993) and, in turn, the likelihood of renewed exchange. The trustees may also incorporate promotive structural changes, such as offer voluntary hostage posting at the individual level (Nakayachi & Watabe, 2005) or facilitate structural and cultural reforms at the organizational level, which can increase the trustworthiness of the trustee and restore positive trusting beliefs (Gillespie, Dietz, & Lockey, 2014). Thus, the main aim of a structural mechanism is to influence external forces in the form of structural, procedural, and normative reforms to create an environment that facilitates trustworthy behavior and denounces untrustworthy behavior.

Trust-Repair Tactics
Over the past few decades, trust-repair scholars have studied the absolute and comparative effectiveness of many repair tactics used at different levels of analysis. Research has shown that repair of trust is tougher at the team and organizational levels than at the individual level (Kim, Cooper, Dirks, & Ferrin, 2013) because of the group polarization effect (Hinsz, Tindale, & Vollrath, 1997), interindividual-intergroup discontinuity effect (Wildschut, Pinter, Vevea, Insko, & Schopler, 2003), appraisal gap leading from out-group homogeneity (Hornsey, Okimoto, & Wenzel, 2017), and competitive victimhood (Sullivan, Landau, Branscombe, & Rothschild, 2012) (see Gillespie et al., 2021, for details). In this section, we will review each of the tactics studied in the literature at both the individual and collective levels of analysis.
These tactics can be classified in terms of their delivery characteristics (Gillespie et al., 2021). Specifically, repair tactics are either verbal or behavioral in nature. In this section, we review the evidence of effectiveness and moderators to the effectiveness of seven verbal tactics—namely, denial, apology, self-disclosure, explanation, acknowledgment of transgression, hostage posting, and trust transference—and four behavioral tactics—namely, compensation, penance, controls, and organizational reforms. The summary of these tactics is available in Table 3.

Verbal Repair Tactics
Denial
This is a repair tactic that the trustee often uses in order to establish their complete innocence. Denial must not be confused with reticence because silence about the transgression is seldom perceived as denial of transgression and signals a possibility of guilt (Ferrin et al., 2007). Hence, denial, which is defined as a statement in which allegation is explicitly declared to be untrue (Kim et al., 2004), is an active (and not passive) form of refusing responsibility for the transgression. As denial is focused on distancing the trustee from the transgression, it is a tactic that is purely attributional in nature.
Research has shown support for both arguments that denial is an effective repair tactic (e.g., Bansal & Zahedi, 2015; Brühl et al., 2018) and that it is not (e.g., Gillespie et al., 2014; Henderson, Welsh, & O’Leary-Kelly, 2020; Lewicki & Bunker, 1996) and can in fact make the situation worse (Gillespie et al., 2014). Digging deeper into the construct, scholars have found evidence that moderating factors play a critical role in the efficacy of denial as a repair construct. For example, denial has been shown to be more effective for integrity-based violations than for competence-based violations (Ferrin et al., 2007; Kim et al., 2004, 2006). Denial has also been found to be effective in reestablishing cooperation in relationships with a longer history (Bottom et al., 2002) or when the trustee is interested in short-term benefits (Fuoli, van de Weijer, & Paradis, 2017). Research has also shown that denial is more effective in repairing trust when its content is bound together in an analytical rather than a narrative format (Van Laer & De Ruyter, 2010) and is communicated through persuasion rather than normative pressure (Kim et al., 2013).

Apology
This is defined as a statement that acknowledges responsibility and regret for a trust violation (Kim et al., 2004) and is one of the most widely studied repair tactics after a trust violation (Bansal & Zahedi, 2015; Byrne, Barling, & Dupré, 2014; Fehr, Gelfand, & Nag, 2010; Grover et al., 2019; Henderson et al., 2020). Research has shown that apologies are more frequently used early in the relationship and when future interactions are anticipated (Ho, 2012) and are more effective when an approach goal remains the dominant force for the trustor in continuing the relationship even after a transgression (De Cremer & Desmet, 2012) and when transgression leads to unrealized gains instead of incurred losses (De Cremer, 2010). Moreover, trustees with low propensity to trust are likely to apologize only if they believe that the trustor is willing to forgive (Leunissen, De Cremer, & Folmer, 2012).
Much research has been done on the effects of apologies combined with other tactics. Apologies have a boosting effect on trust repair when they are complemented with other tactics, such as explanation (e.g., Elangovan, Auer-Rizzi, & Szabo, 2015; Kim & Harmon, 2014), penance (e.g., Gillespie et al., 2014; Grover & Hasel, 2015), and compensation (e.g., Haesevoets, Folmer, De Cremer, & Van Hiel, 2013; Ohtsubo & Watanabe, 2009). An apology accompanied with voluntary or involuntary punishment reduces negative affect as it provides validation of the remorse displayed in the apology (Dirks, Kim, Ferrin, & Cooper, 2011; Gillespie et al., 2014; Henderson et al., 2020; Ohtsubo & Watanabe, 2009). It also fulfills the desire of the trustor to witness the trustee pay a price for the transgression (De Quervain, Fischbacher, Treyer, & Schellhammer, 2004) and helps the trustor acknowledge that the trustee has learned their lesson. For example, analyzing the corruption scandal involving Siemens, Eberl, Geiger, and Aβländer (2015) and Schembera and Scherer (2017) suggested that paying fines of over $1 billion and voluntarily committing to a 15-year $100 million payment to nonprofit organizations fighting corruption over and above the apology were important measures in order to repair trust.
Evidence from the literature shows mixed results in the effectiveness of apology as a repair tactic. Researchers have found that an apology is effective to restore cooperation (e.g., Ho, 2012; Ma et al., 2019; Tomlinson, Dineen, & Lewicki, 2004), affect (e.g., Bottom et al., 2002; DiFonzo, Alongi, & Wiele, 2020), trusting intentions (e.g., Bansal & Zahedi, 2015; Grover et al., 2019; Kim & Harmon, 2014), trusting beliefs (e.g., Dirks et al., 2011; Ferrin et al., 2007; Ma et al., 2019), and justice (e.g., Cugueró-Escofet, Fortin, & Canela, 2014; De Cremer & Schouten, 2008; Tomlinson, 2012) and to facilitate forgiveness (e.g., Basford, Offermann, & Behrend, 2014; Fehr et al., 2010; Zheng, van Dijke, Narayanan, & De Cremer, 2018). On the other hand, other studies have suggested apologies are not effective in repairing affect (e.g., Ma et al., 2019), cooperation (e.g., Haesevoets et al., 2013; Lei, Masclet, & Vesely, 2014), trusting beliefs (e.g., Brühl et al., 2018; Druckman, Lewicki, & Doyle, 2019), or trust (e.g., Schweitzer et al., 2006; Tomlinson, 2012). This suggests the presence of potential moderators to the effectiveness of apologies.
Research has shown that the efficacy of an apology is dependent on many different criteria (Lewicki & Polin, 2012). These criteria include dispositional factors, such as effects of individual construals; content factors, such as apology characteristics, focus, and delivery quality; and transgression-related factors, such as severity and intentionality of the transgression.
Dispositional moderators. The congruence of individual construal, defined as the way individuals perceive their relationship with other people, and apology characteristics affects the effectiveness of apology (Fehr & Gelfand, 2010). Specifically, an apology along with compensation is effective for trustees to receive forgiveness from trustors who emphasize independent self-construal. On the other hand, trustors who emphasize relational self-construal are more likely to forgive if an apology includes expression of empathy, and trustors who emphasize collective self-construal are more likely to offer forgiveness after an apology that acknowledges rules and violated norms. Research has also shown that violations catering to the need for belongingness would demand a socioemotional remedy, such as a public apology (Reb et al., 2006). Furthermore, the effectiveness of an apology is influenced by whether the trustor follows incremental beliefs (i.e., beliefs that moral character can change over time) or entity beliefs (i.e., beliefs that moral character cannot change). Research has shown that trustors who follow incremental beliefs are more likely to cooperate following an apology than trustors who follow entity beliefs (Haselhuhn, Schweitzer, & Wood, 2010; van Houwelingen, van Dijke, & De Cremer, 2016). These arguments suggest that apology quality and dispositional fit increases the likelihood of forgiveness and cooperation. As forgiveness mediates the relationship between apology quality and trust and cooperation (Grover et al., 2019), behaviors that facilitate forgiveness set the platform for eventual repair of trust.
Content-based moderators. The structure of an apology plays an important role in its effectiveness. According to Lewicki, Polin, and Lount (2016), the structure of an effective apology comprises six components, out of which “offering to repair the relationship” and “expressing an intent to not repeat the transgression” are the most efficacious characteristics and “acknowledgment of responsibility” is the most important. The effectiveness of an apology is also directly related to the number of components present in the apology (Lewicki et al., 2016). Thus, apology is a complex tactic that not only follows the attributional mechanism but also incorporates the perspective of the social-equilibrium mechanism to repair trust and mend the relationship, and it is most effective when multiple mechanisms are employed simultaneously.
Self-disclosure is an important component of a high-quality apology, which helps the trustee to signal remorse and guilt. This increases the likelihood that the trustor would perceive that the transgressional characteristics of the trustee are repairable (Kim et al., 2009; Reeder & Brewer, 1979), which would increase the likelihood for the trust to be repaired eventually. Gold and Weiner (2000) found that trustees whose confession expressed remorse are generally perceived by trustors to have a higher moral character and that their transgression has more unstable causality. Also, in a hiring context, Krylova, Longacre, and Phillips (2018) found that individuals who voluntarily disclosed negative information from the past, especially one that had lower likelihood of being discovered, were perceived as possessing higher integrity, enjoyed greater trust, and received higher willingness to hire. On the flip side, a lack of self-disclosure (especially at the individual level) would negatively impact the repair likelihood, as confrontation about the transgression with the trustee would not only highlight the pain from the transgression but also lead to social awkwardness and embarrassment that comes with such a confrontation (Lewicki & Bunker, 1996).
Focus and affect displayed by the apology have also shown to influence the likelihood of receiving forgiveness from the trustor. A victim-focused apology, relative to an offender-focused apology, along with use of specific words that signal repentance (such as “I am sorry” rather than “I am angry” or “I am sad”) increases perceived trustee remorse (Berndsen, Hornsey, & Wohl, 2015; Dirks et al., 2011; Hornsey & Wohl, 2013) and facilitates empathy toward the trustee (Ho, 2012; Tabak, McCullough, Luna, Bono, & Berry, 2012). Meta-analytic results show that empathy is significantly and positively related to forgiveness (Fehr et al., 2010). Research has also shown that perceived remorse has the largest effect on forgiveness (Gold & Weiner, 2000) as it lowers the likelihood of the trustor to perceive the transgression to be repeated in the future (Tomlinson & Mayer, 2009). Therefore, an apology is likely to be effective and a trustor likely to forgive a trustee if the apology of the transgressor expressed primary emotions than secondary emotions (Wohl, Hornsey, & Bennett, 2012) and the negative emotions of the trustor are replaced with positive ones, such as empathy (DiFonzo et al., 2020; Fehr et al., 2010). Sincere (Basford et al., 2014; Cugueró-Escofet et al., 2014) and respectful delivery of an apology (De Cremer & Schouten, 2008) is likely to reduce the feeling of injustice in the trustor and facilitate forgiveness for the trustee (Basford et al., 2014; Ohbuchi, Kameda, & Agarie, 1989). An apology is considered sincere when it originates out of guilt or shame in the trustee and not pity for the trustor, with guilt signaling the highest sincerity levels (Hareli & Eisikovits, 2006). For collective apology, sincerity is a function of the perceived sentiments of the transgressing group by the victim group members (Wenzel, Okimoto, Hornsey, Lawrence-Wood, & Coughlin, 2017). In certain situations, even superfluous apologies may facilitate cooperation by increasing the perceived benevolence of the trustee (Brooks, Dai, & Schweitzer, 2014).
The effectiveness of an apology also depends on the attributions made in the apology, that is, whether the apology signals blame to internal traits for the transgression or external pressures. Tomlinson et al. (2004) studied 48 vignettes and found that an apology with internal attribution had a more positive effect on reconciliation than one with external attribution. Kim, Dirks, Cooper, and Ferrin (2006) conducted hiring-context vignette studies and found that differences in internal versus external attributions do affect willingness to risk and hiring after an integrity-based violation, and perceived integrity after a competence-based violation. In general, the authors found that internal attribution is more beneficial when the violation is competence based, and external attribution is better when the violation is integrity based.
Transgression-based moderators. One of the most important transgression-based moderators of the effectiveness of apology is the nature of violation. Specifically, research has consistently shown that an apology is effective to repair trust when the violation is competence based (Ferrin et al., 2007; Kim et al., 2004). These results were found at multiple levels of analysis (Kim et al., 2013). Apology has also shown a positive effect on perceived ability of the trustee when the strength of evidence is strong rather than weak (Fuoli et al., 2017).
In the past decade, scholars have also started to explore the effects of an apology in a power differential transgression, especially in a leader–follower relationship (Andiappan & Treviño, 2011; Epitropaki, Radulovic, Ete, Thomas, & Martin, 2020). A leader apology after a transgression improved the emotional well-being not only of the follower but also of the leader themselves (Byrne et al., 2014). This provided the follower with mental resources to process the inferential information from the leader's effort to repair the relationship. However, as with an apology to peers, perceived sincerity is a critical moderating factor. Apologies from leaders who were viewed as caring prior to the transgression are considered more sincere (Basford et al., 2014) and receive forgiveness (Radulovic, Thomas, Epitropaki, & Legood, 2019). On the other hand, an apology from leaders who are viewed to possess low benevolence for the follower prior to the transgression is viewed with cynicism and is often unlikely to generate forgiveness from low-power victims (Zheng, van Dijke, Leunissen, Giurge, & De Cremer, 2016). Procedural justice climate (Aquino, Tripp, & Bies, 2006) and forgiveness climate (Radulovic et al., 2019) also moderate the relationship between leader apology and follower forgiveness.

Explanation
This tactic refers to the verbal statements of reason(s) for transgression provided by the trustee and is often used by the trustee to influence the perception of the trustor at both individual and organizational levels (Bankins, 2015; Bottom et al., 2002; Gillespie et al., 2014; Henderson et al., 2020; Lei et al., 2014; Shaw, Wild, & Colquitt, 2003). Once the trustor believes the trustee to be guilty, the trustor delves into finding information about the locus of control of the transgression (Kim et al., 2009). Explanations given by the trustee provide the trustor with information to make judgments about locus of control and attribution of the degree of guilt and can improve damaged trustworthiness of the trustee (Tomlinson & Carnes, 2015). Trustees who engage in perspective taking, defined as the understanding of others' thoughts and feelings from their shoes, are more likely to proactively engage in transgressional behavior in a way that would make it easier to justify the behavior after the breach or to come up with adequate explanations that are more effective to reduce blame (Williams, 2012).
Research has shown that explanation helps in restoring cooperation (Bankins, 2015; Lei et al., 2014) and affect (Heaphy, 2013) in the relationship. Shaw et al. (2003) conducted a meta-analysis on the effects of explanation on cooperation, retaliation, and withdrawal outcomes. They analyzed the effects of explanation provision (i.e., extent to which an explanation is given) and explanation adequacy (i.e., extent to which provided explanations are clear, reasonable, and detailed) separately. They found that both explanation provision and adequacy were positively related to in-role and extrarole cooperation and reduced active and passive retaliation. Only explanation adequacy, however, attenuated withdrawal responses from existing relationships.
Scott and Lyman's (1968) general-purpose taxonomy of explanation distinguishes explanation into two types: justification and excuses. Justification is defined as the kind of explanation wherein the trustee accepts responsibility for the transgression but denies that the act is inappropriate by pointing to the fulfillment of a superordinate goal (Shaw et al., 2003). On the other hand, excuses are explanations where the trustee admits the execution but shifts attribution of controllability of the transgression to an external cause (Crant & Bateman, 1993; Tata, 2000). Meta-analysis results show that excuses are more beneficial than justification after a transgression, especially when the transgression leads to instrumental, relational, or moral implications (Shaw et al., 2003). The effects of justification for trust repair are worse when the transgression benefits the trustee (Kim & Harmon, 2014).

Acknowledgment of Transgression
This tactic refers to the explicit acceptance by the trustee that a transgression occurred. It does not, however, mean that the trustee accepts guilt for the transgression. When the transgression is discovered by the trustor, it leads to a negative affective reaction (Lawler & Yoon, 1996; Topolinski & Strack, 2015) even before the trustor starts to investigate the details of the transgression (Gillespie & Dietz, 2009; Pfarrer, Decelles, Smith, & Taylor, 2008). The role of acknowledgment as a repair tactic is to control this sudden spike of negative affect and further deterioration of the relationship between the parties. Thus, it bases itself on the foundation of the social-equilibrium mechanism and is useful early in the repair process (Božič, Siebert, & Martin, 2019, 2020). Acknowledgment of the presence of the transgression is an important first step as it implies that the trustee is empathetic toward the frustration that the transgression caused the trustor. This has a positive effect on the perceived trustworthiness of the trustee (Berndsen et al., 2015) and increases the likelihood of transgression resolution over the course of the repair process (Božič et al., 2019; T. Jones, Dacin, & Taylor, 2011). A lack of acknowledgment, on the other hand, can not only induce a feeling of revenge in the trustor (Gillespie et al., 2014) but also increase negative affect due to the perception that the trustee is unsympathetic to their condition (Lewicki & Bunker, 1996), which can holistically form a negative perception of the trustee (G. Jones & George, 1998) and hinder the effectiveness of other repair tactics incorporated by the trustee to repair trust (Bankins, 2015).

Hostage Posting
This tactic is defined as a “self-sanctioning system in an uncertain situation” (Nakayachi & Watabe, 2005: 2), wherein the trustee facilitates exchange with the trustor by accepting a tangible cost associated with the risk of failure in the transaction. Thus, it follows the structural mechanism by forming a nonlegal structure around the relationship that reduces the likelihood of trust being abused in the future. For hostage posting to be effective, it should contain at least the following three characteristics: (a) It should be perceived by the trustor as a binding agreement, (b) it should provide compensation for the trustor and/or have consequences for the trustee in case the trust is broken, and (c) it should signal a lower expectation of transgression in the future (Raub, 2004). As hostage posting is nonlegal in nature, the trustor is often willing to accept the posting after a transgression only if some trust remains or is repaired in the relationship.
Hostage posting may be voluntarily offered or be imposed on the trustee. Research has shown that voluntary postings are significantly more effective than imposed posting to restore integrity and ability trustworthiness in the trustee, especially when prior attitude toward the trustee is positive (Nakayachi & Watabe, 2005). The type of posting also has an effect on cooperation in the relationship. Specifically, whereas voluntary posting increased the willingness of the trustor to cooperate, imposed posting leads to a decrease in cooperation between the trustor and the trustee (Nakayachi & Watabe, 2005). Thus, hostage posting is likely to have a positive impact on the trust repair process when it is voluntarily offered by the trustee.

Trust Transference
This refers to the repair of trust by facilitating the transferring of trust from a trustworthy entity to the discredited entity (Bachmann et al., 2015; Gillespie et al., 2021). The trustee can initiate trust repair if they can convince a trustworthy third party to utilize their own trustworthiness to facilitate trust transference in the trustee (Bachmann et al., 2015). Trust transference can lead to trust repair because it can improve attributions about the trustworthiness of the trustee (Ferrin, Dirks, & Shah, 2006). Research has shown that trust transference can assist in trust repair when trustworthy watchdogs provide positive reactions of the trustee after a transgression (Mueller, Carter, & Whittle, 2015; Rhee & Valdez, 2009), third-party individuals (preferably closely related and high-power individuals) persuade or guarantee the trustor about the future behavior of the trustee (Yu, Yang, & Jing, 2017), and reputed endorsers provide persistent support to the trustee (Rhee & Valdez, 2009; Spicer & Okhmatovskiy, 2015). For example, during the post–financial crisis, some trust was repaired in the financial sector when the government provided short-term guarantees of bank accounts and played a more active stewardship role in the financial system (Gillespie, Hurley, Dietz, & Bachmann, 2012). Trust transference can also occur passively if the trustee can attain certification or accreditation from a trustworthy organization for its factor of trustworthiness most severely hit by the transgression (Gillespie et al., 2021; Gillespie & Dietz, 2009) or share common affiliations or membership (Brewer, 1979). Even though there is a considerable amount of research on the general effects of third party on trust development (Bachmann et al., 2015; Ferrin et al., 2006), there is a surprising lack of empirical studies looking into the effects of third party in the trust-repair context.

Behavioral Repair Tactics
Compensation
This is a buffering repair tactic where the trustee tries to balance out the negative event with other positive fulfillments desired by the trustor (Bankins, 2015), such as monetary compensation (Reb et al., 2006). It also helps the trustee to make up for the negative effects of the transgression on the trustor and, thus, restore some equilibrium in the relationship (Ren & Gray, 2009). Compensation has been shown to effectively reverse the negative affect and feeling of injustice within the trustor after the transgression has taken place (Cugueró-Escofet et al., 2014), thus facilitating a return in the social order of the relationship. Individuals who experienced loss (instead of missing out on the gain) are more inclined to forgive if they received financial compensation (De Cremer, 2010). A trustee is likely to compensate the trustor for their loss if the trustee feels guilty and/or shameful for the committed violation (Ghorbani, Liao, Çayköylü, & Chand, 2013). Trustees are more likely to compensate if the violation was unintentional, especially when the relationship is anticipated to be long term (Desmet & Leunissen, 2014).
While the positive effects of compensation are well established in the literature, the conversation about compensation as a repair tactic in the past decade has revolved around the effects of the compensation size. Even though the literature has generally found that overcompensation has a more positive effect on the process of trust repair than equal compensation or undercompensation (e.g., Desmet, De Cremer, & van Dijke, 2011), some research has shown that this positive effect has a ceiling (Haesevoets et al., 2013). There is also evidence that overcompensation may have a negative impact on repair of trust because overcompensating trustees could be perceived as possessing low moral orientation (Haesevoets et al., 2014). Ambiguity of the intent of the transgression (Desmet et al., 2011) and voluntariness of compensation (Desmet, De Cremer, & van Dijke, 2010) moderate the likelihood and degree of trust repaired following overcompensation based on its attributional features.

Penance
This is a repair tactic that assists the repair process of trust by fulfilling the trustor's desire to witness that the trustee pays an adequate penalty for committing the transgression (cf. Bottom et al., 2002), especially if the transgression involved interactional injustice (Reb et al., 2006). As sincerity and authenticity of the trustee's repentance and desire to change are rarely certain, penance often acts as a validation for the trustor that the trustee is genuinely making efforts to restore the relationship. This helps in alleviating moral-emotional reactions (Gromet, 2012; Haidt, 2001) and returning positive affect in the relationship (Bottom et al., 2002; Radulovic et al., 2019), reasserting shared rules and norms (Okimoto & Wenzel, 2009), and accentuating trusting beliefs and behaviors (Dirks et al., 2011; Henderson et al., 2020). Thus, penance plays an integral role in the restoration of the social equilibrium.
Punishment for a transgression is different from compensation as it is focused more on the penalties that the trustee has to incur than the reimbursement that the trustor receives. Thus, it includes monetary and nonmonetary costs that the trustee has to pay apart from the compensation to the trustor, such as fines and regulatory sanctions (Schembera & Scherer, 2017). Punishment could either be voluntarily offered by the trustee or be required by the trustor or a third party. Research has shown that open offers of penance often result in smaller repayments than the cost of harm imposed by the transgression, even though they are not significantly more effective than fixed offers (Bottom et al., 2002). However, such smaller repayments are less likely for short interactions, as a violation early in the relationship leads to transaction-oriented responses often resulting in larger demand for penance. Perspective taking can help trustees to negotiate acceptable penance for the transgression (Williams, 2012). When penalties are levied onto the trustee, reintegration of relationship is more likely to happen if the penalties are accepted and not shown much resistance. For example, in their study of the Severn Trent Waters (STW) data manipulation case, Gillespie et al. (2014: 383) noted that the fact that STW did not contest even the “completely and utterly outrageous” fines helped the organization to signal regret for the behavior of the incumbent team and put the “legacy issues” of the transgression behind through positive trustworthiness attributions.

Transparency Maintenance
Transparency is defined as the “perceived quality of intentionally shared information from a sender” (Schnackenberg & Tomlinson, 2016: 1788). It acts as a gate-keeping condition for trust to be repaired in the trustee after a transgression. Transparency may be established by the trustee by undertaking heuristics changes, such as installing cameras in meat production facilities to show which meat goes into a product (Božič et al., 2019), or by providing periodic reports on the requested operations or violation investigation. Research has shown that transparency maintenance by the trustee increases the repair of trust in the trustee (Bachmann et al., 2015; Child & Rodrigues, 2004; Eberl et al., 2015). Transparency enables inward observability, defined as the ability of external stakeholders (in this case, the trustor) to monitor internal activities and decisions (Grimmelikhuijsen & Meijer, 2014), and involves the disclosure of relevant and accurate information in a manner that is easily interpretable to the trustor (Schnackenberg & Tomlinson, 2016). Because of this, transparent investigations could provide trustors access to damning information and potentially put the trustworthiness of the trustee at risk. When the trustor perceives that the trustee shares unfiltered information frequently, it signals that the trustee is willing to assume that risk as it has nothing to hide. This leads the trustor to attribute higher integrity to the trustee and provides a positive impulse to the trust-repair process. A lack of transparency, on the other hand, would suggest the assurances to be cheap talk (Bottom et al., 2002), which would hurt organizational legitimacy (Beelitz & Merkl-Davies, 2012) and the likelihood of trust repair.

Controls
Controls refers to the establishment of a system that helps to reduce the likelihood of future transgression by the trustee (Dirks et al., 2011). In terms of the level of formalization, controls can be distinguished as formal controls, that is, “codified rules and directives . . . that include explicit goal requirements and performance standards, job descriptions, behavioral or process specifications, and standard operating procedures” (Long & Sitkin, 2018: 728), and informal controls, that is, “unwritten mechanisms that influence employees by motivating them to enact accepted values, norms, and beliefs” (Long & Sitkin, 2018: 728). As some scholars also consider trust as an integral part of informal controls (e.g., Bradach & Eccles, 1989; Cao & Lumineau, 2015), the transgression symbolizes the failure of the informal controls within the relationship. In such a scenario, informal controls cannot be used to repair trust, and the only form of controls that remain relevant is formal controls (Inkpen & Currall, 2004). Thus, we consider only formal controls as a repair tactic in the trust-repair context.
Contracts are one of the most common forms of formal control systems that parties employ in a relationship, especially in interorganizational transactions. They refer to a priori agreements between the parties in the relationship that “define outputs to be delivered, specify monitoring procedures, and detail duties, rights, and contingencies” (Cao & Lumineau, 2015: 15), and form part of the output and process controls in the relationship (Long & Sitkin, 2018). The boundaries stated in the contract become the source of credibility and legitimization of actions by the parties in the relationship (Garud, Gehman, & Karunakaran, 2014). There are two main types of contracts: prevention contracts, in which control provisions are dominant, and promotion contracts, in which provisions are developed in order to encourage greater coordination (Weber, 2017). Prior research on contracts has shown that even though both control and coordination provisions increase confidence in the trustor, only the latter helps to establish new relational norms and boundary structure and to positively influence benevolence-based trust (Malhotra & Lumineau, 2011; Pinnington & Ayoub, 2019). Other research suggests that trusting beliefs can also be restored by incorporating preventive clauses, such as demanding close monitoring or imposing harsh sanctions for future breach, that can fill the loopholes in the transactional relationship (Gillespie et al., 2012; Gillespie & Dietz, 2009) and reduce the need for trust (Schoorman, Wood, & Breuer, 2015) or increase the negative consequences for the trustee if the transgression is repeated (Janowicz-Panjaitan & Krishnan, 2009). However, its effectiveness would depend on the type of transgression, the severity and frequency of the transgression, and the hierarchical level and organizational context in which the transgression occurred (Janowicz-Panjaitan & Krishnan, 2009). Research has also shown that preventive regulations can be effective in repairing trust if the trustor perceives that the trustee recommends a change in regulations based on repentance (Dirks et al., 2011).
Scholars have also found evidence for an alternative conclusion for the effect of contracts when trust is breached. Specifically, after a transgression, distributional approaches are incorporated to resolve the dispute (Lumineau & Malhotra, 2011), and contractual structures are driven more by control provisions than by coordination provisions (Weber, 2017). Research has shown that control provisions may encourage opportunistic behavior that the contracts are designed to discourage (Poppo & Zenger, 2002) and are negatively related to the intent to continue in the relationship (Malhotra & Lumineau, 2011). Thus, whenever there is a breach of trust, repair through contracts leads to cooperation only in the presence of perceived net benefit in the relationship by both parties. It does not necessarily repair trust because contractual remedies address only reliability concerns and not value concerns that are necessary to repair trust (Sitkin & Roth, 1993). Formal control contracts, therefore, may provide short-term transactional benefits by repairing cooperation but are likely to be detrimental for the long-term viability of the relationships.

Organizational Reforms
This repair tactic refers to the changes made in the organization after the transgression to improve its trustworthiness. Leadership, culture, strategy, and structures, policies, and processes form the internal components that influence the trust in an organization (Gillespie & Dietz, 2009). Research has shown that leadership replacement and cultural and structural reconsideration after a transgression can lead to repair of trust at the organizational level. In the next few paragraphs, we will expand on each of these tactics for organizational reforms.
Leadership replacement. When using this repair tactic, an organizational-level trustee attempts to repair trust by placing the blame for the transgression on the leader or replacing the incumbent leader with one who is more trustworthy. Thus, it is a repair attempt that is possible only at the organizational level and involves two elements: incumbent leader resignation or dismissal and replacement by a trustworthy leader. The former element primarily impacts repairing of trust through the attributional mechanism, and the latter primarily influences trust repairing through the structural mechanism.
Many organizations employ leader replacement as a strategy especially after integrity-based transgressions, such as organizational corruption, take place in the organization (Eberl et al., 2015; Okhmatovskiy & Shin, 2019). This was evident right after the 2008 global financial crisis hit when many heads of various key institutions of the financial system, such as banks, government regulation, and rating agencies, were pressured to step down and make way for new leaders with better reputation (Gillespie et al., 2012). After an integrity-based violation, a “changing of the guard” speeds up and increases the likelihood of repair of trustworthiness of the trustee (Bachmann et al., 2015; Gillespie et al., 2014) because of two main reasons: (a) Dismissal of the blamed or incompetent incumbent leader washes out the impact of the transgression from the organization, and (2) the introduction of the new leader as a replacement increases the likelihood of repair of trust by influencing a positive change in the organizational culture (Eberl et al., 2015; Gillespie & Dietz, 2009). Especially in scenarios where blame for the transgression is placed on the incumbent leader, formal and informal distancing of the organization from the incumbent leader validates in the eyes of the trustor that the organization has nothing to do with the potential benefits received from the transgression and that the trustee is ideologically different from the incumbent leader (Ferrin, Cooper, Dirks, & Kim, 2018). This indirectly decreases the perceived trustworthiness of the departed leader and directly increases the trust in the organization (Ferrin et al., 2018). Moreover, because leaders “symbolize and shape the conduct of organization” (Gillespie & Dietz, 2009: 130), which can influence the perception of stakeholders about an organization's trustworthiness (Kouzes & Posner, 2002), the replacement of the incumbent leader with a trustworthy one is important for the repair to be sustainable.
Structural and cultural reconsiderations. An organizational violation creates dissolution not only between the trustee organization and the external stakeholders but also within the trustee and the internal stakeholders (i.e., the employees) as it negatively affects the identification of the employees with the organization (Petriglieri, 2015). After the violation, the likelihood of the external stakeholders to cooperate reduces considerably if they do not perceive a net benefit in transacting with the trustee organization (Lewicki & Bunker, 1996), and employees are more likely to voluntarily leave if they do not reidentify with their employer organization (Petriglieri, 2015). Organizational reforms, in the form of changes in regulations and organizational culture, assist in repairing the relationship. Specifically, rules and regulations help in the repair process as they provide clarity of behavioral expectations to the employees (Heaphy, 2013). This reduces the uncertainty cost of transacting with the organization and signals reduced likelihood of future transgression. Internalized cultural reforms help in reidentification of the employees and repairs trust in the organization. It also creates social controls (Pinnington & Ayoub, 2019) and provides the energy to the organization to regain its lost legitimacy due to the transgression.
Changes in rules and regulations can be either self-propelled (Ahmed, Bangassa, & Akbar, 2020) or recommended by an external party (Mueller et al., 2015). For example, after the global financial crisis, the government and regulatory bodies shifted from the “anti-regulation, free market” ideology to a more “proactive, pro-regulation, and corporate accountability” stance and recommended certain value changes to the financial industry. At the same time, some banks also took voluntary steps to come up with rules that increased the oversight and transparency of their operations and encouraged employees to work more responsibly (Gillespie et al., 2012). Post-transgression, external regulation recommendations are often control oriented and focus on preventing future transgressions by the trustee (Mueller et al., 2015). However, these recommendations should be followed as they can increase the probability of trust recovery (Božič & Kuppelwieser, 2019). On the other hand, cultural reforms are often internally driven, especially by the leadership and top management (Gillespie & Dietz, 2009). The main aim of the cultural reforms is to realign an organization's norms and values with its vision and social expectations and establish organizational trustworthiness in the eyes of the stakeholders. New cultural values are often induced top-down through clear leadership communication and targeted emphasis (Beelitz & Merkl-Davies, 2012).
Research has shown that neither formal reforms (i.e., rules and regulations) nor social reforms (i.e., organizational culture) can alone repair trust in the organization. For organizational reforms to be effective, two conditions must be met: (a) Changes in rules and regulations and cultural values should be aligned with each other (Okhmatovskiy & Shin, 2019; Pinnington & Ayoub, 2019), and (b) concerns of both external and internal stakeholders must be addressed (Ahmed et al., 2020; Gillespie et al., 2014). The importance of these conditions is visible in the difference in responses to integrity-based violations by Siemens (see Eberl et al., 2015, for complete case details) and STW (see Gillespie et al., 2014, for complete case details).
In the case of Siemens, even though tighter rules employed by the new leadership were popular with external stakeholders as they signaled lower likelihood of future transactions, they did not go over well with the internal stakeholders because they were imposed by the leadership and made conducting of even legal business tough, and in some cases, challenged the core values of the company itself. This resulted in the fact that “strong trust attributions never reappeared” (Eberl et al., 2015: 1214). On the other hand, STW used a more effective strategy wherein the content of the reforms increased trustworthiness in the eyes of external stakeholders, and clear cooperative communication of the reforms to the employees helped them reidentify with the organization. This led to the repairing of trustworthiness of the organization, so much so that STW won the Utility of the Year award within 5 years of its reform initiative.

Trust Repair as a Process
Various scholars have discussed the process of repair after a transgression (e.g., Gillespie & Dietz, 2009; Pfarrer et al., 2008; Ren & Gray, 2009). Pfarrer et al. (2008) provided a four-stage model of organizational legitimacy recovery after a transgression, comprising discovery, explanation, penance, and rehabilitation. Gillespie and Dietz (2009) studied repair of trust and suggested a four-stage sequential model of trust repair also at the organizational level. They segregated the trust-repair process into immediate response, diagnosis, reforming interventions, and finally, evaluation. In both these models, the first stage of the repair process involves the surfacing of the transgression to the trustor and the initial response of the trustee. The second stage consists of the parties trying to analyze the transgression more thoroughly to come to a logical conclusion of who is responsible for the transgression, why did it occur, and, based on the perceived characteristics of the trustee, what is the likelihood for it to occur in the future. The third stage pertains to the trustee trying to make up for the loss of strength in the relationship with the trustor and paving the platform for possible eventual trust repair by incorporating various reform interventions. According to Gillespie and Dietz (2009), such interventions can be broadly placed under trustworthiness demonstration, which focuses on increasing positive trusting beliefs, and distrust regulation, which focuses on decreasing negative trusting beliefs. Finally, in the fourth stage, the trustee analyzes both explicit and implicit cues from the trustor to know the amount of trust restored in the relationship, which can potentially act as a motivation for the trustee to continue the positive interactions in order to complete the repair process. On the other hand, the trustor constantly renews the perceived trustworthiness of the trustee through information from each subsequent interaction in this stage. Repeated positive interactions lead to an incremental repair in the trustor's trusting beliefs and intentions until trust is repaired completely.
As trust-repair dynamics are not isomorphic across levels of analysis and can vary significantly depending on the level at which the trust-repair process takes place (Gillespie et al., 2021), the proposed repair-process models in the literature at individual levels were different from that at the organizational level. Specifically, according to the trust-repair process model provided by Ren and Gray (2009), the repair process starts with a challenge stage, in which the trustor explicitly or implicitly communicates to the trustee about the transgression. At the individual level, the attribution of guilt is often extremely quick as it is much clearer what happened, why it happened, and who caused it (Gillespie et al., 2021). The challenge stage is followed by the offering stage, in which the trustee engages in repair tactics with an intent to revert the broken relational order back to equilibrium. However, this restoration of equilibrium happens only if and when the trustor acknowledges the “offerings” made by the trustee, which forms the acceptance stage of the restoration process. In the final stage, that is, thanks stage, the trustee is expected to show gratitude to the trustor for accepting the offerings in order for the relationship to be cemented into order again.
Lewicki and Bunker (1996) also elaborated on the process of trust repair at the individual level. However, instead of following a clear multistage approach, they explained the trust-repair process as initial steps and subsequent steps and the multiple courses that the repair process can take. Specifically, according to the authors, the initial steps are a set of four steps that starts with the recognition by the trustee that the violation occurred. This sets the stage for the possibility of trust to be repaired. The trustee, thereafter, attempts to determine and convey to the trustor what caused the transgression. At the individual level, the trustee is often able to determine the cause easily. This step is followed by admission of the trustee that the event was “destructive” of trust and that they are willing to accept the responsibility. At the initial stage, the trustor is responsible for the attribution of the blame and intent of the trustee. Initial steps, therefore, are focused on finding information and clarity on the locus, control, and stability of the transgression (Weiner, 1985) and are predominantly attributional in nature.
In the subsequent steps, the trustee attempts to restore the relationship using appropriate repair tactics. The trustor can drive the relationship in one of four courses depending on their attribution about the transgression and perceived sincerity and appropriateness of the efforts taken by the trustee to restore trust in the relationship. One of the courses that the trustor can take is to not forgive the trustee and deem the relationship unrepairable. It is not necessary that the trustee believe the relationship is unrepairable as well. If there is a discrepancy in the perception of the relationship, the trustee is responsible for changing the perception of the trustor. The other courses that the trustor may take is to forgive the trustee but have either unreasonable demands, no demands, or reasonable demands for the relationship to be restored. These demands may be explicit or implicit in nature. Trust is unlikely to be repaired if the demands are unreasonable, because it would make the trustee question the worth of repairing the relationship. Trust-repair efforts will be undertaken further only if demands can be negotiated down. As both trustor and trustee should be willing for trust to be repaired (Kim et al., 2009), unreasonable demands will put roadblocks on the trust-repair process. Trust is also unlikely to be repaired if forgiveness comes with no demands, because in such a scenario, forgiveness often is only cathartic in nature. Such forgiveness takes out the relevance of the transgression from the relationship but not its effects on the relationship and future expectations, which resists the repair of trust. Trust is most likely to be repaired if the trustor forgives with reasonable demands, because these acts of reparation not only demonstrate that the trustee is sincere and committed to repair trust in the relationship but also provide the trustee an opportunity to expiate any guilt or remorse to avoid awkwardness in the relationship.
Even though these suggested repair processes are different in terms of level of analysis and content of repair, there are certain commonalities in these repair processes. Specifically, the pre-blame stage of the repair process is dominated by the attributional mechanism, and the post-blame stage predominantly involves the social-equilibrium and structural mechanisms, even though the attributional mechanism continues to play a crucial indirect role. Thus, different mechanisms play complementary roles to completely repair trust. Even though there have been calls from repair scholars to explore the complementary nature of different mechanisms in the trust-repair process (e.g., Bachmann et al., 2015; Dirks et al., 2009), in terms of studying the complementarity, the quantitative studies on trust repair have mostly focused on the effectiveness of incorporating multiple tactics on trust repair versus a single tactic or no tactic at a single time point (e.g., DiFonzo et al., 2020; Kim & Harmon, 2014). This provides empirical evidence about the compatibility of the tactics with each other and their boosting effects on trust repair in general but not about whether these tactics can facilitate a complete repair of trust or whether, when, and how different repair mechanisms act complementarily to repair trust.

Future Research Agenda
Exploring Composite Processes as an Approach to Study Trust Repair
Our review has highlighted the fact that there is a considerable body of research that has demonstrated that the use of different tactics that are a part of several mechanisms can successfully increase the amount of repaired trust in a relationship following a transgression (see Bachmann et al., 2015; Gillespie et al., 2014). And while (as noted earlier) there is work emerging studying trust repair as a process, we have identified a significant opportunity in the study of trust repair in examining how the use of multiple mechanisms may have a meaningful way to achieve a complete repair of trust. To that end, we speculate about particular pathways that may be utilized in trust repair. Given that there are three different mechanisms that form the basis through which trust repair is studied and that each mechanism follows a unique perspective focusing primarily on a unique aspect of the repair process (Bachmann et al., 2015; Dirks et al., 2009), there are at least three possible pathways through the mechanisms of trust repair identified in the literature through which relationships may be repaired (Dirks et al., 2009; Kramer & Lewicki, 2010). As scholars have proposed that a single tactic is often not able to attain complete trust repair (Kim, 2018) and multiple tactics that draw on different mechanisms are needed (Bachmann et al., 2015; Dirks et al., 2009; Gillespie et al., 2014), future research should explore the combinations of different tactics that can help in executing each of these repair tactics effectively. Each of these pathways merits further exploration as a means of more completely repairing trust.
First, the attribution mechanism focuses primarily on the effect of the transgression on the perceived trust by the trustor. One way to completely repair trust is to invalidate the effect of the transgression, potentially through tactics such as denial and explanation (Bansal & Zahedi, 2015; Tomlinson & Carnes, 2015). Analyzing the attribution stage of the repair process (Kim et al., 2009), researchers suggest the effects of the transgression can be invalidated if complete responsibility for the transgression is transferred to an external entity. As trust has been shown to be repaired by redirecting the blame away from the self (Ferrin et al., 2007; Kim et al., 2004, 2006), we term this repair pathway the “redirect pathway.” Second, the social-equilibrium mechanism focuses predominantly on restoring the norms of relationship in the repair process. According to this mechanism, if the relationship can be brought back to equilibrium by engaging in tactics such as apology and compensation, the literature suggests that trust repair is likely to occur (Desmet et al., 2011; Haesevoets et al., 2013; Kim & Harmon, 2014). Thus, the second way of repairing trust is by restoring the respect for relational norms and returning the perceptions of the trustworthiness of the trustee to the pre-transgressional levels (Dirks et al., 2009; Ren & Gray, 2009). As the repair occurs when the relationship is replenished of what was lost, we term this repair pathway the “replenish pathway.”
Finally, the structural mechanism focuses on the environment within which the relationship exists. According to this mechanism, the trustor is likely to restore the perceived trustworthiness of the organization if the internal structure and culture are transformed in a way that puts control systems in place that discourage the members of the organization from transgressions again in the future and encourage them to behave on the basis of the renewed image of the organization (Gillespie & Dietz, 2009). Research has shown that combinations of particular tactics along this pathway, such as institution of controls and organizational reforms (Mueller et al., 2015; Pinnington & Ayoub, 2019), have been shown to lead to trust repair (Eberl et al., 2015; Gillespie et al., 2014). As appropriate organizational reforms are capable to repair trust by replenishing the lost trustworthiness in the organization due to internal transformations, structural mechanisms in some scenarios may facilitate repair of trust through the replenish pathway at the organizational level. However, environmental changes in the form of incorporation of formal external controls, especially at the individual level, may not lead to trust repair, as in the presence of such control systems, the trustors tend to attribute the positive behavior of the trustee to the control mechanisms rather than to their improved trustworthiness (Long & Sitkin, 2018). In such scenarios, cooperation may still be restored because the reduction of risk (cost) in the future transactions due to the incorporation of control systems may increase the perceived net benefit for the trustor to continue the relationship. As the relationship is often transformed from one based on trust to one primarily based on cooperation after the transgression, we term this repair pathway the “redefine pathway.” It must be noted that as the redefine pathway focuses on environmental transformation and does not contradict the assumptions of the other pathways, the trustee may utilize it at any point in the repair process irrespective of whether a different pathway was utilized earlier. Future research should explore how different mechanisms can complement each other to attain complete trust repair as well as explore the optimal time for the utilization of each of the three pathways and the absolute and relative implications of choosing to follow one pathway over the other.
From an empirical perspective, investigating the relative efficacy of pathways that combine these tactics (versus examining one tactic at a time) to repair trust completely will necessitate the use of repeated-measures designs and involve diverse research methods and contexts that explicitly take time and the ordering of tactics into account. Investigating whether the order of trust-repair tactics influences the amount of trust repaired will involve active manipulation in experimental settings so that different sequences can be compared. We would also encourage the use of mixed methods including qualitative research in order to use the experiences and accounts of trustors and trustees to inductively develop and test hypotheses regarding these pathways. Doing so will facilitate a richer understanding of the microdynamics of trust repair and understanding of how person perception changes within trustors after a breach. We also suggest greater emphasis of research contexts not only within the research design but also as an independent construct that can help to explore the important role that professional and market norms may play in repair of trust. For example, in the financial markets, there are licensing and enforcement mechanisms as well as different expectations with regard to competitive versus collaborative orientation in exchanges that impact trust repair (Abolafia, 1996). It would be important to use these contexts to understand how and why particular norms develop that can influence the length of time it takes to repair trust following a breach.
Another practical issue in the trust-repair process is that even when the trustee knows the best solution to repair trust, not all trustees are always willing or able to expend the resources needed to incorporate the requisite tactics. Having knowledge about appropriate alternatives not only at the pathway level but also at the repair tactic level would provide the trustee greater opportunity to customize their repair strategy as per their situation and desires. Although the trust-repair literature provides the trustee with many different repair tactics, we still have little knowledge on which repair tactics can act as an appropriate replacement for the other. The pathway approach to understand the trust-repair process could provide an opportunity to develop and empirically test a trust-repair model, which, along with the knowledge of the characteristics of the trust-repair tactics, would help in providing a more nuanced role of each repair tactic within the trust-repair process and, in turn, find tactic alternatives. This would not only lead to theoretical progress of the literature but would also provide comprehensive prescriptions.

Measurement Issues and Recommendations
We also reviewed the research designs used by trust-repair scholars for quantitative studies, with a particular emphasis to understand the extent to which repeated-measures design is used in the literature. This is important because even though exploration about effectiveness of the repair tactics does not require a repeated-measures design, it is critical to possess information about the level of trust at least at three time points—prior to the transgression (baseline), after the transgression (post-transgression), and after the employment of the repair tactics (post-intervention)—to conclude whether and how much trust repair occurred in the relationship. As the repair of complex constructs like trust is difficult to distill to one precise number, and the trustor may not always be able to accurately predict meaningful changes in trust level during the repair process, repair of trust should be considered statistically complete when there is a statistical difference between baseline and post-transgression trust level, and between post-transgression and post-intervention trust level, and there is a statistical invariance between baseline and post-intervention trust levels. The lack of either condition will make either the concept of repair of trust irrelevant (if the former condition is not met) or the trust-repair process incomplete (if either of the latter two conditions is not met). Thus, conclusions about complete trust repair are not possible without a baseline trust level and evidence that the trust levels went through a trough and then rose back again. It must be noted that research on such a drop and rise of the trusting relationship relative to the baseline, pre-transgressional trust levels are not confined to experimental quantitative studies but can (and should) also be used in qualitative and field studies exploring the process of trust repair by collecting data on trust levels relative to the baseline level retrospectively.
Prior review has suggested that initial level of trust is “seldom directly measured” in trust-repair studies (cf. Lewicki & Brinsfield, 2017: 290). We conducted a specific analysis of our literature to examine this question. Our analysis showed that scholars in the trust-repair literature have predominantly used a between-subjects research design. Only eight articles (out of 94 empirical articles) incorporated a complete or partial within-subject design, four of which used a policy-capturing technique and drew conclusions based on their between-subjects results. Out of the remaining four articles, only Bankins (2015) incorporated a repeated-measure design to understand the patterns of the repair-and-reconstruction-of-expectations process after a psychological contract breach. The other three articles conducted within-subject analyses to analyze the relative effectiveness of different manipulation conditions on the dependent variable.
In terms of dominant research designs, a large majority of the quantitative articles have used either vignettes or experimental designs to study trust repair (72 articles). Other research designs that have been used by trust-repair scholars are case study methodology (12 articles), qualitative methodology (78 articles), archival data collection design (three articles), critical incident methodology designs (eight articles), and field questionnaires (four articles). Because vignettes and experiments are most feasibly used in a student or a vendor-provided sample (such as MTurk or Prolific), more than 65% of the empirical studies in the trust-repair literature have relied on these two sample sources. The construct demands of trust repair make it difficult to study the trust-repair process in the field with within-subject designs, but high-quality experimental and vignette studies and qualitative and mixed-method studies could act as a feasible and practical substitute, and we call on researchers in trust repair to make efforts to continue to work to incorporate these designs in their work as, in our view, they are essential for reaching strong conclusions about trust repair.

Exploring the Effects of Active Versus Passive Time on Trust Repair
Another issue that deserves greater attention in future research is a focus on the impact of passive and active time on trust repair. Some studies have suggested that passive time, that is, time with lack of repair activity, after the transgression can play a positive role in repairing trust because it can blunt the pain caused by transgression (Wohl & McGrath, 2007), making it easier for the trustor to forgive (McCullough, Fincham, & Tsang, 2003). On the other hand, research has also shown that irrespective of the degree of “correctness” of the decision, delays in decision-making by the trustor can make the decision be perceived as less procedurally fair and lead to more severe harm (Haesevoets et al., 2016) because individuals are highly averse to delays especially when they lead to negative outcomes (Blount & Janicik, 2001). These findings are also supported by Gillespie and Dietz (2009), who posit that timely initial repair interventions after the transgression positively influences perceived organizational trustworthiness, although an extremely quick investigation or premature diagnosis could negatively impact organizational trustworthiness. Furthermore, research on the attributional mechanism has suggested that an individual shifts attribution to internal traits over time (Moore, Sherrod, Liu, & Underwood, 1979). This could influence the likelihood of trust repair by either accentuating the locus of causality and control on the trustee (which would decrease the likelihood of trust repair) or making the trustor believe that the trustee feels remorse for the action (which would increase the likelihood of trust repair). As research on the effects of passive time on trust repair is still in its infancy, future research should continue to explore its role in the trust-repair process. It should also look into the moderating factors that impact its effectiveness to repair trust.
Repair-of-trust scholars should also look into how active time, that is, time where the trustee is actively trying to repair trust, influences the repair process. Future research could study how a stretched investigation by the trustee after a transgression influences the attribution of blame by the trustor and impacts repair of trust. Undue delays from the trustee to provide investigation results could lead to exhaustion of the trustor's patience and increase anxiety. Research by attributional theorists suggests that the strength of situational factors that provide information about locus of causality increases over time (Lau, 1984). Thus, delays in investigation could direct the attention of the trustor toward third-party sources of information, which often are more negative (Burt & Knez, 1996) and attenuate the likelihood of trust repair. On the other extreme, abnormally quick investigations may not be advisable either, as they could place doubt in the thoroughness and sincerity of the investigation, which would negatively impact trust repair. This suggests that there might be a curvilinear relationship between active investigation time and trust repair. From the trustee's perspective, excessive time taken by the trustor to repair the trust could reduce the expectancy of trust repair by the trustee and, in turn, reduce the motivation of the trustee to continue putting in the effort toward repair of trust (Leunissen et al., 2012; Vroom, 1964). As trust repair is a two-way street (Kim et al., 2009; Lewicki & Bunker, 1996), this would decrease the likelihood of trust repair in the relationship. Future research should study the role of active time on trust repair from both the trustor’s and trustee's perspective and explore what is and/or what determines the right balance of active time in the repair process.

Conclusion
A comprehensive and systematic review of the literature on the repair of trust following a transgression reflects a growing body of research that has focused largely on verbal and behavioral tactics that might help mend the breach. Our review organizes this research by repair mechanism (attribution, social-equilibrium, and structural) and tactics (e.g., apology, denial, hostage posting) and reports on the efficacy of each. We develop a definition of trust repair that is based on the broadly accepted definition of trust and propose that the extent of repair is any increase in trust up to the pre-transgressional level, which is considered complete repair. We find that there is a dearth of research that studies repair in within-subject designs that measure trust pre-transgression, post-transgression, and post–repair effort. We recommend that researchers develop more within-subject designs and focus on more holistic, process-based “pathways” to studying trust repair. We hope that by doing so, researchers will continue their progress in investigating the complex dynamics behind this essential aspect of relationships.
