## Cybersecurity oversight by non-expert boards

Boards must oversee cybersecurity, and most do so without the expertise to judge it. The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC, 2023) requires listed companies to describe how the board oversees cyber risk, but it dropped the plan to disclose whether any director has cyber expertise, reasoning that directors with broad risk skills can oversee cyber as they oversee other technical matters (p. 85). Two theories frame what follows (Lowry et al., 2026). Agency theory sees an overseen party with power and an incentive to filter what the board learns; institutional theory sees boards without expertise adopting the practices that make oversight look legitimate. Lowry's evidence favours the second, but the CISO's control over what reaches the board remains (Opdenbusch et al., 2025). The technology for an independent view is already on the market: smart glasses answer questions about what the wearer sees (Meta, 2024), and Copilot in Teams, asked during a meeting, will say where the holes in a participant's argument lie (Microsoft, n.d.) no matter the actual context.

Field studies show what oversight without expertise looks like. Lowry et al. (2026) interviewed directors, CISOs and consultants and found non-expert directors' oversight mostly symbolic even when they did the same things as expert directors: they believed their business experience let them ask the right questions, but the expert directors and consultants in the same study saw them unable to judge the answers, left to assess the credibility of the executive in front of them. In the same study's CISO survey, the median estimate was that 40% of CISOs filter their reports to the board to look better; most non-expert directors thought this was unlikely. In the United Kingdom, directors stayed silent rather than expose their lack of knowledge, most board members could only judge by the budget for cyber decision within the board room, and the CISOs held the power because they decided what reached the board (Opdenbusch et al., 2025). Lowry et al. (2026) read the same deference as a gap in expertise rather than power; the two studies agree that the CISO controls what the board hears. In Australia, directors challenged management more confidently where external assurance was routine; without cyber literacy, boards often accepted management's account (Aamir et al., 2026). Power itself works against trust: in exchange experiments, the party with better alternatives trusted its partner less (Schilke et al., 2015).

Several things are missing. First, all of this is interview and survey evidence; no study has put a checking tool in a director's hand and measured what it does to trust between two human beings. Second, the two research fields that could answer the question do not meet. Trust research is psychology: a human accuses, a human replies, and no tool is involved (P. H. Kim et al., 2004; Ferrin et al., 2007). HCI research on checkers mostly studies the tool and measures belief in claims or trust in the AI (Danry et al., 2020; Lu et al., 2025); the few studies that measured trust in the person checked had the expert consult the AI or let it run on its own (Chen et al., 2026; D. Kim et al., 2026), never a listener holding it. The augmented human, a person who checks another person with a tool, falls between the two. Third, the consequences for the board-CISO relationship are unknown before adoption. A board that cannot judge the substance and does not expect filtering has no view beyond the checker's, so a flag will carry weight whether or not it is right. A wrong flag may cost a correct CISO the board's trust and with it the budget and actual protection for the company; a well-answered flag may strengthen it and implement an unnecessary project. Whether the checker improves the board's questions is secondary but seen in the papers, as a key focus surrounds the competence and outcome with correct questioning; what it does to trust is the open question.

This study adds the experiment to the board research and the tool to the trust research. A participant acts as a board member who decides the CISO's budget and holds a checker during the briefing. Every flag is right by the textbook but wrong for this company, and the CISO answers it with a reason or a bare denial. Trust in the CISO is rated after every flag and every answer and compared with board members who hear the same briefing without a checker. The result is a measure that neither field has taken: trust between two people when one of them is augmented with a checker.
