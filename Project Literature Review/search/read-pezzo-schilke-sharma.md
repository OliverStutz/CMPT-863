## Pezzo, Nash, Vieux & Foster-Grammer (2022), Medical Decision Making. Effect of having, but not consulting, a computerized diagnostic aid. (abstract only, 2026-10-19)

**Reference (APA 7).** Pezzo, M. V., Nash, B. E. D., Vieux, P., & Foster-Grammer, H. W. (2022). Effect of having, but not consulting, a computerized diagnostic aid. *Medical Decision Making*, *42*(1), 94–104. https://doi.org/10.1177/0272989X211011160

Checked against Crossref and PubMed (vol. 42, issue 1, January 2022, pp. 94–104; online first 2021-05-08), not against the PDF. The USF repository gives the year as 2021 (online date); the issue year, 2022, is the one to cite.

**Agent reading (Oliver has not read it yet).** Two vignette experiments on how lay readers judge a professional who had a computerized diagnostic aid available and did not use it. Experiment 1 compares a physician with an auto mechanic; Experiment 2 uses only a bad outcome and varies the reason the physician did not consult the aid, against conditions where he consulted it. Only the abstract could be read, so everything below is limited to what the abstract says.

**What the full text says.** Not read; this is what the abstract says.

- Experiment 1: participants read about a diagnosis by a physician or an auto mechanic, with a good or a bad outcome. Half read that an aid "was available but never actually consulted"; the other half read nothing about an aid. For the physician, not consulting the aid "had no significant effect on competence ratings" after either outcome. For the mechanic, not consulting it "actually increased competence ratings following a negative but not a positive outcome". Negligence was judged higher for the mechanic than for the physician overall.
- Experiment 2: bad outcome only. Two reasons for not consulting the aid were given, and participants saw accuracy information showing the aid was better than the physician. "In neither condition was the physician rated lower than when no aid was mentioned." Ratings were "lower when the physician did not trust the CDA and, surprisingly, higher when the physician believed he or she already knew what the CDA would say." The physician who consulted the aid and followed it was rated high; the one who consulted it and then ignored it was rated low. Numeracy did not change the results.
- Not in the abstract: sample sizes, who the participants were, the rating scales, the cell means, whether "lower" and "higher" are against the no-aid control or against each other, how the reason was delivered (the physician speaking, or the vignette describing what he believed), and the authors' limitations.

**Consequence for our study.** The structure (section 4, "The expert and the machine") plans to say this is "the only study in which a professional's own stated reason about a computer aid changed lay ratings; 'I knew what it would say' raised competence ratings, 'I don't trust it' lowered them". The abstract does not support that wording, on four points.

1. "Lowered them" is contradicted by the abstract's own sentence that in neither condition was the physician rated lower than when no aid was mentioned. The distrust reason was rated lower than the "I knew" reason, or lower than some other condition, but not below the no-aid baseline. Safe wording: "a physician who said he already knew what the aid would say was rated higher than one who said he did not trust it".
2. "Own stated reason" and "answer" are not established. The abstract describes what the physician "believed" or "did not trust"; it may be the vignette's narration, not the physician speaking. Nobody accused the physician, the aid never disagreed with him (it was never consulted), and he did not reply to anything. It is a reason for setting a tool aside, not a reply to a flag. Section 5's "Kim et al. (2004) and Pezzo et al. (2022) supply the reply paradigm" overstates this for Pezzo.
3. The physician was not right. Experiment 2 has only a bad outcome, judged in hindsight. Our CISO is correct and nobody learns an outcome. This is the opposite of our situation on correctness.
4. The condition nearest to our CISO points against H2. Our CISO sees the checker's finding (in Pezzo's terms, the aid has been "consulted") and rejects it. Pezzo's "consulted but ignored" physician was rated low, as in Pezzo and Pezzo (2006). That was after a bad outcome, so it may not carry over, but the review cannot cite Pezzo as support for H2 without saying so.

What can still be used: there is no reason-versus-no-reason contrast, so the paper does not test H3 as worded. It does show that the content of a professional's stated reason about a machine changes how lay readers rate him, and that a reason that shows his own knowledge does better than one that only dismisses the machine. That is a loose analogue of H2 against H3: our bare denial is closer to a content-free dismissal of the checker than to silence. "The only study" cannot be checked from the abstract and should go. Proposed clause for section 4, safe on the abstract alone: "Pezzo et al. (2022) varied a physician's reason for not consulting an available diagnostic aid after a bad outcome: he was rated higher when he said he already knew what it would say than when he said he did not trust it, and never below a physician with no aid." The narrowing of section 5's gap sentence can stay, but on different grounds: no study lets the expert answer a machine's flag, and Pezzo comes closest only by varying a stated reason about an unused machine.

**Open.** Full text needed before any number or quote is cited: sample sizes, scales, cell means, what "lower" and "higher" are compared with, how the reason was delivered, and the limitations. Route: SFU library (Sage, Medical Decision Making) through the proxy, or EBSCO Academic Search Complete (see the memory note on SFU access). Until then, cite only the abstract-level clause above.

## Schilke, Reimann & Cook (2015), PNAS. Power decreases trust in social exchange. (agent-read 2026-10-19; full text in `../references/` as `Schilke-2015-power-decreases-trust-PNAS.pdf`)

**Reference (APA 7).** Schilke, O., Reimann, M., & Cook, K. S. (2015). Power decreases trust in social exchange. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, *112*(42), 12950–12955. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1517057112

Checked against the PDF footer ("12950–12955 | PNAS | October 20, 2015 | vol. 112 | no. 42") and Crossref.

**Agent reading (Oliver has not read it yet).** Four online experiments (MTurk) asking whether having more or less power in an exchange changes how much a person trusts the exchange partner. Power is "structural dependence": who has better alternatives and needs the exchange less. In all four, the more powerful participant trusted the partner less, and in Study 4 the effect ran through hope and then perceived benevolence.

**What the full text says.**

- Theory. Two opposing predictions (p. 12950). A rational account says the powerful party should trust more, because the weaker party needs the relationship and has reason to behave well. A motivated-cognition account says the weaker party trusts more because it wants the partner to be trustworthy: "Power-disadvantaged actors thus effectively protect themselves by perceiving power holders in a positive light" (p. 12950). Results favour the second.
- Power manipulations. Study 1: seller in a negotiation who first received a generous (high power) or a poor (low power) offer from another buyer, then negotiated with a second buyer. Study 2: the participant or the partner "Karen" was the "power player" who could switch partners. Studies 3 and 4: a typist who needed the job badly and had few clients (low power) or did not need it and had many (high power), with the client's power also varied. All partners were preprogrammed, not real people (SI).
- Study 1 (N = 401: 203 low, 198 high). Seven-item trust scale, 1 to 5, before and after the negotiation. Overall, high-power participants trusted less (mean 2.35 against 2.52, p. 12951). Before the negotiation the groups were identical (2.59 and 2.59); after it, high power 2.12 against low power 2.46. Trust fell over the negotiation in both groups, by 0.48 with high power and 0.15 with low power (p. 12951).
- Study 2 (N = 348 after comprehension checks: 181 low, 167 high). Trust game with real cents: send 10 cents (tripled, partner may share) or keep it. 81 percent of high-power and 91 percent of low-power participants sent (p. 12951). A rerun with doubled stakes (N = 484, SI): 77 against 86 percent.
- Study 3 (N = 413, four groups of about 100). Offer a free three-hour sample to a new client: 31 percent with high own power against 52 percent with low own power (p. 12952). The client's power made no difference.
- Study 4 (N = 401). Same vignette: 20 against 29 percent (p. 12952). The client's power again made no difference. Hope and perceived trustworthiness (16 items on ability, benevolence and integrity, after Mayer and Davis) were measured after the choice. Lower power raised hope; hope raised perceived trustworthiness and the choice to trust; once hope and trustworthiness were taken into account, power itself no longer predicted trust (p. 12953). Split by dimension, only benevolence carried the effect, not ability or integrity: "benevolence is a more relevant trustworthiness dimension than either ability or integrity when predicting trust decisions based on power differentials" (p. 12953).
- The partner's power did not matter: "people seemed to focus mainly on their own situation when making their trust judgments and decisions" (p. 12953).
- Limits and future work as the authors state them (pp. 12953–12954): the studies look only at "the initial round of interaction"; how power and trust develop over repeated interaction is open; whether low- and high-power people react differently to breaches of trust is open (they speculate that low-power people "may be less forgiving", since they process trust-related information more intensively); social distance may moderate the effect; negotiated against reciprocal exchange may differ; US samples only. All samples were MTurk workers, paid about one dollar. PNAS later published a letter (Wu & Wilkes, vol. 113, p. E1417) and the authors' reply (p. E1418) on durable against situational power; not read.

**Consequence for our study.** The structure (section 1, one clause) plans: "the more powerful party in an exchange trusts the other less, which is why the holder's power is a variable and not scenery". The first half is supported. The second half is not, and three differences should shape the clause.

1. In our design power is not a variable. Every participant is a board member, and nobody plays a low-power role. The paper can justify why the board member's power is part of the setting and why our numbers may not carry over to a listener without power. It cannot justify calling power a variable in our study, and we estimate nothing about it. Better wording: "a holder of power is not a neutral listener: across four exchange experiments the more powerful party trusted the other less (Schilke et al., 2015). Every participant in our study holds the board's power, so the results describe a powerful listener."
2. Their power is not the board's power. In Schilke it is having better alternatives in an exchange with an anonymous stranger. The board's power is formal authority over the CISO and the budget, while the board depends on the CISO's expertise. In the paper's own terms (dependence), the board is also the dependent party on information, which is the side that trusted more. Section 1 already frames power in two parts (design rule 7); the clause should say that the paper measures the dependence part, not authority. The partner was never an expert, and the effects are small (0.17 points on a 1-to-5 scale in Study 1; 9 to 21 percentage points in Studies 2 to 4).
3. The mechanism is benevolence, and our flags target competence. The effect ran through hope and perceived benevolence, not ability or integrity. Our flags are competence-type (design rule 20), and rule 14 predicts benevolence to stay flat in a first meeting. If power acts here at all, it should show on the benevolence item, not on competence.

One result is more useful than the planned clause. In Study 1 the groups started at the same trust (2.59) and diverged only during the interaction: trust fell more than three times as much with high power. In a first meeting, then, power may matter less for the starting rating than for how far trust falls during the session. That bears on H1 (the fall after a flag) and on the exploratory H4b (larger moves in the checker group). It is a reason to describe the board member's power as something that may enlarge the drops, not just lower the baseline. Their speculation that low-power people are "less forgiving" would, read the other way, make a powerful listener easier to win back after the CISO's reason. That helps H2, but it is speculation, and the review should not lean on it. The finding that the partner's power did not matter fits our scope: whether the CISO seems powerful should not change the board member's trust, so we do not need to manipulate or measure it.

**Open.** The Wu and Wilkes letter and the reply (PNAS 113, E1417–E1418) dispute situational against durable power; read them if the review leans on the board as a durable power holder. Decide whether the clause stays in section 1 or moves to the limits paragraph in Method ("all participants hold the board's power").

## Sharma, Schoorman & Ballinger (2023), Journal of Management. How can it be made right again? A review of trust repair research. (agent-read 2026-10-19; full text as web text in `../references/` as `Sharma-2023-trust-repair-review-JOM-Wayback.txt`)

**Reference (APA 7).** Sharma, K., Schoorman, F. D., & Ballinger, G. A. (2023). How can it be made right again? A review of trust repair research. *Journal of Management*, *49*(1), 363–399. https://doi.org/10.1177/01492063221089897

Checked against the Sage article page (title, authors, "Volume 49, Issue 1", DOI; first published online April 27, 2022) and Crossref (pp. 363–399). Not checked against the PDF, which was not available.

**Agent reading (Oliver has not read it yet).** A narrative review of 121 articles and chapters on repairing trust after a transgression, mostly in organisational settings. It defines trust repair and complete repair, sorts tactics into three mechanisms (attributional, social equilibrium, structural) and eleven tactics, reviews process models, and ends with a research agenda that calls for designs measuring trust before the violation, after it, and after the repair attempt.

**What the full text says.**

- Search and sample ("Review Process"): Google Scholar, ABI/Inform, Web of Science, PsycINFO, from Sitkin and Roth (1993) on; 121 relevant works, 97 of them empirical; 80 of the 97 used to build the review (footnote 1).
- Definition ("What Are the Limits of Trust Repair?"): "we define trust repair as any improvement in trust level following a transgression. Complete repair is achieved when the trust level reaches the pre-transgression level." On going beyond the start: "Improvement of trust beyond the pre-transgressional levels falls outside the limits of the effect that the transgression had on trust and, hence, should be considered not as 'repair' but as further development of trust." They grant that trust "can return to its pre-transgressional level, or even exceed it" because its sources may shift (for example from cognition to affect). The term "over-repair" does not appear anywhere in the text. In the introduction, citing Kim (2018): "most of the studies have shown that repair tactics do not return the trust levels to the pre-transgression levels."
- Denial ("Denial", Table 3): "a statement in which allegation is explicitly declared to be untrue", following Kim et al. (2004); distinct from reticence, since silence "signals a possibility of guilt" (Ferrin et al., 2007). Evidence is "mixed": denial sometimes works and sometimes "can in fact make the situation worse". It works better for integrity-based than for competence-based violations, in longer relationships, and "when its content is bound together in an analytical rather than a narrative format" (Van Laer & De Ruyter, 2010).
- Explanation ("Explanation"): "verbal statements of reason(s) for transgression provided by the trustee", used once the trustor believes the trustee is guilty. It assumes the act happened. Excuses (blame shifted to an outside cause) do better than justifications (responsibility accepted, act defended), from the Shaw et al. (2003) meta-analysis.
- Attribution stages ("Attributional Mechanism", after Kim et al., 2009): first, innocent or guilty? Then, situation or person? Then, can the person change? "Denial is effective to establish innocence of the trustee, that is, in the first stage of the attribution process, whereas explanation and apology are particularly effective in the second and third stage."
- Third parties ("Trust Transference"): "there is a surprising lack of empirical studies looking into the effects of third party in the trust-repair context".
- Process ("Trust Repair as a Process"): in the final stage the trustor "constantly renews the perceived trustworthiness of the trustee through information from each subsequent interaction". Most quantitative studies test tactics "at a single time point".
- Measurement ("Measurement Issues and Recommendations"): trust must be known at "at least ... three time points", before the transgression, after it, and after the repair tactic. "Conclusions about complete trust repair are not possible without a baseline trust level and evidence that the trust levels went through a trough and then rose back again." Complete repair is shown when trust differs between baseline and after the violation, differs between after the violation and after the repair, and does not differ between baseline and after the repair. Only 8 of 94 empirical articles used any within-subject design, and only one (Bankins, 2015) measured repeatedly; 72 used vignettes or experiments; more than 65 percent drew on student or panel samples (MTurk, Prolific).
- The authors' own caveats: the pathway model (redirect, replenish, redefine) is offered as speculation for future work. The degree of repair is "measured as the percentage of lost trust that is repaired", with the details deferred to the research agenda, which does not actually give a formula. The counts are not fully consistent (97 empirical articles in the review process, 94 in the measurement section; "qualitative methodology (78 articles)" next to 72 vignette or experimental studies). Check against the PDF before quoting a count.

**Consequence for our study.** The structure (section 4, one clause) plans: "partial, complete and over-repair, measured against a pre-violation baseline; why 'above the start' is exploratory". Supported in substance, with one correction and two further uses.

1. Correction: the paper names partial and complete repair, but not "over-repair". It says a rise above the start is not repair but "further development of trust". Write: "Sharma et al. (2023) count any rise after a violation as repair and a return to the pre-violation level as complete repair; a rise beyond it they treat as further trust development, which most repair studies do not reach." That supports keeping "above the start" exploratory in H2 for two reasons: complete repair is rare, and going beyond it is outside what repair tactics are expected to do.
2. Support for the design, and for section 5's "none rates trust at each step". Our first rating, after-flag rating and after-answer rating are exactly the three time points they say a repair claim needs, and they found such designs in 8 of 94 empirical studies. This is the strongest use of the paper, and it is worth more than one clause. With 40 participants, a non-difference between the first and last ratings cannot show that trust returned to the start. H2 should say "higher than after the flag" (as it does) and report the comparison with the start without claiming complete repair.
3. Taxonomy, which bears on H2, H3 and the optional Shaw et al. (2003). By their definitions our bare denial is a denial, "a statement in which allegation is explicitly declared to be untrue". Our reasoned answer is a denial with evidence, a first-stage innocence claim, not an "explanation" in their sense, since explanations give reasons for a transgression the trustee admits. Two things follow. (a) Their finding that denial works less well for competence-based violations predicts a weak bare denial after our competence-type flags, which supports H3. Their note that denial works better presented "analytically" fits the reasoned answer, which supports H2 against H3. (b) Shaw et al.'s finding that justifications help less than excuses concerns a party who did the act, so it does not straightforwardly "temper H2". Drop the optional Shaw clause, or reword it to say our answer is a rebuttal and not an account of a misdeed.
4. Gap. Their "surprising lack of empirical studies" on third parties in repair fits our accuser, a third-party machine, and can support section 5. Note that a third party is not an AI, and that their framework assumes a transgression that happened or is believed to have happened, while ours is an alleged one, as in Kim et al. (2004).

**Open.** Get the PDF (SFU library, Journal of Management) for page numbers for the three quotes likely to be used (the definition, the "further development of trust" sentence, the 8 of 94 count) and to settle the 94 against 97 count. The supplementary table of all empirical studies may list the within-subject studies by name; worth a look if section 5 cites the count.
