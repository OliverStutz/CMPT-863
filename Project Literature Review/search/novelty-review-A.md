# Novelty review A: CMPT 863 Project Abstract v7

Reviewer A, 2026-10-19. Written alone; reviews B and C not read.

## What the abstract claims to add (step 1, my own words)

A board member (non-expert, holds budget power) watches a CISO present five correct slides. In the checker group a simulated VLM flags slides 2 to 4 with claims that are wrong for this company. The CISO then answers, either with the company's reason (two slides) or with a bare denial (one slide). Trust in the CISO (0 to 10) is rated at the start, after each flag and after each answer, and compared with a neutral group that hears the same answers without flags. The claimed addition over Chen et al. (2026) is: the checker sits with the powerful listener rather than the expert, the flags are specific and wrong, the expert replies, and trust is tracked step by step. The hypotheses predict a drop after a flag (H1), recovery beyond baseline after a reasoned answer (H2), a further drop after a bare denial (H3), and larger swings and more questioning in the checker group (H4).

## A. Preliminary verdict (written after step 3, before reading the project notes; unchanged afterwards)

Verdict: (b), novel only if the claim is narrowed.

Every ingredient of the design already exists in published work, but in separate studies, and no study I read combines them.

- An AI that disagrees with a human expert, with trust in the expert measured afterwards, is done: Chen et al. (2026, review in references/reviews/chen.md: doctor reports that his AI disagrees, lower trust via laziness and uncertainty) and Madanay et al. (2025, JMIR, N = 1606: radiologist who "undercalled" the AI was agreed with less and rated lower, 6.82 vs 7.45 to 7.58 on 1 to 10, Table 2). In neither is the AI's verdict shown to be wrong, and in neither does the expert reply.
- An AI that visibly flags an expert in front of a lay recipient is done by Kim et al. (2026, AIES, N = 157, pp. 1515 to 1520), but there the AI is always right, the advisor really erred, trust is rated once, between subjects, and the authors list "the AI was always correct when it intervened" as a limitation and call for varying AI reliability (Limitations, p. 1520). That is an explicit opening for the wrong-flag condition.
- Wrong AI flags on true content are done (Horne and Nevo 2025, wrong labels on true headlines reduce trust in the headline; Gupta et al. 2026, false-positive wearable nudges during videos reduce belief in true claims; Lu et al. 2025, a 55% accurate AI misleads people even against an expert's judgement). All measure belief in the content, not trust in the person who said it. Du et al. (2026) is closest on this point: a fictitious 87% AI-detection score lowered teachers' evaluation of an unchanged student paper, so a baseless AI flag in the hands of the power holder degraded judgement of the person's work. But the target is a student, not an expert the evaluator depends on, and there is no reply.
- An accused person's reply as trust repair, with a trustor who holds power and with an unproven accusation, is classic: Kim et al. (2004, JAP, a hiring manager watches a candidate accused on hearsay, apology vs denial; the pilot rated trust before and after the allegation; Study 2 added later evidence of innocence) and Ferrin et al. (2007, adds reticence, i.e. a non-answer). The accuser there is a human recruiter or the news, not an AI.
- AI fact-checks with the claimant's evaluation measured: Chae and Tewksbury (2026, N = 3978) found neither AI nor human fact-checks moved feeling or perceived competence of Biden or Trump. Checks were real and correct, targets were famous, no reply.
- The board and CISO setting has qualitative support only (Aamir 2026; Lowry et al. 2026; Opdenbusch et al. 2025; Proudfoot et al. 2023); no experiment.

So what is new is the combination: a wrong AI flag, held by the non-expert who holds power, followed by the expert's own reply, with trust tracked within the session, in a governance setting. What is not new: that an AI disagreement lowers trust in the expert (Chen, Madanay, Kim 2026), that wrong flags are believed (Horne and Nevo, Gupta, Lu), and that the accused's response type changes repair (Kim 2004, Ferrin 2007). H1 and H3 are close to predictions already supported in those literatures; H2 (rise above baseline) and H4b (amplification by the checker) are the parts with genuinely open answers. The abstract should stop calling Chen the closest design without naming Kim et al. (2026) and Madanay et al. (2025), and should cite the trust-repair tradition (Kim 2004, Ferrin 2007) because H2 and H3 rest on it. Preliminary confidence: medium.

## B. Final answers (after reading the project notes and the web search)

### What step 4 changed

The project notes reach the same verdict as section A ("novelty claim must narrow", Literature Review - Candidate papers.md, cross-examination section). Testing their claims against the texts raised four points, two of which make the case against novelty stronger than the notes admit.

1. **The flags are wrong only from the outside.** Participants are never told during the briefing that a flag is wrong; they learn it only in the debrief (Abstract v7, Method step 5). From the participant's side the manipulation is "an AI objects, the expert answers". So the claimed difference from Chen et al. (2026), Madanay et al. (2025) and Kim et al. (2026), that "our flags are wrong", is a difference in ground truth the participant cannot see. The real differences are (i) the expert rebuts instead of accepting (in Kim et al. 2026 the advisor accepts the AI's correction, p. 1518 to 1519; in Chen and Madanay the expert says nothing) and (ii) the repeated ratings. The notes list "wrong flags" first among the differences (verified-view.md, paper 1; Candidate papers, gap table). It should not be listed first, and it should not be claimed as novel without that caveat.
2. **The notes say Madanay's AI was "right by convention" (Candidate papers, gap table).** The paper gives no ground truth for either reading; it only varies the direction of the discrepancy (Madanay 2025, Methods and Textbox 1). The flag-direction asymmetry (penalty only when the radiologist recommended less than the AI) is the finding to cite, not AI correctness.
3. **The notes say Ferrin et al. (2007) has no rating before the accusation (verified-view.md, paper 10).** That is right for Ferrin. But Kim et al. (2004) ran a pilot (N = 71) that rated trust immediately before and immediately after the allegation, within person (Kim-2004 text, pilot study section, p. 107 to 108: paired t tests, all p < .001). So "trust rated before and after an unproven accusation in the same session" already exists for a human accuser. The notes do not mention this.
4. **Two papers in the project's own results weaken the gap table and are missing from it.** Pezzo et al. (2022) (results-4, entry 2): the reason a physician gives for setting an aid aside changes rated competence ("I already knew what it would say" raised it, "I don't trust it" lowered it). That is the expert's stated reason against the tool, so E3 is partly covered for decision aids. Song et al. (2026, medRxiv preprint) (results-4): lay jurors judged the radiologist less liable when the AI was wrong, and judged most harshly a radiologist who abandoned a correct reading to follow a wrong AI. That is partial coverage of E1 (wrong AI against a human expert, observer judgement measured), though the outcome is liability after a bad outcome, not trust.

Notes I could not test: Hendriks et al. (2016), which the notes call "the closest lay-expert analogue", is summarised from a blog and a search snippet only (results-3, entry for Hendriks 2016). It should not carry any novelty claim until read.

### Q1. Verdict: (b) novel only if the claim is narrowed

The abstract's research question, read alone, asks how trust in an expert changes when an AI flags his content. That question has been asked and answered in the direction H1 predicts: AI disagreement lowers trust in the expert (Chen et al. 2026; Madanay et al. 2025, Table 2; Kim et al. 2026, p. 1520, for a correct AI flag), and baseless automated flags lower the evaluation of the person flagged (Du et al. 2026, 7% vs 87% detection rate, N = 214; Goulas et al. 2026, found in the web search, N = 1,339 teachers). The reply as a repair step has been studied in depth for human accusers (Kim et al. 2004; Ferrin et al. 2007), including a within-person drop after an unproven allegation (Kim 2004 pilot). What no study I read or found does is put these together: an AI tool held by the non-expert who holds power, raising objections the expert then rebuts with a reason or merely denies, with trust tracked flag by flag in one session. The study is novel as that combination and as a first experiment in the board and CISO setting. It is not novel in H1, and H3 as written ("lower than after the flag") restates a prediction the trust-repair literature has tested and partly contradicted (Kim 2004 Study 2: denial by an innocent party repairs well; Schiff et al. 2025: unsupported denial can help). H2's "above the start" and H4b (the checker amplifies the effect of the answer) are the parts with open answers.

### Q3. The closest single study

Kim, Ferrin, Cooper and Dirks (2004) is the closest design, closer in structure than the AI papers. A participant in a role with power over the target (a hiring manager) watches a videotaped expert (a senior tax accountant candidate) receive an unproven accusation from a third party (the recruiter relays hearsay), the accused replies at once (apology or denial), and trust is measured on competence, integrity and willingness to risk plus a decision (hiring); the pilot even rates trust before and after the allegation within person, and Study 2 adds evidence that the accused was innocent. What separates it from the abstract: the accuser is a human relaying hearsay, not an AI tool in the trustor's own hand; the accusation concerns a past act, not live content the trustor is watching; there is one accusation, not a sequence with unflagged slides; the denial carries an account, so the reasoned-answer versus bare-denial contrast is not tested; the main studies rate once, between subjects; and participants are students in a hiring scenario, not a board. Among AI papers, Kim, Daneshi, Draper and Sreedharan (2026) is closest (non-expert recipient, expert, AI that flags the expert, trust in the expert as the outcome, METI), but its AI is always right, the advisor accepts the correction, the recipient holds no power or tool, and trust is rated once. The abstract still calls Chen et al. (2026) "the closest design"; that is no longer defensible without naming both of these.

### Q4. Strongest case against novelty (hostile reviewer, under 150 words)

This is Kim et al. (2004) with the recruiter replaced by a chatbot. That an unproven accusation lowers trust, and that the accused's reply shapes repair, is twenty years old; that AI disagreement lowers trust in an expert was shown by Chen et al. (2026), Madanay et al. (2025, N = 1,606) and Kim et al. (2026). The "wrong" flags are wrong only in the debrief; participants experience an AI objecting and an expert answering, which Chen et al. already listed as their next study. The "power" manipulation is a sentence in a vignette read by 40 unpaid volunteers, not board members, assigned round robin rather than at random, with no condition in which the flag is right, so the design cannot separate a wrong flag from any flag. The board setting is a coat of paint. The combination is new only because nobody has bothered to run it.

### Q5. Strongest case for novelty (under 150 words)

The literatures that hold the pieces have never met. AI-disagreement studies (Chen, Madanay, Kim 2026) silence the expert and rate trust once; Kim et al. (2026) state that their AI was always right and call for studies where it fails. Trust-repair studies (Kim 2004, Ferrin 2007) have human accusers and no tool. Checker studies (Gupta, Lu, Horne and Nevo) never rate the person speaking. Yet the configuration they jointly miss is now real: camera assistants and Copilot let any listener check a presenter live, and boards are the textbook case of non-experts who must oversee experts and who stay silent for fear of exposure (Opdenbusch 2025; Lowry 2026). Whether a listener-held, confidently wrong AI gives the expert a chance to rise (H2) or lowers the bar for doubt (H4) is untested, and only a sequential design with the expert's own answer can show it.

### Q6. What the abstract should claim

"Prior work shows that an AI's disagreement lowers trust in a human expert when the expert stays silent (Chen et al., 2026; Madanay et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2026), and that an accused person's reply shapes how trust recovers after a human accusation (Kim et al., 2004; Ferrin et al., 2007). We bring the two together: we give the AI checker to the non-expert who holds power over the expert, make its objections wrong for the context, and track trust in the expert after each objection and after the expert's reasoned answer or bare denial." The abstract should also drop "closest design" for Chen alone, and should not present "the flags are wrong" as observable to participants.
