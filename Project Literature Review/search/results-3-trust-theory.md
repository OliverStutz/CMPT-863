## A. Definition and dimensions of trust

### Mayer, R. C., Davis, J. H., & Schoorman, F. D. (1995). An integrative model of organizational trust. *Academy of Management Review*, *20*(3), 709–734. https://doi.org/10.2307/258792
- **Type:** management or economics
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18 (the AOM alias https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.1995.9508080335 resolves to this DOI); abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.5465/amr.1995.9508080335
- **Design:** Conceptual model; no data. Defines trust as the willingness of a party to be vulnerable to the actions of another, based on the expectation that the other will perform an action important to the trustor, irrespective of the ability to monitor or control that other party. Separates trust from trustworthiness (ability, benevolence, integrity) and from the trustor's propensity, and adds perceived risk and risk taking.
- **Finding:** The model holds that a trustor's trust follows from three perceived qualities of the trustee: ability (skills in a specific domain), benevolence (wanting to do good to the trustor) and integrity (adhering to principles the trustor finds acceptable), moderated by the trustor's propensity to trust. Outcomes of risk taking feed back into later judgements of the three qualities.
- **Relation:** Definition; theme anchor for the trust measure and for the optional competence, benevolence and integrity ratings. The phrase "irrespective of the ability to monitor or control" makes the checker interesting: the checker is exactly such a monitoring device, so our study asks what happens to trust when the board member gains a way to check. Our study measures a trustor's trust in an expert in real time across flags and answers, where the model describes a slow, feedback-driven process.
- **Access:** SFU library (JSTOR / AOM)
- **Score:** 1=4 2=0 3=2 4=5 5=3 total=14/25

### McAllister, D. J. (1995). Affect- and cognition-based trust as foundations for interpersonal cooperation in organizations. *Academy of Management Journal*, *38*(1), 24–59. https://doi.org/10.2307/256727
- **Type:** management or economics
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.2307/256727
- **Design:** Field survey of 194 managers and professionals, each rating trust in a named peer. Measured cognition-based trust (reliance on the peer's competence and dependability) and affect-based trust (emotional bonds and care), their antecedents, and their links to monitoring and helping behaviour.
- **Finding:** The abstract reports that cognition-based and affect-based trust are distinct forms with different antecedents, and that both shape behaviour and performance in working relationships. (Detailed coefficients are not in the abstract.)
- **Relation:** Definition and measure; a CISO briefing a board for one session is a case where cognition-based trust dominates, so our 0 to 10 rating and the competence item are measuring mostly cognition-based trust. Our study uses a short, one-off encounter, not an ongoing peer relationship.
- **Access:** SFU library (JSTOR / AOM)
- **Score:** 1=5 2=0 3=1 4=4 5=3 total=13/25

### Rousseau, D. M., Sitkin, S. B., Burt, R. S., & Camerer, C. (1998). Not so different after all: A cross-discipline view of trust. *Academy of Management Review*, *23*(3), 393–404. https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.1998.926617
- **Type:** management or economics
- **Verified:** OpenAlex metadata 2026-10-18 (Crossref returned HTTP 200 for the DOI; volume, issue and pages match); abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.5465/amr.1998.926617
- **Design:** Cross-disciplinary review, no data. Introduces the widely used definition of trust as "a psychological state comprising the intention to accept vulnerability based upon positive expectations of the intentions or behavior of another", names risk and interdependence as its two conditions, and separates deterrence-based, calculus-based, relational and institution-based trust.
- **Finding:** Scholars across psychology, sociology and economics largely agree on this core definition; trust and distrust can coexist and vary over time within the same relationship.
- **Relation:** Definition (the most quoted one, also used by Sharma et al. 2023 to define trust repair). The board member accepts vulnerability to the CISO's expertise; the checker offers a partial substitute for that vulnerability. Our study measures how the state moves within a ten-minute exchange.
- **Access:** SFU library
- **Score:** 1=3 2=0 3=1 4=4 5=3 total=11/25

### Colquitt, J. A., Scott, B. A., & LePine, J. A. (2007). Trust, trustworthiness, and trust propensity: A meta-analytic test of their unique relationships with risk taking and job performance. *Journal of Applied Psychology*, *92*(4), 909–927. https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.92.4.909
- **Type:** psychology
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1037/0021-9010.92.4.909
- **Design:** Meta-analysis of 132 independent samples relating trustworthiness (ability, benevolence, integrity), trust propensity and trust to risk taking and job performance, with trust referents being leaders or coworkers.
- **Finding:** Trust only partly carried the effects of trustworthiness and propensity: ability, benevolence and integrity each still explained extra variance in risk taking and performance after trust was accounted for. The results held across trust measured as positive expectations, as willingness to be vulnerable, and as a direct rating.
- **Relation:** Definition and measure; supports measuring a single overall trust rating plus the three trustworthiness dimensions, since they are not interchangeable, and supports a direct trust item such as our 0 to 10 scale. Our study adds a time course within one encounter and an external source of doubt.
- **Access:** SFU library
- **Score:** 1=5 2=0 3=1 4=4 5=3 total=13/25

## B. Measuring laypeople's trust in experts

### Hendriks, F., Kienhues, D., & Bromme, R. (2015). Measuring laypeople's trust in experts in a digital age: The Muenster Epistemic Trustworthiness Inventory (METI). *PLOS ONE*, *10*(10), e0139309. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0139309
- **Type:** psychology
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0139309 (via OpenAlex)
- **Design:** Scale development. Exploratory factor analysis (n = 237) and confirmatory factor analysis (n = 345) of laypeople's ratings of experts met online, then an experiment (n = 137) that varied source characteristics. METI has 14 bipolar adjective pairs.
- **Finding:** Laypeople's judgements of an expert form three factors: expertise, integrity and benevolence. All three shifted when the experimenters changed what was said about the source, so the scale is sensitive to manipulated source information.
- **Relation:** Measure; the direct source for our optional competence, benevolence and integrity items in a non-expert-judges-expert setting. Its expertise factor maps onto "Architecture likely wrong" and "Numbers do not add up", which attack expertise. Our study measures change within one briefing and adds an AI checker as the source of doubt.
- **Access:** open access https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0139309
- **Score:** 1=5 2=0 3=5 4=5 5=4 total=19/25

### Hendriks, F., Kienhues, D., & Bromme, R. (2016). Disclose your flaws! Admission positively affects the perceived trustworthiness of an expert science blogger. *Studies in Communication Sciences*, *16*(2), 124–131. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scoms.2016.10.003
- **Type:** communication
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; publisher abstract not retrievable (ScienceDirect 403, Semantic Scholar abstract elided). Findings taken from two secondary descriptions: https://mirjamglessmer.com/2024/01/10/revisiting-the-hendriks-et-al-2016-trust-model and a search-engine summary of the abstract. Treat the details as provisional until the full text is read.
- **Design:** Laypeople read a blog entry by an expert science blogger, then a commentary that pointed out a flaw in it, written either by the blogger (self-disclosure) or by another person (external criticism). Ratings of expertise, integrity and benevolence (METI dimensions). Sample size not found.
- **Finding:** Rated expertise fell in both conditions. When the blogger pointed out the flaw, rated integrity and benevolence were higher than when another person pointed out the same flaw in nearly the same words.
- **Relation:** H1 and H2; the closest lay-expert analogue to our design: an expert's work is flagged by someone else, and expertise is damaged. It suggests the flag (external criticism) costs expertise, and that how the expert responds can move integrity and benevolence separately. Our flags come from an AI checker and are wrong, and our expert answers in a live exchange.
- **Access:** SFU library
- **Score:** 1=5 2=1 3=4 4=5 5=2 total=17/25

## C. Trust repair after a competence or integrity question

### Kim, P. H., Ferrin, D. L., Cooper, C. D., & Dirks, K. T. (2004). Removing the shadow of suspicion: The effects of apology versus denial for repairing competence- versus integrity-based trust violations. *Journal of Applied Psychology*, *89*(1), 104–118. https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.89.1.104
- **Type:** psychology
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1037/0021-9010.89.1.104 (preprint open at https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.398221)
- **Design:** Two studies. Participants watched a job candidate who was accused of a past violation framed as either competence-based (a technical error) or integrity-based (an intentional misdeed), who then either apologized or denied culpability; in a second study later evidence showed guilt or innocence. Measured trusting beliefs and trusting intentions toward the candidate.
- **Finding:** Trust was repaired better when the accused apologized for a competence violation but denied an integrity violation. When later evidence showed guilt, apologizing had worked better; when evidence showed innocence, denying had worked better. Trusting beliefs carried the effect on trusting intentions.
- **Relation:** H2 and H3; the core trust-repair anchor. Our flags are competence-type and, by design, the CISO is right (the "innocent" case), so this paper predicts that a denial could repair trust rather than lower it, which is a counter-prediction to H3 worth stating. Our "denial with no reason" differs from their denial in giving no account, and our accusation comes from an AI checker the participant holds, not from a third-party report.
- **Access:** open access preprint https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.398221 ; journal via SFU library
- **Score:** 1=5 2=0 3=2 4=5 5=3 total=15/25

### Kim, P. H., Dirks, K. T., Cooper, C. D., & Ferrin, D. L. (2006). When more blame is better than less: The implications of internal vs. external attributions for the repair of trust after a competence- vs. integrity-based trust violation. *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*, *99*(1), 49–65. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2005.07.002
- **Type:** psychology
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract summary read at https://scholarship.miami.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/When-more-blame-is-better-than/991031578717202976 (publisher abstract elided elsewhere)
- **Design:** 189 college students played a manager deciding whether to hire an accountant who had misfiled a tax return at a previous employer, shown on video. The accountant apologized and attributed the violation either internally (own fault) or externally (outside causes); the violation was framed as competence or integrity.
- **Finding:** For a competence violation, trust was restored better when the accountant took the blame (internal attribution). For an integrity violation, trust was restored better when the accountant pointed to outside causes (external attribution), even though such accounts are less believed.
- **Relation:** H2; our reasoned answers ("the company's reason") are a kind of external, situational account for an apparent competence lapse. This paper predicts that external accounts help less for competence violations, a counterweight to H2. Our CISO is not admitting a lapse but explaining why the flag does not apply, which is closer to a justification than to an apology.
- **Access:** open access repository record https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/lkcsb_research/2578 ; journal via SFU library
- **Score:** 1=5 2=0 3=2 4=4 5=2 total=13/25

### Ferrin, D. L., Kim, P. H., Cooper, C. D., & Dirks, K. T. (2007). Silence speaks volumes: The effectiveness of reticence in comparison to apology and denial for responding to integrity- and competence-based trust violations. *Journal of Applied Psychology*, *92*(4), 893–908. https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.92.4.893
- **Type:** psychology
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1037/0021-9010.92.4.893
- **Design:** Two laboratory studies with participants from two countries; an accused party responded to a competence or integrity violation with apology, denial, or reticence (declining to confirm or deny). Measured trust.
- **Finding:** Reticence did worse than the better of apology and denial in each case: for integrity violations it, like apology, failed to address guilt, and for competence violations it, like denial, failed to signal that the person would improve.
- **Relation:** H3; reticence is the nearest tested analogue to a reply that gives no information. Our "denial with no reason" mixes denial with reticence (a claim but no account), and this paper predicts such answers do poorly after a competence question. It also bears on the participant's own "say nothing" option, which is a form of reticence from the board member's side.
- **Access:** SFU library
- **Score:** 1=5 2=0 3=2 4=5 5=3 total=15/25

### Tomlinson, E. C., & Mayer, R. C. (2009). The role of causal attribution dimensions in trust repair. *Academy of Management Review*, *34*(1), 85–104. https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.2009.35713291
- **Type:** management or economics
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18 (Crossref misspells the second author as "Mryer"); abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.5465/amr.2009.35713291
- **Design:** Conceptual paper, no data. Applies Weiner's attribution theory (locus of causality, controllability, stability) to the repair of perceived ability, benevolence and integrity.
- **Finding:** Whether trustworthiness needs repair, and how a trustee can repair it, depends on where the trustor places the cause of a negative outcome, whether it was controllable, and whether it is likely to recur; the trustor's emotions also shape the process.
- **Relation:** H2 and H3, mechanism; a reasoned answer works if it moves the cause of the flagged problem away from the CISO's ability (for example, a company-specific constraint), while a bare denial leaves the attribution unchanged. Our study tests this in a live exchange where the "negative outcome" is an AI flag, not an actual loss.
- **Access:** SFU library
- **Score:** 1=4 2=0 3=1 4=4 5=3 total=12/25

### Sharma, K., Schoorman, F. D., & Ballinger, G. A. (2023). How can it be made right again? A review of trust repair research. *Journal of Management*, *49*(1), 363–399. https://doi.org/10.1177/01492063221089897
- **Type:** management or economics
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1177/01492063221089897
- **Design:** Narrative review of four decades of trust-repair research, including the research designs used and how trust repair is measured.
- **Finding:** Defines trust repair as any increase in trust above the post-transgression level and complete repair as a return to the pre-transgression level. Groups repair tactics under three mechanisms (attributional, social equilibrium, structural) and gives cautions on measuring repair.
- **Relation:** Definition and measure for H2: our "restores trust, possibly above the start" needs exactly this distinction between partial repair, complete repair and over-repair, which requires a pre-violation baseline (our start rating) and a post-violation rating (our after-flag rating). It is the most recent review and replaces the older Kramer and Lewicki (2010) and Lewicki and Brinsfield (2017) reviews for our purposes.
- **Access:** SFU library
- **Score:** 1=4 2=0 3=1 4=5 5=3 total=13/25

## D. Explanations and accounts

### Shaw, J. C., Wild, E., & Colquitt, J. A. (2003). To justify or excuse?: A meta-analytic review of the effects of explanations. *Journal of Applied Psychology*, *88*(3), 444–458. https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.88.3.444
- **Type:** psychology
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1037/0021-9010.88.3.444
- **Design:** Meta-analysis of 54 independent samples on whether an explanation was given and how adequate it was, measuring justice judgements and cooperation, retaliation and withdrawal.
- **Finding:** Giving an explanation had strong effects on fairness judgements and on reactions. Explanations helped more when they were excuses (pointing to outside causes) than justifications (arguing the act was right), when they followed unfavourable outcomes, and when the context had relational and moral weight.
- **Relation:** H2 and H3; explanation versus no explanation is our reasoned answer versus denial contrast. Our reasoned answers are justifications ("this is right for our company"), which this meta-analysis finds less effective than excuses, a point worth stating. Our outcome is trust in an expert, not fairness of a decision.
- **Access:** SFU library
- **Score:** 1=3 2=0 3=2 4=5 5=3 total=13/25

### Bies, R. J., & Shapiro, D. L. (1987). Interactional fairness judgments: The influence of causal accounts. *Social Justice Research*, *1*(2), 199–218. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01048016
- **Type:** psychology
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract content read via search-engine extract of the publisher abstract (https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/BF01048016 redirects to a login)
- **Design:** Three studies (two laboratory, one field) comparing a decision maker who gave a causal account for an improper or unfavourable action with one who gave none. Measured interactional fairness, procedural fairness and support for the manager.
- **Finding:** Participants rated a manager as fairer and supported the manager more when the manager gave a causal account claiming mitigating circumstances. The effect depended on how adequate the account seemed. In the field study, an adequate account for an unfavourable outcome raised both interactional and procedural fairness ratings.
- **Relation:** H2 and H3; an account must be judged adequate to help, which supports giving the CISO a concrete company reason (two answers) against a bare denial (one answer). Our outcome is trust in an expert after an AI flag rather than fairness after a managerial decision.
- **Access:** SFU library
- **Score:** 1=3 2=0 3=2 4=4 5=2 total=11/25

## E. Continued influence, innuendo and belief echoes

### Thorson, E. (2016). Belief echoes: The persistent effects of corrected misinformation. *Political Communication*, *33*(3), 460–480. https://doi.org/10.1080/10584609.2015.1102187
- **Type:** communication
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1080/10584609.2015.1102187
- **Design:** Three experiments exposing participants to negative information about a political candidate that was then effectively discredited, including an immediate correction. Measured attitudes toward the candidate.
- **Finding:** Negative information kept lowering attitudes toward the candidate after it had been discredited and after participants accepted the correction ("belief echoes"). The effect appeared even when the correction came immediately, and arose through both automatic and deliberative processes.
- **Relation:** H2 counter-result; predicts that even a convincing CISO answer will not bring trust fully back to the start, because the flag leaves an echo. This is the main reason to expect H2's "above the start" to fail. Our correction comes from the accused expert, not from a neutral fact-checker, and our misinformation comes from an AI tool the participant chose to use.
- **Access:** open access https://repository.upenn.edu/bitstreams/fde2b15d-38dd-4d96-9205-6ca7bfb356e2/download
- **Score:** 1=4 2=0 3=2 4=5 5=3 total=14/25

### Wegner, D. M., Wenzlaff, R., Kerker, R. M., & Beattie, A. E. (1981). Incrimination through innuendo: Can media questions become public answers? *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, *40*(5), 822–832. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.40.5.822
- **Type:** psychology
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract content read via search-engine extract of the abstract (ResearchGate record https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232589859); PsycNET page did not render
- **Design:** Three experiments, 182 undergraduates, rating a target person after reading newspaper headlines that were neutral, an incriminating assertion, an incriminating question, or an incriminating denial; the second study varied the credibility of the source.
- **Finding:** A headline that only asked an incriminating question shifted impressions of the target in a negative direction. A headline that denied the incriminating claim did the same, though much more weakly. The credibility of the source changed how persuasive direct assertions were, but had much less effect on innuendo.
- **Relation:** H1 and H3; our flags ("Architecture likely wrong") are hedged, like innuendo, and the result that source credibility matters little for innuendo predicts that even a doubted AI checker will lower trust. The denial result predicts that the CISO's bare denial can itself repeat the charge. Our target is an expert present in the exchange, and the innuendo comes from an AI tool.
- **Access:** SFU library
- **Score:** 1=4 2=0 3=1 4=5 5=2 total=12/25

### Letourneau, D., & Gawronski, B. (2024). Incrimination through innuendo: A replication and extension. *Social Psychology*, *55*(1), 51–61. https://doi.org/10.1027/1864-9335/a000540
- **Type:** psychology
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1027/1864-9335/a000540
- **Design:** Two preregistered studies (N = 506), close replication of Wegner et al. (1981) Study 1, adding whether the target was a political ingroup or outgroup member. Measured favourable impressions of the target.
- **Finding:** Questions that insinuated something negative about a target lowered favourable impressions of that target, replicating the 1981 result. The effect was the same size for ingroup and outgroup targets. Merely asking a question about a false proposition can shift opinion without any false assertion.
- **Relation:** H1; a modern, preregistered anchor that a hedged, question-like flag is enough to lower trust in a person, even with no evidence. Our flags are hedged machine verdicts about an expert's slides, shown to the participant in a private panel.
- **Access:** open access author PDF https://www.bertramgawronski.com/documents/LG2024SP.pdf
- **Score:** 1=4 2=0 3=1 4=5 5=3 total=13/25

### Ecker, U. K. H., Lewandowsky, S., Cook, J., Schmid, P., Fazio, L. K., Brashier, N., Kendeou, P., Vraga, E. K., & Amazeen, M. A. (2022). The psychological drivers of misinformation belief and its resistance to correction. *Nature Reviews Psychology*, *1*(1), 13–29. https://doi.org/10.1038/s44159-021-00006-y
- **Type:** psychology
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract summary read at https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/publications/889ddb0f-0d44-44f4-a54f-57c260ae4917
- **Design:** Review of cognitive, social and affective drivers of misinformation belief and of prebunking and debunking interventions.
- **Finding:** Misinformation keeps influencing reasoning and judgement after it has been clearly corrected (the continued influence effect); the review sets out the cognitive, social and emotional reasons people accept false information and resist revising it, and evaluates which corrections work.
- **Relation:** H2 counter-result and theme anchor for continued influence; together with Thorson (2016) it predicts residual trust loss after a good answer. It replaces Lewandowsky et al. (2012) as the up-to-date citation. Our "misinformation" is an AI flag about a person's work, not a factual claim about the world.
- **Access:** open access accepted version https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/publications/889ddb0f-0d44-44f4-a54f-57c260ae4917

### Hovland, C. I., & Weiss, W. (1951). The influence of source credibility on communication effectiveness. *Public Opinion Quarterly*, *15*(4), 635–650. https://doi.org/10.1086/266350
- **Type:** communication
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18 (Crossref gives first page 635 only; 635–650 per the journal record); abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1086/266350
- **Design:** Participants read identical messages attributed to high- or low-credibility sources; opinion change measured right away and again some weeks later.
- **Finding:** At exposure, participants discounted material from untrustworthy sources. Over time they separated the content from its source, their scepticism faded and the untrustworthy material came to be accepted (later called the sleeper effect).
- **Relation:** Source credibility anchor and H2 counter-result; even if the board member learns the checker was wrong, the flag's content may outlive the memory that it came from a discredited source. Our study measures trust minutes after the flag, before any sleeper effect, which is a limit to state.
- **Access:** SFU library

### Tenney, E. R., MacCoun, R. J., Spellman, B. A., & Hastie, R. (2007). Calibration trumps confidence as a basis for witness credibility. *Psychological Science*, *18*(1), 46–50. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01847.x
- **Type:** psychology
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://escholarship.org/content/qt92c2910g/qt92c2910g.pdf (via OpenAlex)
- **Design:** Two experiments in which mock jurors judged witnesses whose testimony was confident or unconfident and was later shown to contain an error or not. Measured overall witness credibility.
- **Finding:** An error damaged the credibility of a witness who had been confident about the wrong statement more than that of a witness who had not been confident. After an error, a less confident witness could seem more credible than a confident one. The authors read this as observers judging how well a source's confidence matches its accuracy.
- **Relation:** H3 and H4; a CISO who flatly denies a flag stakes high confidence, so if the participant believes the checker, the denial should cost more than a hedged answer. It also suggests the AI checker's own confident wording ("Numbers do not add up") could cost the checker credibility if the participant sees it fail. Our study does not show the participant which side is right.
- **Access:** open access https://escholarship.org/content/qt92c2910g/qt92c2910g.pdf

## F. Power and trust

### Schilke, O., Reimann, M., & Cook, K. S. (2015). Power decreases trust in social exchange. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, *112*(42), 12950–12955. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1517057112
- **Type:** psychology
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/4620861
- **Design:** A series of experiments that manipulated participants' power position as structural dependence in an exchange relationship, across several paradigms, and measured trust perceptions and trusting behaviour toward the exchange partner.
- **Finding:** Across paradigms, participants with more power placed less trust in their partner than participants with less power. Mediation analyses showed that low power raised participants' hope, which raised their perception of the partner's benevolence, which raised trust; this supports a motivated-cognition account over a rational-actor one.
- **Relation:** Context for the power asymmetry; the board member holds formal power and the budget, which predicts lower baseline trust and less motivated trust, while the board member's dependence on the CISO's expertise pushes the other way. Our study does not manipulate power, so this paper frames the setting and a possible moderator, and our role framing (board member) should be described as a power cue.
- **Access:** open access https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/4620861
- **Score:** 1=5 2=0 3=4 4=4 5=3 total=16/25

## Notes on known papers

- **Kim et al. (2004) and Ferrin et al. (2007)** were listed as known but not yet read; they are now written up above. The material point for H3: in Kim et al. (2004), denial beat apology for a competence violation when later evidence showed innocence. Our CISO is right by design, so a pure denial may repair trust if the participant later sees reasons to doubt the checker. H3 should be phrased as "a denial without a reason restores less trust than a reasoned answer", not "lowers trust further", unless the pilot shows otherwise. Ferrin et al. (2007) is the better support for H3, because a denial without reasons shares the weakness of reticence.
- **Mayer & Davis (1999)** (known): the appraisal-system finding pairs naturally with Mayer et al. (1995) and Colquitt et al. (2007) as the ability, benevolence, integrity measurement lineage; no new reading.
