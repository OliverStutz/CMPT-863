## 1. Danry, Pataranutaporn, Mao & Maes (2020), Augmented Humans. Wearable Reasoner. (agent reading 2026-10-19; full text in `../references/` as `Danry-2020-wearable-reasoner-AugmentedHumans.pdf`)

**Reference (APA 7, checked against the PDF and Crossref).**
Danry, V., Pataranutaporn, P., Mao, Y., & Maes, P. (2020). Wearable Reasoner: Towards enhanced human rationality through a wearable device with an explainable AI assistant. In *Proceedings of the Augmented Humans International Conference* (pp. 1–12). Association for Computing Machinery. https://doi.org/10.1145/3384657.3384799

**Agent reading (Oliver has not read it yet).** This is a short HCI prototype paper. It describes a pair of audio glasses (Bose Frames) that listen to spoken arguments and tell the wearer through the earpiece whether a claim came with evidence. It also reports a small lab study of whether that feedback changed how wearers judged the arguments. It is the only research system in our set that checks spoken content for the listener in real time. But nobody in it is a speaker who could be trusted or distrusted.

**What the full text says.**
- *System.* The device converts speech to text, adds punctuation through a cloud service, and sends each sentence to a random-forest classifier trained on the IBM Debater claims-and-evidence data. The classifier labels the claim as "supported" or "unsupported" (pp. 4–5). In the explainable mode, the feedback repeats the claim, names the evidence type and says why, for example "... but there were given no evidence to support them ... the claims could be either true or false". In the non-explainable mode it gives a one-word answer (p. 5). Figure 1 (p. 1) lists "high stake meetings" among the intended uses, next to political speech and advertisements.
- *Accuracy.* The classifier was 90.5% accurate offline and 85.1% on the study's argument set. Running live in the lab it was right on **63.5%** of statements (p. 8). The errors came from speech-to-text (6.7%), missing punctuation (13.8%) and misclassification (16.0%). The authors expect still more errors in a noisy real setting, such as a family watching a TV debate (p. 8).
- *Design.* The study was within-subjects, 3 x 2: explainable mode, non-explainable mode and device off (control, run three times), crossed with arguments with or without evidence (p. 6). The 96 arguments were adapted from the IBM data, on 12 divisive topics (immigration, abortion, gun control, Holocaust denial and others). They were balanced for length, reading level and pro or con (pp. 6–7). Each participant heard 24 arguments, 8 per condition (p. 7). **The arguments were pre-recorded and played from a loudspeaker next to the laptop**; no person presented them (p. 7, Figure 5).
- *Sample.* 21 English speakers were recruited by email and 3 were dropped for technical problems, leaving **N = 18**: ages 18 to 54, 17 with higher education, 11 women (p. 7).
- *Measures.* After each argument the participant gave three ratings: agreement (1 to 7), reasonableness (1 to 7) and willingness to donate to an organisation backing the claim (1 to 5) (p. 7). Usefulness, ease of use and helpfulness were asked afterwards, followed by open comments. No item asked about the speaker or about trust in anyone.
- *Findings.* In the explainable mode, claims with evidence got higher ratings than claims without: agreement 4.5 against 3.8 and reasonableness 4.4 against 3.3. Donation was 2.6 against 2.3, which the authors report as no reliable difference (p. 8). They say this gap did not appear in the non-explainable mode or with the device off (p. 8, p. 10), but give no numbers for those conditions in the text (Figure 9 has them). Their conclusion is that people "are less likely to simply follow the answers from the AI unless it can explain its process" (p. 10). Participants found the device fairly easy to use (3.6 of 5) and helpful (3.7 of 5) (p. 8).
- *Quotes that matter for us.* "I believed what it said to me ... I gave the device the benefits of doubt" (p. 9). "If the device says something is supported then I answer all the way to the right [agree]. If the device says something is unsupported then I lean left" (p. 9). Several participants called it "a second opinion" and felt "semi-dependent" when it was off (p. 9).
- *Analysis by actual evidence, not by what the device said.* The results are grouped by whether the argument really had evidence. Because the device was right only 63.5% of the time live, roughly a third of the feedback participants heard was wrong. The paper does not analyse those trials.
- *Reporting problems.* The printed test figures do not always fit the text. The reasonableness gap is called reliable with a test value of 0.13. A rating "significantly higher than neutral" carries a figure the paper itself marks as not reliable (p. 8). The analysis is named as both a rank test and an analysis of variance. With 18 people, the numbers are best read as direction only.
- *Authors' limitations and future work.* The setting was structured and controlled; real arguments are more complex, mixed with non-arguments, emotional and dialectical, and rely on unstated assumptions (p. 10). The authors call for testing "what would happen when the AI makes the wrong classifications ... how would users react and cope ... when receiving bad advice from their AI counterpart?" (p. 10).

**Consequence for our study.**
- *Section 2 (checkers are available and often wrong): keep it, but rewrite the claim.* `_review-design-v2.md` says it "changed how users rated a speaker's arguments". That is not what happened. There was no speaker, only recorded statements from a loudspeaker, and the ratings were of the statements. The paper measured nothing about any person. Suggested wording: "Danry et al. (2020) built audio glasses that tell the wearer whether a heard claim is backed by evidence. In a lab study (N = 18), explained feedback widened the gap in rated agreement and reasonableness between supported and unsupported claims. Running live, the device was right on 63.5% of statements." The 63.5% belongs in this section. It is a second, independent data point for "often wrong", next to Squash's 20 of 34 irrelevant pop-ups, and it comes from a research system built for exactly this listener-side use.
- *Gap (section 5).* It strengthens "none rates trust in the speaker": the nearest real-time listener tool measured belief in statements only. The authors themselves ask what wrong feedback does, which is our question with a person added. That is a clean sentence for the gap.
- *H1 (flag lowers trust).* The support is indirect, and the paper also contains a warning. For support: the quotes show default belief in the device ("I gave the device the benefits of doubt"), which fits rule 19. The warning: the one-word, non-explainable verdict reportedly did **not** shift judgements; only the explained one did. Our flags ("Numbers do not add up", "Architecture likely wrong") are closer to the one-word mode than to their explained mode, although the marked slide region adds something. If Danry's pattern held, a bare flag would move trust less than H1 expects. This is weak evidence (N = 18, the reporting problems above, statements rather than a person) and it conflicts with Horne & Nevo (2025) and Lu et al. (2025), where short labels did move judgements. Still, it is a reason to fix how much reason each flag carries and keep it identical across flags. It may also be a reason to add a short "because" clause, decided before the pilot.
- *H2 to H4.* No bearing. Nobody answered the device, the listener held no power over anyone, and no action was measured.
- *Not the closest design.* The search entry called it "the closest design precedent to our listener-held checker". The form factor matches, but the design does not: no expert, no trust in a person, no reply. Kim et al. (2026) stays the closest (rule 22).

**Open.** Read Figure 9 for the non-explainable and control means if the review needs the contrast in numbers. Decide whether each flag carries a short reason (the explainable mode) or only a verdict, and keep it the same on slides 2 to 4. Danry, Pataranutaporn, Mao & Maes (2023, CHI) and Danry et al. (2025, CHI) are in `results-1-hci-checkers.md`. The 2025 paper (deceptive AI explanations change beliefs more than honest ones) may be the better citation for "explained wrong feedback is believed", if room allows; it has not been read.

## 2. Kempt & Nagel (2022), Journal of Medical Ethics. Responsibility, second opinions and peer-disagreement. (agent reading 2026-10-19; full text in `../references/` as `Kempt-Nagel-2022-second-opinions-peer-disagreement-JME.pdf`)

**Reference (APA 7, checked against the PDF and Crossref).**
Kempt, H., & Nagel, S. K. (2022). Responsibility, second opinions and peer-disagreement: Ethical and epistemological challenges of using AI in clinical diagnostic contexts. *Journal of Medical Ethics*, *48*(4), 222–229. https://doi.org/10.1136/medethics-2021-107440

(It was published online first on 14 December 2021; the issue year 2022 is the one to cite. The article is free to read on the BMJ site but carries "No commercial re-use"; it is not an open licence.)

Replies in the same issue, for the reference list if cited:
Jongsma, K. R., & Sand, M. (2022). Agree to disagree: The symmetry of burden of proof in human–AI collaboration. *Journal of Medical Ethics*, *48*(4), 230–231. https://doi.org/10.1136/medethics-2022-108242
Lang, B. H. (2022). Are physicians requesting a second opinion really engaging in a reason-giving dialectic? Normative questions on the standards for second opinions and AI. *Journal of Medical Ethics*, *48*(4), 234–235. https://doi.org/10.1136/medethics-2022-108246

**Agent reading (Oliver has not read it yet).** This is a conceptual ethics paper with no participants ("This study does not involve human participants", p. 228). It is about the physician's duties when a diagnostic AI disagrees with them. It argues that the AI cannot be a real peer, because it cannot exchange reasons. It proposes that a second human physician settle any disagreement between the AI and the physician. It says nothing about how patients or other observers judge the physician.

**What the full text says.**
- *Three kinds of second opinion* (p. 223): patient-initiated, physician-initiated, and institutionalised or case-initiated (for example the double reading of breast-cancer screening in Germany). Second opinions are treated as peer opinions of equal weight ("equal views principle", p. 224).
- *Burden of proof with a human peer* (p. 224): "Without a disagreement, the physician-in-charge has no reason to assume they could be mistaken ... A disagreement between initial and second opinion, however, establishes the burden of proof as falling on the physician-in-charge: as the bearer of responsibility for the final decision, their disagreement with a peer-opinion on the same diagnosis ought to be justified." And: "Independent of whether a second opinion is correct or not, the physician-in-charge may not dismiss it without justification." With a human peer, this burden is discharged by "an exchange of reasons" (p. 224).
- *What changes with an AI* (pp. 225–226): AI output is at best interpretable, not explained. Interpretation methods such as LIME require the physician's own assumptions, so "an interpretable AI ... is not providing reasons" (p. 225). The core passage (p. 226): "A physician having a disagreement with a machine can put forward all the reasons they want, the machine will not react to it on the basis of reason ... the physician ought not to ignore the second opinion as misguided or false; yet they also cannot overturn it in any meaningful way based on an exchange of reasons ... Thus, the burden of proof of the diagnostic process moves from an epistemic equilibrium (in the equal weights view-sense) between the initial and the second opinion, towards the proponent of the initial opinion." And again (p. 227): without explanatory reasons a disagreement "is not resolved but merely decided in favour of one side". It "does, however, move the burden of proof towards the physician-in-charge, as they have to incorporate AI-DSS as evidence, but cannot reject the evidence even if they think it is wrong."
- *The rule of disagreement* (p. 227, set in bold in the paper): "If a diagnosis provided by an autonomous AI diagnostic system contradicts the initial diagnosis of the physician-in-charge, it shall count as disagreement requiring a second opinion of another physician." If the AI agrees, it counts as confirming evidence and "no further steps need to be taken" (pp. 222, 227). The analogy: if a colleague gives a contrary opinion and dies before they can discuss it, the physician may not simply adopt it, because the colleague can no longer bear responsibility (p. 227). The rule applies only to systems at Topol's levels 3 and 4, which form their own diagnosis (p. 225), and only to practical disagreements about specific cases (footnote i, p. 224).
- *Counterarguments the authors take up* (p. 226). Braun et al. argue that automated diagnosis does not shift the burden at all, because the physician always balances uncertain inputs. The authors answer that this treats every second opinion, human ones included, as mere evidence. The other counterargument is the accuracy argument: a certified, accurate AI could reduce the physician's responsibility. They also concede costs (p. 228): over-reliance, and the loss of AI's efficiency, since any disagreement now needs a human.
- *Jongsma & Sand (pp. 230–231), read in full (accepted manuscript).* They dispute the asymmetry directly. They restate the first premise as "an increased burden of proof (better to be conceived as a burden for justification)" in disagreement, which "does allegedly not arise" in agreement. Against it: asking for a second opinion already needs justification; each agreement also needs reasons, or it may be coincidence or the result of different heuristics; and "even in cases of agreement, medical professionals must be wary that their judgement ... is fallible." They accept the second premise, that AI opacity blocks reason exchange. They conclude that the rule would then have to apply to agreement as well, which removes the efficiency gain, and they propose explainability work and forward-looking responsibility instead. Note that "increased burden of proof" is their paraphrase; Kempt & Nagel themselves write "shifts" or "moves" the burden "towards" the physician.
- *Lang (pp. 234–235), only the first paragraph and footnotes readable.* His target is a different premise. The title and footnotes question whether physicians who ask for a second opinion actually exchange reasons at all. Patient-initiated second opinions are designed to be independent. Physicians may go on impressionistic "clinical gestalt", or may not seek reasons when they could. That "calls into question the indispensability of the equal-view peer-disagreement model". From what could be read, Lang disputes the reason-exchange model, not the asymmetry as such. `_review-design-v2.md` says "the replies in the same issue dispute the asymmetry". That is true of Jongsma & Sand and not shown for Lang.

**Consequence for our study.**
- *Section 4, "the expert and the machine": the citation holds for the first half of the planned sentence and not for the second.* Abstract v8 writes: "Kempt and Nagel (2022) argue that an AI's contrary opinion shifts the burden of proof onto the expert ... After a flag, a reason meets that burden and a denial fails it". The first sentence is accurate (pp. 222, 226, 227). The second is our inference, and it runs against the paper's own argument. Kempt & Nagel say the expert's reasons **cannot** settle a disagreement with an AI, because the machine does not respond to reasons. That is exactly why they need a second human. A reason "meets the burden" in their account only when another reason-giver takes it up. To keep the citation honest, the review should either say: "Kempt and Nagel (2022) argue that when an AI contradicts the expert in charge, the burden of proof moves to the expert, who cannot settle the disagreement by reasons because the machine does not answer them; they propose that a second human settle it." Then it should add, as our own step: "In our setting the CISO's reason is addressed not to the machine but to the board member, a human who can weigh it; whether it discharges the burden in the listener's eyes is what H2 and H4b test." That second sentence is a fair extension, but it must be marked as ours.
- *Normative, not descriptive.* The paper is about what a physician ought to do and who is responsible, not about how a lay observer's trust moves. It is a reason to expect H4b, not evidence for it. The review should not write that it "predicts" an observer effect.
- *H3 and H4b (b2), denial: the paper fits best here.* "The physician-in-charge may not dismiss it without justification" (p. 224) is the normative core of why a bare denial should cost the CISO. A denial with no reason is dismissal without justification. This is the strongest use of the paper and the one to keep.
- *H4b (b1), reason after a flag moves trust further than the same reason without a flag.* Weak support. The asymmetry is disputed in print by Jongsma & Sand: on their view the CISO in the neutral group carries the same justificatory burden, so there is nothing extra for the reason to discharge after a flag. Together with Kim et al. (2026)'s null for AI involvement, this keeps H4b exploratory, as v8 already has it. The review can cite the dispute in one clause ("Jongsma and Sand (2022) reply that agreement carries the same burden"). That clause gives section 4 a stated disagreement, which the thematic-synthesis criterion rewards.
- *"No issues found" slides.* Kempt & Nagel treat an agreeing AI as confirming evidence with no further steps (p. 227). That is the logic behind rule 16 (a small rise after "No issues found"). Jongsma & Sand predict instead that agreement needs justification too. Rule 16's prediction can cite the pair as the two readings.
- *Wording in v8.* "Shifts the burden of proof onto the expert" is acceptable. "Increased burden", used in the search entry and in `_review-design-v2.md`, is Jongsma & Sand's paraphrase; use "moves the burden of proof towards" if quoting Kempt & Nagel. The paper also has no observers, no patients rating anyone and no data, so it should not be listed among "studies".

**Open.** Check how Chen et al. (2026) "take this up"; the claim in v8 and `_review-design-v2.md` was not verified here. Lang's full text needs SFU access if the review cites him for more than his title; otherwise cite only Jongsma & Sand for the dispute. Kempt & Nagel may have answered the replies in the same issue; the BMJ issue table of contents was not reachable, so this is not checked.

## 3. Da Silva & Jensen (2022), PACM HCI (CSCW). "Cyber security is a dark art": The CISO as soothsayer. (agent reading 2026-10-19; full text in `../references/` as `DaSilva-Jensen-2022-CISO-soothsayer-CSCW-arXiv.pdf`, arXiv version)

**Reference (APA 7; author order, title, volume, issue and pages checked against Crossref; article number from the Royal Holloway repository record, since the ACM page was not reachable).**
Da Silva, J., & Jensen, R. B. (2022). "Cyber security is a dark art": The CISO as soothsayer. *Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction*, *6*(CSCW2), Article 365. https://doi.org/10.1145/3555090

(The arXiv PDF prints Jensen's name first in its header. Crossref and the published version have Da Silva first. The published version runs 31 pages; APA 7 uses the article number, not "1–31".)

**Agent reading (Oliver has not read it yet).** This is an interpretive interview study of what CISOs are for in large, mostly UK-based listed companies. It reads one role of the CISO through the analogy of a soothsayer: an interpreter of signs the organisation cannot read itself, both elevated and at risk of being scapegoated. The analogy is the authors' own; the title phrase "dark art" comes from one CISO. The paper describes the relationship between the CISO and senior leaders; it measures nothing.

**What the full text says.**
- *Sample and method* (arXiv pp. 3–5). There were 21 semi-structured interviews in 18 companies, October 2019 to July 2020. The interviewees were 15 CISOs and 6 senior leaders: 2 CEOs, 2 CFOs, 1 CIO and **only one non-executive director (NED1)**. The companies were listed on a major stock market; half of them (9) were in industrial goods and services or utilities. Interviews lasted 25 to 56 minutes, and eight were held remotely because of COVID-19. All interviews were conducted by one researcher who is a practising CISO. The sample was recruited through professional networks by snowballing, and leaders were reached through participating CISOs. Analysis was thematic (Braun and Clarke), coded in NVivo, then read through identity work and ontological security.
- *Authors' limitations* (arXiv p. 5): the sample leans to one industry; it does not represent all CISOs or companies; participants selected themselves; every CISO self-identified as male and all participants are "elites"; the researcher is a CISO, which may help the analysis but risks bias; there are few non-CISO participants; levels below senior leadership are left out; and some interviews were remote.
- *"Dark art".* This is CISO4's phrase (arXiv p. 7): "cyber-security is a dark art ... most people won't understand it". It comes up while CISO4 describes "a particularly sophisticated set of attack activity". The authors gather it with CISO5's "another language" and "almost ... hieroglyphics", and with "lay-person" (CISO2) (arXiv p. 8). They argue that if mystique makes cyber security an interpretive practice that needs an interpreter, "this may motivate CISOs ... to position the subject in this way, e.g. to explain it as a dark art" (arXiv p. 8). In the discussion (arXiv p. 12), positioning the field as "art not science" and "a dark art" may be the CISO's way to protect a precarious post: "it is easier to replace a chemist than a soothsayer." They also write that whether cyber security is a dark art "is perhaps obsolete" (arXiv p. 14).
- *"Soothsayer".* This is the authors' analogy, "a term we neither consider nor intend to be denigratory" (arXiv p. 1). Soothsayers read signs others cannot, judge partly on "gut feeling", advise on the path to take, may have a stake in their own prophecies, and lose their post if the people they serve are displeased (arXiv p. 10). The CISO's matching traits in the data: CISO2 says cyber security is "not at all" a science and relies on "judgement and gut feeling" (arXiv p. 6); 13 of 15 CISOs called themselves "pragmatic" (arXiv pp. 6, 12); and the role is precarious, as in CISO12's "if something goes wrong ... you're the guy [that gets fired]" (arXiv p. 10). The authors recommend treating the CISO as a "weather forecaster" rather than a "security totem" (arXiv pp. 14–15).
- *"Cyber sophistry"* (arXiv p. 13): "The CISO plays a role in not just interpreting, but also relaying information. They hold a position of power, and that power provides opportunities for sophistry." Several participants were aware of this, and "there is a suggestion from the data that their interpretations may not be entirely objective". The proposed mechanism: forecasting ever more "sophisticated" threats and long-term plans may protect the CISO's job. The direct evidence is thin and mostly self-report. CISO7 speaks of "the subjectiveness of it" and of "wordsmithing things" when asking the board whether it wants "good practice or best practice" (arXiv p. 6). CISO6 says: "I like to make sure that we are very honest about the risks because it's very easy to mislead because ... they [the board] don't know enough ... so they have to have some faith and trust in me" (arXiv p. 7). The authors' remedy (arXiv p. 14): "additional cyber-security expertise ... at senior levels within an organisation, possibly in a non-executive capacity, in order to identify and challenge this".
- *What the six leaders said about the board and the CISO.* NED1: "we're [board members] not going to have the deep expertise", "we're going to trust that ... but we do ultimately have to understand the right questions to ask to make sure someone like you [a CISO] is doing your job appropriately" (arXiv p. 7). NED1 also said the board has the power to "hire [and] fire people" and must decide whether "whoever is accountable for it has the right level of expertise", and that cyber news had "got me a little scared" (arXiv pp. 9–10). CEO1: "you have to make judgments on the fly about whether you think somebody is bullshitting or not" (arXiv p. 10); "if something serious happened I would be told quickly", and the aim is to avoid "shroud-waving ... because people then don't take it seriously" (arXiv p. 9). CFO1: "never going to be a cyber-security expert", "always going to be looking to genuine experts" (arXiv p. 7). CFO2: information must be "expressed in a way that is understandable to a more lay person" (arXiv p. 5); acronyms have to be "unpick[ed]"; specialists have "a smell and a sense for their particular area" (arXiv pp. 6–7). CEO2: keep it "as simple as possible" (arXiv p. 6).
- *What CISOs said about boards.* "the board didn't know which question to ask" before CISO arrived, "management by anecdote" (arXiv p. 7). CISO3: the board "still struggle a little" (arXiv p. 7). CISO7: the board "quite like" the NIST framing (arXiv p. 7). CISO8: the board says "we seem to be spending a lot of money on security" (arXiv p. 9). CISO12 goes to the board only "if it's something going wrong" (arXiv p. 9). CISO11 described war games in which "you watch them [the executive committee] shit themselves" (arXiv p. 9). Footnote 10 (arXiv p. 14): some CISOs were expected to decide what risk is acceptable on the leaders' behalf.

**Consequence for our study.**
- *Section 1 (boards oversee what they cannot judge): supports the planned optional clause, and gives one better quote than the "dark art" phrase.* The paper supports "the CISO holds informational power" (gatekeeper of information, arXiv p. 5; "very easy to mislead", arXiv p. 7). It also supports "the board judges the presenter's credibility, not the substance": CEO1's "judgments on the fly about whether you think somebody is bullshitting or not" says this in a leader's own words, and is the strongest single line for section 1 after Lowry et al. (2026). If words allow only one clause, suggested wording: "Da Silva and Jensen (2022) describe the CISO as interpreter of a field leaders call opaque, one CEO judging 'on the fly' whether the CISO is bluffing." Quote page numbers only after checking against the ACM version.
- *What it does not support.* (a) "Dark art" is one CISO's phrase, and the authors read it as possibly self-serving positioning; it is not a finding about boards. Do not write that boards see cyber as a dark art on this source. That wording is in Opdenbusch et al. (2025), where EX6 says boards see cyber as "a dark art, a bunch of chaps in hoodies"; cite Opdenbusch for the board's view. (b) Only one board member was interviewed (NED1), so "what boards think" rests on one NED plus five executives. (c) There is no trust measure, no tool and no experiment. (d) The search entry says "an AI checker breaks the CISO's monopoly on interpretation". That is our framing; the paper never discusses tools or checks. The nearest it comes is the authors' call for independent expertise at senior level "to identify and challenge" sophistry (arXiv p. 14). That call can be cited as the motivation for an independent check, alongside Aamir et al. (2026).
- *H1 (flag).* The paper gives a plausible reason why a wrong flag might bite. If board members already suspect CISOs of shading reports (CEO1, CISO6, "cyber sophistry"), a flag looks like confirmation. This fits rule 9 (ask at the end whether the participant believes CISOs shade their reports, and use it as a moderator of H1). Da Silva & Jensen are a second source for that item, next to Lowry et al. (2026).
- *H2 and H3 (answer and denial): a design warning.* The paper shows the CISO's typical defence is appeal to expertise and judgement ("gut feeling", "not a science", "most people won't understand it"). A reasoned answer that leans on "this is complex, trust my judgement" would be, in the paper's terms, soothsaying, and is close to our bare denial. Rule 3 already requires the reasoned answer to give more inputs than the flag. Add: the reason must be checkable content the participant can follow (the company's figure, the system the checker could not see), never an appeal to the CISO's expertise. Otherwise H2 and H3 differ in length, not in whether a reason was given.
- *H4a (participation).* Weak. NED1's "the right questions to ask" echoes Opdenbusch et al. (2025), but nothing here shows silence or its cause. Opdenbusch remains the source for H4a.
- *Scenario detail.* The cost complaint (CISO8's board, "spending a lot of money") supports the budget decision as the board's real lever. It also supports the standard board question proposed in the Opdenbusch entry of `verified-view.md` ("Are we investing enough?").

**Open.** Check the article number (365) and the quoted passages against the ACM version before citing page numbers; the arXiv version may differ. Da Silva's PhD thesis (Royal Holloway, 2023, `pure.royalholloway.ac.uk/files/50570739/2023dasilvajphd.pdf`) and a companion paper ("Teacher, enforcer, soothsayer, scapegoat: the purpose of the CISO") turned up in a search and may have more board material; neither has been read.
