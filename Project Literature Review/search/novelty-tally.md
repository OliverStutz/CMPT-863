## Closest study (Q3)

- B and C: Kim, Daneshi, Draper & Sreedharan (2026, AIES). AI flags the advisor in front of the dependent lay person, trust in the advisor measured with METI. Differences: their flags were correct, the AI sat in the workflow and not in the user's hand, the advisor never answered, trust rated once between subjects, low stakes.
- A: Kim, Ferrin, Cooper & Dirks (2004) is structurally closest (power holder watches an accused expert reply to unverified hearsay, trust on competence, integrity and willingness to risk, plus a decision), with Kim et al. (2026) closest among AI papers.
- All three: the abstract's "Chen et al. (2026) is the closest design" is no longer defensible without naming Kim et al. (2026), and the abstract cites none of Kim 2026, Madanay 2025, Kim 2004 or Ferrin 2007.

## The hostile case (Q4), points raised by two or more reviewers

1. "Kim et al. (2004) with the recruiter replaced by a chatbot": the repair step is twenty years old; the AI-disagreement penalty is shown by Chen, Madanay (N = 1,606) and Kim 2026 (A, B, C).
2. Participants never learn the flags are wrong, so "wrong flag" is, from their side, "an unverified allegation from an AI" (A, B). There is no condition with a correct flag, so the design cannot separate a wrong flag from any flag (A).
3. The board setting is a coat of paint: lay volunteers role-playing directors (A, B, C).
4. Forty unpaid participants, assigned in turn rather than at random, with groups that differ in who asks (A, B, C).
5. Chen et al. (2026) already list "the expert answers" as their next study (A).

## The case for (Q5), common ground

The literatures that hold the pieces have never met: AI-versus-expert studies silence the expert and rate trust once; trust-repair studies use human accusers and no tool; wrong-flag studies never rate the person. Kim et al. (2026) call for varying AI reliability; Chen et al. and Madanay et al. call for testing the expert's communication. Consumer camera assistants and meeting copilots make the listener-held checker current, and boards are the textbook non-expert overseers (Opdenbusch 2025; Lowry 2026). The stepwise design with the expert's own answer and a neutral group that hears identical answers is what isolates flag, checker presence and reply.

## What to change in the abstract (Q6), where the three converge

1. Replace "Chen et al. (2026) is the closest design" with Kim et al. (2026), or cite both, and add Madanay et al. (2025).
2. Cite the trust-repair tradition (Kim et al. 2004; Ferrin et al. 2007) as the precedent H2 and H3 extend to an AI accuser.
3. State the contribution as the combination: a listener-held checker in the hands of the party with power, flags plausible but wrong for the context, the expert's reasoned answer versus bare denial as the repair step, trust tracked after each flag and answer against a neutral group. Each reviewer offers a sentence; B's and C's are the tightest (see their Q6).
4. Present H1 as a replication in a new setting, not as the finding; H2 and H3 carry the novelty (C). H2's "above the start" as exploratory (B, and the earlier selection reviewers).
5. Do not present "the flags are wrong" as something participants experience; it is true by construction and known only at debrief (A, B).

## Not part of the vote but raised for the design

- No correct-flag condition (A). A single correct flag, or the "No issues found" slides, is the only contrast the design has; say what it can and cannot separate.
- Allocation in turn, not at random (A, B, C). Randomise, or state the reason.
- Role-play by lay volunteers (A, B, C). Recruit people with board, audit or oversight experience where possible, and report the sample's background.
