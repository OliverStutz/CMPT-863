# Novelty review C: Abstract v7

Reviewer C, 2026-10-19. Worked alone; did not open novelty-review-A.md or novelty-review-B.md.

## What the abstract claims to add (step 1, my own words)

The study puts an AI content checker (a VLM pointed at the slides) in the hands of a non-expert who holds power over an expert (a board member deciding the CISO's budget). The checker raises flags that are wrong for this company on three of five correct slides. The expert then answers each flag, either with the company's reason (two slides) or with a bare denial (one slide). Trust in the expert (0 to 10, possibly split into competence, integrity, benevolence) is rated at the start and before and after each flag and answer, so the flag's cost and the answer's repair are read within one session, against a neutral group that hears the same answers without flags. The claimed additions are: (1) the checker is the listener's tool, not the expert's; (2) the flags are wrong; (3) the expert answers; (4) trust is tracked step by step; (5) the setting is the boardroom.

## A. Preliminary verdict (written after step 2, before reading project notes; unchanged afterwards)

**Verdict: (b) novel only if the claim is narrowed.**

Each ingredient already exists somewhere in what I read; the combination does not.

- Wrong AI flags on correct content: Gupta et al. 2026 (wearable fact-checker, one false-positive nudge per participant on a true claim in a video; outcome is belief in the claim, not trust in the speaker), Horne and Nevo 2025 (wrong AI-style warning labels on true headlines; outcome is trust in the headline; source not shown), Lu et al. 2025 (55%-accurate AI credibility indicator disagreeing with "experts"; outcome is the participant's own veracity judgement), Du et al. 2026 (fictitious 87% AI-detection report on an unchanged student paper; outcome is the teacher's grading of the paper). None measures trust in a correct human expert after a wrong flag.
- AI checking a human expert, trust in that human measured: Kim et al. 2026 AIES (reactive AI oversight flags advisor errors; the flags were correct, the advisor really erred), Madanay et al. 2025 (AI and radiologist disagree; which is right is left open; agreement and provider rating drop when the radiologist recommends less testing than AI), Chen et al. 2026 (AI assistant "disagrees" with the doctor, no content, no reply), Chae and Tewksbury 2026 (AI-labelled fact-checks of Biden/Trump claims; no effect on politician competence or feeling thermometer). In all four the AI is part of the expert's or a third party's workflow, the expert never rebuts, and trust is rated once.
- The listener holding the tool and the power: Du et al. 2026 (teacher holds the detector and the grade, but the checked person is a student, not an expert the teacher depends on); von Schenk et al. 2024 (accusers consult an algorithm and the accusation costs the accused money; accused cannot reply). Gupta et al. 2026 has a listener-held checker but no power relation.
- The accused answers and trust is repaired: Kim et al. 2004 and Ferrin et al. 2007 (hiring manager role; human recruiter relays an unverified allegation; candidate apologises, denies, or stays reticent; Kim 2004 pilot rates trust before and after the allegation; Study 2 adds later evidence of guilt or innocence). These cover the repair logic thoroughly, but the accuser is human hearsay, not an AI tool, and the contrast is apology versus denial (and reticence), not reasoned rebuttal versus bare denial.
- Board and CISO: Lowry et al. 2026, Opdenbusch et al. 2025, Proudfoot et al. 2023, Aamir et al. 2026 are all qualitative interview or field studies; none is an experiment, none involves an AI tool.

So the defensible new claim is narrow: the first experiment in which a non-expert who holds power over an expert uses an AI checker whose flags are wrong, and the expert's reasoned answer versus bare denial is tested as the repair, with trust tracked flag by flag, in a board-CISO setting. A claim of novelty for "AI flags lower trust in the expert" or for "the expert's answer repairs trust" taken alone is not defensible. The abstract currently names Chen et al. 2026 as "the closest design", but Kim et al. 2026 is closer on most dimensions (AI oversight surfacing flags in front of an epistemically dependent lay user, trust in the human expert measured with the Hendriks expertise/integrity/benevolence instrument the abstract itself now proposes), and the abstract does not cite it, Madanay 2025, Gupta 2026 or Kim 2004. A reviewer who knows any of these would read the current framing as unaware of them.

## B. Final answers

### What step 4 changed

The project's notes back up the preliminary verdict, and in two places they make it stronger. (1) Kim et al. 2026 say plainly in their limitations that the AI "was always correct" and call for work that varies AI reliability (Kim-2026 text, Limitations, around p. 1520 and the discussion). That openly leaves E1 open as a gap in that line of work. (2) Two papers I had not read change E3. Pezzo et al. 2022 (Medical Decision Making, Experiment 2, from the abstract only, per results-4) varied the physician's stated reason for not consulting a diagnostic aid, and the reason moved competence ratings. Hendriks et al. 2016 (abstract not verified, results-3) showed an expert's flaw being pointed out by another person versus by the expert. So "the expert's reason for departing from the tool changes lay judgement" is partly covered, though not as a live reply to a flag. Two claims in the notes I would push back on. First, "Candidate papers" describes Madanay 2025 as "AI right by convention". The paper does not say which party was right (Madanay text, Methods; Limitations: "We also do not provide measures of accuracy for either AI or radiologists"), so it should not be read as a correct-AI study or as a wrong-AI study. Second, results-6 says "no study has measured trust in a live presenter while an audience member holds an automated checker". That holds for what I read, but it says nothing about Gupta et al. 2026, whose design is exactly that except for the outcome measure, so the gap statement has to rest on the outcome measure and not on the setup. The notes agree that the claim must narrow (Candidate papers, "Where the reviewers agree", and verified-view entry 16), and that matches my own verdict, which I reached before reading them.

### Q1. Verdict: **b**

The study as the abstract states it combines five things: an AI checker, wrong flags, the checker in the hands of the powerful non-expert, the expert's reasoned answer versus a bare denial, and trust tracked step by step. That combination has not been published. No single element is new, though, and the abstract as written gives the reader no sign that the nearest neighbours exist. It calls Chen et al. 2026 "the closest design" but does not cite Kim et al. 2026, Madanay 2025, Gupta 2026, Du 2026, Lu 2025, Horne and Nevo 2025, Kim et al. 2004 or Ferrin et al. 2007. A reader who knows that literature will find a novelty claim for "AI flag lowers trust in the expert" (H1) unconvincing: Madanay, Chen, Kim 2026 and Du between them already show it, or the closely parallel case. "A reasoned answer repairs more than a denial" (H2, H3) is trust-repair literature that has existed since 2004. What is new is the conjunction, and in particular the test of whether a correct expert's answer can undo a wrong AI flag that the evaluator holds. The claim has to be narrowed to that.

### Q2. Element by element

- **E1 (wrong AI flags on a correct human expert): partly.** Wrong AI flags on correct *content* are covered. Horne and Nevo 2025 put "AI Tool Says This Story is False" labels on true headlines and trust in the headline fell by 0.30 on a -1 to +1 scale (no source shown). Gupta et al. 2026 used one false-positive haptic nudge per participant on a true claim in a video, and belief in that claim fell, with no rating of the speaker. Lu et al. 2025 Experiment 3 used a 55%-accurate AI that misled veracity judgements, and the "expert" was an inserted vote whose standing was never measured. Du et al. 2026 used a fictitious 87% AI-detection report and teachers graded an identical paper lower, but the flagged person is a student, not an expert. The cases where the evaluated person is an expert either have a correct AI (Kim et al. 2026, stated limitation) or leave correctness open (Madanay 2025, Chen 2026). No study measures trust in a correct human expert after a wrong AI flag.
- **E2 (checker held by the non-expert who holds power over the expert): partly.** Du et al. 2026: the evaluator holds both the tool and the grade, but has more expertise than the person checked. von Schenk et al. 2024: the participant chooses to consult a lie detector and the accusation costs the accused money, but no expertise asymmetry is involved. Gupta et al. 2026: the listener holds the checker, with no power relation and no expert. Kim et al. 2004 / Ferrin et al. 2007: the participant holds hiring power, but the accusation is human hearsay. Kim et al. 2026: the user is epistemically dependent and holds no tool and no power. Spassova and Palmeira 2026 (new, see Q7) study the reverse side, the advisor's reaction when the client consults AI. No study combines the tool, the power and the lower expertise in one person.
- **E3 (the expert answers the flag; reasoned answer versus bare denial as the repair step): partly.** For human accusers it is covered well: Kim et al. 2004 (apology versus denial, where the denial carried an account blaming office politics; Study 2 adds third-party evidence of innocence) and Ferrin et al. 2007 (reticence did no better than the worse reply; no reply moved perceived competence). Close AI-domain neighbour: Pezzo et al. 2022, where the stated reason for not consulting a diagnostic aid changed competence ratings (abstract only, not verified by me). Not covered: an expert answering an AI flag, and the specific contrast of reasoned rebuttal versus bare denial. Chen 2026 and Madanay 2025 both note the missing reply as future work.
- **E4 (trust rated before, after the flag and after the answer in one session): partly.** Kim et al. 2004's pilot rated trust before and after the allegation (Table 1). Greitemeyer and Sagioglou 2015 and Mickelberg et al. 2025 rated before and after clearing. Nurek and Kostopoulou 2023 rated before and after the outcome was revealed (abstract, results-4). In the AI-checks-expert studies (Kim 2026, Madanay 2025, Chen 2026, Chae and Tewksbury 2026, Du 2026) there is a single post-measure. Repeated within-session trust ratings are a standard method and add no novelty on their own. Here they only add something because of what they are applied to.
- **E5 (E1 to E4 combined): not covered.** No study in the 129-entry index, the references folder, or my web search puts all four together.
- **E6 (board member and CISO): not covered** as an experiment. Lowry et al. 2026, Opdenbusch et al. 2025, Proudfoot et al. 2023 and Aamir et al. 2026 are interview or field studies with no trust measure and no AI tool. The setting is new, but a reviewer will treat it as an application domain and not as a contribution. The contribution has to be the mechanism.

### Q3. Closest published study

Kim, Daneshi, Draper and Sreedharan (2026, AIES; `Kim-2026-asymmetric-trust-corrective-AI-AIES.pdf`) is closer than Chen et al. 2026, the study the abstract names. It has an epistemically dependent layperson (a student), a human expert (an academic advisor), and an AI that in the reactive condition monitors the expert and flags problems where the layperson can see them. The outcome is trust in the human expert, measured with METI's expertise, integrity and benevolence, the same three dimensions Abstract v7 now proposes. N = 157, and the design is 3 (no AI, advisor-invoked, automatic monitoring) x 2 (advisor error or not). What separates it from Abstract v7: (1) its flags were always correct, because the advisor really erred, while ours are always wrong; (2) the AI belongs to the workflow, not to the trustor, and the trustor holds no power; (3) the advisor never defends the advice and simply corrects the error, so there is no rebuttal step; (4) trust is rated once after the task, between subjects; (5) the stakes are low by design. Its central null (the way the AI was involved did not redistribute trust) also goes against the abstract's H4b amplification. Chen et al. 2026 is close on a different axis: an AI that disagrees, with no content and no reply. It has no visible flag on the work, no check of correctness, and no power relation.

### Q4. Strongest case against novelty (hostile reviewer, under 150 words)

"Every component has been published. AI disagreement with a professional lowers lay evaluation of that professional (Madanay 2025, N = 1,606; Chen 2026; Kim 2026). People follow AI flags even when they are wrong (Horne and Nevo 2025; Lu 2025; Gupta 2026), including power holders judging identical work (Du 2026). An accused party's reply repairs trust according to whether it supplies the missing information (Kim 2004; Ferrin 2007), and exoneration restores trust to baseline but not above it (Greitemeyer 2015). The proposal puts these together in a boardroom vignette and predicts what each paper already predicts. The 'repair' step is Kim 2004 with an AI as the accuser. The boardroom framing adds no mechanism: 40 unpaid network contacts, assigned in turn rather than at random, are not board members. The authors cite none of the closest designs and call a weaker one 'closest'."

### Q5. Strongest case for novelty (same length)

"The literature has a hole exactly where AI checkers are heading. Every study of an AI checking a professional uses an AI that is correct or of unknown accuracy, and none lets the professional answer. Kim et al. 2026 call for varying AI reliability, and Madanay 2025 and Chen 2026 call for testing the professional's communication. Trust-repair studies use human hearsay and have no tool. Wrong-flag studies measure belief in content, never trust in the person whose content was flagged. This design asks the policy-relevant question no one has tested: when a fallible tool held by the person with formal power wrongly flags a correct expert, does the expert's reason undo the damage, and does a bare denial fail? Flags on unflagged slides, a neutral group that hears identical answers, and stepwise ratings let the study separate the costs of the flag, the presence of the checker and the reply. Consumer camera assistants make this scenario current."

### Q6. Suggested contribution wording (verdict b)

"We test, for the first time to our knowledge, whether a correct expert can repair the trust lost to a wrong AI flag raised by the non-expert who oversees them: board members using a simulated slide checker hear the CISO answer each flag with the company's reason or with a bare denial, and trust is rated before and after each flag and answer against a group that hears the same answers without flags. Prior studies of AI checking professionals used correct or unverified AI and gave the professional no reply (Kim et al., 2026; Madanay et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026); trust-repair studies used human accusers (Kim et al., 2004; Ferrin et al., 2007)."

Two consequences for the abstract itself: replace "Chen et al. (2026) is the closest design" with Kim et al. 2026, or cite both. And present H1 as a replication in a new setting, not as a finding, so that H2 and H3 carry the novelty.
