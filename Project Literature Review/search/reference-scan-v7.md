## 1. Summary

1. Section 1 cites only sources that describe boards from the outside or the CISO's side; it is missing the one practitioner source that says boards judge the CISO by delivery (Proudfoot et al., 2023), the director-guidance view that opposes the SEC's reasoning (NACD & ISA, 2026), and any evidence that directors already use AI (Nolen, 2026).
2. Claim 12 is contradicted inside the review's own pool: D. Kim et al. (2026, AIES) and Chen et al. (2026, IJHCS) are checker studies that did measure trust in the person checked. The sentence must say that in those studies the tool was not in the listener's hand.
3. Claim 8 sets Opdenbusch's "the CISOs are the ones in power" against the board's formal power, but Lowry et al. (2026, p. 799), already cited, say the opposite: their CISOs "are not considered 'powerful'" and boards defer "due to a gap in expertise rather than power".
4. Top three additions: (1) D. Kim et al. (2026) as the qualifier of claim 12; (2) Proudfoot et al. (2023, pp. 240, 246) for claim 3; (3) NACD & ISA (2026, p. 17) as the counterweight to the SEC's p. 85 reasoning in claim 2.
5. All additions should be clauses, not sentences: the section is 762 words of a 1,500-word review.

### Current section 1 citations that a pool source would replace or must be paired with

- **Opdenbusch et al. (2025, p. 2), "the CISOs are the ones in power" (claim 8): keep, but pair with Lowry et al. (2026, p. 799).** Lowry et al. write that "Unlike CEOs, the CISOs in our study are not considered 'powerful'" and that non-expert boards defer "due to a gap in expertise rather than power" (printed p. 799, verified in the full text). The two sources the paragraph relies on disagree on exactly the point it makes. One clause resolves it in the draft's own terms: "Lowry et al. (2026) attribute the same deference to a gap in expertise rather than power (p. 799)". This is the reconciliation design rule 7 in `verified-view.md` already asks for.
- **Meta (2024) (claim 6): replace with Nolen (2026) if a word must go.** The Ray-Ban page shows that a camera assistant exists; it says nothing about directors. Microsoft (n.d.) should stay, because its "holes in [a participant's] argument" prompt is the live meeting function closest to the study's checker. If both product pages stay, add Nolen for the "almost every director" part.
- **Claim 6, "an evident need for a check that does not depend on its own expertise" (no citation, marked in the working notes as the study's inference): NACD & ISA (2026, p. 18), "trust but verify", can carry it,** which turns an unsourced inference into a sourced professional norm.
- **Claim 12, "All of this evidence comes from interviews": imprecise.** Lowry et al. (2026) add a survey of 33 CISOs (the source of the 40% in claim 8) and a proxy-statement check of 1,000 firms. "Interviews and small surveys" is accurate. If Nolen (2026) is added, the same applies.
- **Claim 12, "Studies of checking tools in human-computer interaction ... measure belief in claims or trust in the AI rather than trust in the person checked": wrong as stated** (ranks 1 and 5). Danry et al. (2020) and Lu et al. (2025) are correct examples of what they illustrate; the generalisation needs the qualifier.
- **Schilke et al. (2015) (claim 10): no better source in the pool.** Keep; the caveat already in the draft answers Wu & Wilkes (2016).
- **P. H. Kim et al. (2004), Ferrin et al. (2007), Aamir et al. (2026), SEC (2023), Lowry et al. (2026, pp. 783, 796, 798): no better source in the pool.**
