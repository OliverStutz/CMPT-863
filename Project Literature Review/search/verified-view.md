## 1. Kim, Daneshi, Draper & Sreedharan (2026), AIES. Asymmetric trust effects of corrective AI in expert advisory workflows under epistemic dependence. (read 2026-10-18)

**Oliver's reading.** Highly connected to what we do, although it is more workflow-related. It uses the METI scale.

**What the full text adds.** The connection is in the roles: a non-expert who cannot verify the advice, a human expert, and an AI that monitors and flags the expert, with trust in the expert as the outcome. The workflow focus is the manipulation: the AI is absent, invoked by the advisor, or monitors automatically, and that factor changed nothing. What moved trust was whether the advisor had erred. The AI was always right, the advisor never replied, and the student held no power over the advisor. Trust was measured with METI (expertise, integrity, benevolence), an entity trust scale, a 100-point rating and intention to reuse the advisor. The authors name the AI's constant correctness as a limitation and call for work that varies AI uncertainty or failure.

**Consequence for our study.** Three things make ours different and should be said in the review: the flags are wrong, the expert answers, and the listener holds the power. METI is the measure to adopt for the three-part trust rating (competence, benevolence, integrity), which also makes our results comparable with theirs. Their null for "how the AI is involved" cuts against H4b.

**Open.** Pull the cell means for the no-error monitoring condition to see whether the checker's presence alone moved trust (bears on our "No issues found" slides).

## 2. Kim, Ferrin, Cooper & Dirks (2004), J. Applied Psychology. Removing the shadow of suspicion: Apology versus denial. (read 2026-10-18)

**Oliver's reading.** The paper seemed to be about CEO pay or trust; the connection to our study is only in the finding that a denial of wrongdoing followed by evidence of innocence will probably not damage trust.

**What the full text says.** The paper is not about CEO pay. Participants acted as a hiring manager and watched a videotaped interview of a job applicant for an accounting position who was accused of a past violation: filing an incorrect tax return through lack of knowledge (competence) or on purpose (integrity). The candidate apologised or denied. Study 1 (N = 200) found apology repaired trust better after the competence violation and denial after the integrity violation, on perceived integrity and on the intentions (willingness to risk, hiring, job responsibilities), not on perceived competence. Study 2 (N = 444) added a memo from HR reporting a state board ruling of guilty or innocent. Evidence of innocence raised every measure, and a response that did not fit the later evidence cost trust: apology followed by innocence did worse than denial followed by innocence. Oliver's takeaway is correct for that part. Two details matter for us: their denial was not bare (the candidate denied, blamed office politics at the previous firm, and gave an assurance), and the innocence came from a third party, not from the accused.

**Consequence for our study.** Our "denial with no reason" is weaker than their denial, which carried an account. The paper therefore does not predict that a bare denial repairs trust; it predicts that an innocent party who denies with an account does better than one who apologises. H3 should be worded as "a denial without a reason restores less trust than a reasoned answer", and Ferrin et al. (2007) on reticence is the closer test of a reply with no information. The paper also shows why our participants never learn that the CISO was right: in their design the clearing came from a board ruling, and in ours the CISO's reason has to do that work alone.

**Open.** None on the design. Table 6 (p. 113) has the cell means if a number is needed.

## 3. Madanay, O'Donohue & Zikmund-Fisher (2025), JMIR. Patient reactions to AI–clinician discrepancies. (read 2026-10-18)

**Oliver's reading.** It crosses an interesting point: the penalty appeared only when the human's advice fell short of the AI's. In our case, even if the checker lists an issue, the outcome should differ. We need to design the study so that we do not prove the same thing, and make sure the human always has more inputs than the AI and more suggestions than the AI gives.

**What the full text says.** 1,606 US adults imagined a lung-cancer screening CT. The radiologist who recommended less testing than the AI was agreed with less (mean 4.01 against 4.55 to 4.63), was least likely to be recommended to family and friends, and was rated the worst provider. The radiologist who recommended more testing than the AI paid no penalty. Patients who prefer aggressive care sided with the radiologist who overcalled and against the one who undercalled. The AI never explained itself and the radiologist never answered.

**Consequence for our study.** Oliver's design rule: the CISO's answer must always contain more than the flag. A flag is one line ("Numbers do not add up"); the reasoned answer should give the company's reason, the figure the checker misread, and what the CISO already did about it, so that the human visibly exceeds the AI in inputs and in proposals. The denial condition is then the only case where the CISO offers less than the checker, which is the contrast H3 needs. Second rule from the asymmetry: a flag that makes the CISO look less careful than the checker ("Architecture likely wrong") is the Madanay direction and should move trust; a flag that makes the CISO look more careful than needed would not, and we do not use one. Say in the review that Madanay's observers never heard the expert answer, which is what we add.

**Open.** Check whether the published version reports the three outcome measures separately by condition, for the review's numbers.

## 4. Hendriks, Kienhues & Bromme (2015), PLOS ONE. METI. (read 2026-10-19)

**Oliver's reading.** A great reference for what trust in an expert is made of, and the scale Kim et al. (2026) used. But it has shortcomings for our study. We want participants to respond casually to the three metrics in Abstract v7, not to think too much and too precisely about the CISO; a full inventory at every step would overpollute the study and tire the participants out. We test this properly by running the study first with the measures as written in Abstract v7. METI is the reference the three items are drawn from, not the instrument.

**What the full text says.** Epistemic trustworthiness is defined (p. 3) as "those features of experts that decide whether recipients will depend on and defer to them due to their own limited resources": a layperson's judgement of whether to accept an expert's claim they cannot check themselves. The authors set it apart from source credibility, which names what makes a message persuasive; epistemic trustworthiness is the recipient's guard against being misled, and trust adds dependence and exposure to risk. The scale is fourteen antonym pairs rated on a 7-point semantic differential, in three subscales. Expertise (6): competent, intelligent, well educated, professional, experienced, qualified. Integrity (4): sincere, honest, just, fair. Benevolence (4): moral, ethical, responsible, considerate. Cronbach's alpha in Study 1 was .91, .82 and .90. Validated by exploratory factor analysis (n = 237), confirmatory factor analysis (n = 345) and an experiment (n = 137) in which a source description aimed at one dimension moved that subscale most. The factors correlate (expertise with integrity .63, integrity with benevolence .71), so they are facets of one construct, not independent traits. The inventory was built for a single judgement of an unfamiliar online source and was given once per study, in a survey of about ten minutes. The paper says nothing about repeated administration within one session.

**Consequence for our study.** Our procedure takes seven trust ratings in about ten minutes: one baseline, then one after each flag and one after each answer on slides 2 to 4. The full METI at each point would be 98 adjective ratings; the Abstract v7 measures are 28 (the 0-to-10 trust rating plus one item each for competence, benevolence and integrity). The design therefore stays with Abstract v7 and METI supplies the wording: one pair per subscale, taken from the strongest-loading items (competent–incompetent, sincere–insincere, moral–immoral), so the three items are defensibly METI-derived and the paper is cited as their basis. This replaces design rule 1's "adopt METI". Two costs to state in the review: single items are less reliable than four- to six-item subscales, and the results compare with Kim et al. (2026) in construct but not in instrument. The paper's own factor correlations predict that the three ratings will move together, which is acceptable for a casual reading. If the three items turn out uninformative in the first run, the fallback is the full METI once, at the end of the session, never at every step.

**Open.** Fix the wording of the three items (strongest-loading pair or subscale label). Pilot whether seven rounds of four ratings already feel repetitive.

## 5. Opdenbusch, Hielscher & Sasse (2025), NDSS. "Where are we on cyber?" (read 2026-10-19)

**Oliver's reading.** The paper reinforces the idea that C-level managers and board members need proper help with cybersecurity knowledge. That makes the study of trust in the CISO, and of what a checker does to it, highly relevant.

**What the full text says.** Eighteen interviews in the UK: six C-level executives, four non-executive directors (NEDs), five CISOs and three C-level consultants, from companies with tens to hundreds of thousands of employees; nine of the eighteen sat on a board. Recruitment started through the National Cyber Security Centre and continued by snowballing, which the authors name as a possible bias toward leaders who already think about cyber. All participants saw asking the right questions as the NED's primary task, and the best question boards usually manage is "Have you had penetration testing?" (EX6). Most participants said the budget is the only decision boards make on cyber; CISO1: "The board does not really make decisions. They look at what we do and ask questions." Four participants (CISO4, EX6, NED3, NED4) said NEDs are "too shy to ask questions since they do not want to get embarrassed for their missing knowledge"; NED3 called it "a confidence issue" and EX6 said boards see cyber as "a dark art, a bunch of chaps in hoodies". The phrase "IT novices" is the authors' own wording in the abstract, not a participant quote. The authors' third contribution is that "the CISOs are the ones in power", because they and the executives decide what information reaches the board; the discussion calls this a power imbalance in which CISOs "unfold their power indirectly by silently deciding what information reaches the executives". CISOs translate cyber into the language of risk, in one case through the CFO, who presents for the CISO and relays the board's questions back. The remedies the paper proposes are education of the board (CISO3: "They needed to be educated first before they could be challenging"), benchmarks published by public agencies, regulation that sets what information boards must receive, and a cyber risk subcommittee with the CISO and at least one NED. The authors add that available training is "time-consuming and generic" and that NEDs prefer to learn from peers. NED1, who does ask questions, reported that the executive team now sends "more technical people" to the board because of it. The paper reports no measurements: no trust scale, no AI, no checker, and the authors write that the sample "can not be used for generalization".

**Consequence for our study.** Three things. First, the paper gives the mechanism behind our default silence, and it is fear of exposure, not lack of interest. H4a should name that mechanism: a flag from a checker gives the board member an external, face-saving reason to ask, because the doubt is the tool's and not theirs. Second, "power" needs two words in the review. The board holds formal power (budget and oversight); the CISO holds informational power (what reaches the board). Our scenario puts the checker on the side with formal power and asks whether it shifts informational power back, which is a cleaner statement of the gap than "the listener holds the power". Third, the paper's remedies are all heavy (training, committees, regulation), and the authors themselves say training is slow and generic; a checker is a lighter intervention that none of the eighteen mentioned, which is the review's motivation for testing one. Two design details follow: the scenario should include the standard board question "Are we investing enough?", since that is the question every CISO in the sample gets, and the flags should be more specific than "Have you had penetration testing?", which the paper gives as the ceiling of what boards ask unaided. State the paper's limits: qualitative, eighteen UK participants from the largest firms, and no measure of trust, so it motivates the study and predicts no magnitude.

**Open.** When quoting, attribute "IT novices" to the authors' abstract, or use NED3's "confidence issue" for a participant's voice. Pair with Lowry, Vance & Vance (2026), paper 8, for field numbers on the same pattern.

## 8. Lowry, Vance & Vance (2026), Management Science. Inexpert supervision. (read 2026-10-19)

**Oliver's reading.** Two things caught his attention. First, non-expert board members claim they can ask the right questions even without expertise; a checker would either reinforce or destroy that self-perception. Second, and more important, CISOs withhold information from the board. That underlines how much the relationship between board members and the CISO matters: the CISO has a direct reporting line to the board, but the job is at stake if they report something the board does not want to hear. This matches Oliver's own experience as an auditor. Must be added.

**What the full text says.** A US field study: 41 interviews with 38 people, about 50 minutes each. Twenty directors of small-, mid- and large-cap firms (5 with cybersecurity expertise, 15 without), 11 cybersecurity executives who report to boards and 7 senior consultants who advise boards; for three directors the authors also interviewed the CISO who reports to them. Two supplements: a survey of 33 further CISOs, and a proxy-statement check of 1,000 Russell 3000 firms, of which 14.7 percent disclosed a director with cybersecurity skills (100 percent disclosed a financial expert). The abstract's wording is that non-expert directors' efforts "lack substance and therefore are mostly symbolic, even when they perform the same oversight activities as expert directors". On questioning: non-expert directors said their general experience lets them "ask the right questions" (D-3: "We ask the right questions, we get the right people in front of us"), and 8 of 13 coded non-expert directors said expertise makes no real difference to questioning, while all 5 expert directors and 12 of 13 executives and consultants said it does (Table 2). The experts' view is that non-experts ask superficial questions ("How are we doing?"), reactive ones after a breach in the news, or questions off a professional body's list, and cannot judge the answer: "a dialogue of the deaf" (D-12), "smart enough to ask the questions, just not yet smart enough to interpret what the CISO is saying" (C-7), and the value lies in "the second, and third, and fourth, and fifth questions" (C-6). One executive gives the same silence mechanism as Opdenbusch et al.: "if you don't know, you don't want to ask questions" (E-5). On the relationship: non-expert boards rely on the CISO to "coach" them, which includes setting what good looks like, deciding what is reported, and in one case handing the board the list of questions to ask the CISO; the authors call this "circular governance". On filtering (section 4.2.4): some CISOs overstate performance or understate risk to make themselves or their boss look better; a CISO described a former CIO whose direction was "paint your performance in the best possible light" and a new CIO who "refused" to show poor metrics. Most non-expert directors did not believe CISOs filter ("like internal auditors. They're not trying to hide things", D-10); 4 of 13 saw it as a concern, against 4 of 5 expert directors and 12 of 16 executives and consultants (Table 3). The 33 surveyed CISOs put the share of CISOs who filter their board reports at a median of 40 percent, all but one above zero. Two framings to carry: an expert director's line that for most boards "all they can do is assess the credibility of the executives that are put in front of them" (D-9), and the authors' claim, against Opdenbusch et al., that CISOs are not powerful relative to boards and that the deference comes from low expertise, not CISO power. One correction to the reading: the paper's stated motive for filtering is looking good, for oneself or one's superior; it does not say the CISO's job is at risk for bad news. The only job-related statement runs the other way: expert boards can direct management to replace the CISO. The job-at-stake point is Oliver's inference from audit practice and should be labelled as such.

**Consequence for our study.** First, D-9's line is the review's anchor for the outcome variable: when a board cannot judge the substance, what it judges is the credibility of the person presenting, and that is what we measure. Second, the paper describes a checker's role before anyone built one. A checker is a question list from a third party, like the professional bodies' lists, and the paper says such lists produce the first question but not the follow-ups, and that non-experts cannot interpret the answer. So H4a (more questions) is well grounded, while H2 and H3 carry a risk the paper names: participants may judge the CISO's answer on delivery, not content, as the consultant who saw CISOs use "likability and personality to put the board at ease" (C-6) warns. The reasoned answer and the bare denial must therefore be matched on delivery (same speaker, tone and confidence), so that the reason is the only difference. Third, filtering gives the checker its appeal and the wrong flag its cost: a board that believes CISOs shade their reports has a prior that makes any flag credible, and the paper shows this belief splits directors. That belief is worth one question at the end of the session, as a moderator of H1. Fourth, Oliver's first point is a second, exploratory outcome: a checker may raise or lower the board member's confidence that they asked the right questions. One item on that would be cheap; it stays exploratory. Fifth, in the review, pair this paper with Opdenbusch et al. (2025) as the US and UK accounts of the same mechanism, and reconcile their disagreement on power with rule 7: the board holds formal power, the CISO informational power, and the paper's own term for the result is circular governance. Limits to state: qualitative, snowball sample, US public companies, no trust measure; the 40 percent is CISOs' impression of other CISOs, not observed behaviour.

**Open.** Decide whether to add the two end-of-session items (belief that CISOs filter; confidence in one's own questions). If a number is needed, Table 2 (p. 794) and Table 3 (p. 796) have the counts; footnotes 22 and 23 (p. 804) describe the CISO survey.

## 10. Ferrin, Kim, Cooper & Dirks (2007), J. Applied Psychology. Silence speaks volumes. (read 2026-10-19; full text in `../references/` as `Ferrin-2007-silence-speaks-volumes-JAP.pdf`)

**Oliver's reading.** At first sight unrelated to Abstract v7; after the link to H3 was laid out, keep it as comparison data for our results. The question it lets us ask: does the board member treat the checker's output as verified, undisputed information, and then follow the classical trust-loss pattern when using a checker?

**What the full text says.** Two between-subjects experiments, each a 3 (reply: apology, denial, reticence) x 2 (accusation: integrity, competence) design with trust measured once, after the reply; there is no rating before the accusation. Study 1: 102 graduate students in negotiation courses at a university in the western United States acted as a hiring manager and watched a videotaped interview in which the recruiter reported, as hearsay from references, that the applicant had filed an incorrect tax return, either on purpose (integrity) or through inadequate knowledge of tax codes (competence). The apology admitted responsibility and promised it would not recur; the denial denied, blamed office politics at the previous firm, and gave an assurance; the reticent applicant "would not go on the record as saying the accusations were either true or false", called the situation complex and confidential, and gave the same assurance. Study 2: 241 undergraduate business students in Singapore read a news article about the CEO of a US Fortune 500 company accused of receiving preferred stock he had agreed to forgo; the reticent CEO said "I cannot confirm or disconfirm the veracity of the accusation", and the article closed by saying independent reports could not verify the details and some might never become known. Measures: perceived integrity, perceived competence, willingness to risk, and in Study 1 a hiring decision, on 7-point scales. Results: the reply type never moved perceived competence in either study (the interaction was not significant), so the authors dropped that measure; replies moved perceived integrity and willingness to risk. For an integrity accusation, denial did best; for a competence accusation, apology did best. Reticence was below the best reply and indistinguishable from the worse one. In the competence condition, Study 1 perceived integrity was 4.98 after apology, 4.21 after denial and 4.17 after reticence; willingness to risk 3.57, 3.00 and 3.26. Study 2: 4.70, 3.99 and 3.60 for perceived integrity; 3.70, 3.43 and 3.03 for willingness to risk. Pooled across accusation types in Study 2, the best reply gave 3.77 on perceived integrity, reticence 3.04 and the worse reply 3.13. Effect sizes for best reply against reticence were about d = 0.5 to 0.9. The authors' explanation is information: a reply repairs trust only where it supplies what the listener weighs most, which for a competence accusation is a signal that the person will be reliable from now on; denial and reticence both fail to supply it.

**Consequence for our study.** First, this is the H3 citation. Our bare denial sits between their denial (which carried an account) and their reticence (which took no stance), and in the competence case those two performed alike, so the prediction for ours is the same: no repair. The paper cannot say whether a bare reply lowers trust further, because it has no before-and-after rating; H3 should therefore be worded as "restores less than a reasoned answer", as rules 2 and the reviewers already say. Second, Oliver's comparison: their accusations were hearsay that nobody verified and that still cost trust. If our flag, from an AI that participants are told nothing about, produces the same ordering (reasoned answer well above the bare denial, bare denial no better than the flag left it) with gaps of the same order on our scale, then the board member gave the AI's flag at least hearsay-level credibility. The neutral group, which hears the same answers without a flag, is what separates the flag's credibility from the answer's quality; that comparison should be a planned analysis, not an afterthought. Third, their finding that no reply moved perceived competence matters for our three items: expect the CISO's answer to move the integrity item and the overall rating, and expect the competence item to stay where the flag put it. Analyse the items separately and say this in advance. Fourth, the paper confirms a design choice: the accused answers for themselves and the audience never learns the truth, as in ours. Limits to state: students, a stranger judged once, scales not ours.

**Open.** Decide whether a single end-of-session manipulation check ("Did you think the checker's flags were correct?") is allowed under rule 10; it measures belief in the flags, not trust in the AI, but it is close. Table 2 (p. 900) and Table 5 (p. 903) have the cell means if the review needs them.

## 11. Mayer, Davis & Schoorman (1995), AMR. An integrative model of organizational trust. (read 2026-10-19)

**Oliver's reading.** It supports the metric (competence, benevolence, integrity). At first sight the paper is about something else, trust and risk taking inside a relationship, while our board members have no relationship with the CISO yet; this is their first meeting. On reflection it supports our cause, though how much of it applies is not yet clear. The risk-taking part applies to both groups through the budget approval, so we can find out how much the checker changes risk taking at the end, that is, whether integrity is sustained when a human is augmented with a fact-checker.

**What the full text says.** A theory paper, no data. Trust is defined (p. 712) as "the willingness of a party to be vulnerable to the actions of another party based on the expectation that the other will perform a particular action important to the trustor, irrespective of the ability to monitor or control that other party". The model has four inputs and one output. Inputs: the trustor's propensity to trust (a stable disposition, Proposition 1, p. 716) and three perceived characteristics of the trustee: ability, "that group of skills, competencies, and characteristics" that give influence in a specific domain (p. 717); benevolence, "the extent to which a trustee is believed to want to do good to the trustor" apart from any profit motive (p. 718); and integrity, the perception that the trustee adheres to principles the trustor finds acceptable (p. 719). Trust is a function of the three (Proposition 2, p. 720). Output: trust is compared with the perceived risk of a specific act; if trust exceeds that risk the trustor takes the act, which the authors call risk taking in the relationship (Proposition 5, p. 726); the act's outcome feeds back into the three perceptions (Proposition 6). On the first-meeting question, the authors are explicit (p. 722): the model "can explain trust (based on propensity) before any relationship between two parties has developed"; early on, the trustor gets integrity data "through third-party sources and observation, with little direct interaction", while benevolence data needs repeated interaction. Proposition 3: integrity matters most early, before any benevolence data; Proposition 4: benevolence gains weight as the relationship develops. The authors also separate trust from its outcome and from perceived risk: a person may cooperate because the risk is low, not because they trust, and trust itself carries no risk until an act is taken.

**Consequence for our study.** The first-meeting setting is the model's early stage, not an exception to it. Four things follow. First, the baseline rating, taken before any slide, is mostly propensity to trust; Abstract v7 already uses each participant's first rating as their own baseline, and this paper is the reason. Second, a prediction for the three items in a ten-minute first meeting: competence and integrity carry the judgement, because they come from observation and from third-party sources, and the checker's flag is a third-party source in the paper's sense; benevolence should stay near flat. With Ferrin et al. (paper 10): the flag hits competence, the answer repairs through integrity, benevolence barely moves. Third, Oliver's point: the budget decision is the paper's risk taking in the relationship, taken by both groups after the same slides. Proposition 5 says it depends on trust and on the perceived risk of the act, so the stakes of the budget decision must be identical across groups and visible on the slide, and the decision by group is the test of whether the checker's effect on trust reaches an act. Fourth, the feedback loop (Proposition 6) is what our participants never get: they never learn whether the CISO was right, so the study measures trust before any outcome feeds back; say so as a limit and as a design choice shared with Ferrin et al.

**Open.** Decide whether to add one item on the perceived risk of the budget decision, so that Proposition 5 can be tested rather than assumed. Correction to an earlier note: Propositions 3 and 4 are on p. 722, not 723.

## 13. Proudfoot, Cram, Madnick & Coden (2023), MISQE. Board member actions for cybersecurity governance. (read 2026-10-19)

**Oliver's reading.** Supports the study, and especially the study design: we cannot simply make the CISO look credible, or we would influence the outcome ourselves.

**What the full text says.** A practitioner article built on in-depth interviews with 35 cybersecurity experts and current board members (business executives, CISOs, CTOs, compliance officers, board advisors) from finance, technology, communications, media, healthcare, critical infrastructure and insurance, from Fortune 100 companies to smaller firms; no counts by role in the text, the appendix lists them. Four challenges and ten actions. Challenge 2, board-executive interaction dynamics (pp. 240 to 241), carries the finding: the "board's perceived competence of the CISO ... is heavily determined by the CISO's ability to effectively communicate"; a technically excellent CISO who cannot explain plainly "may be perceived as incompetent or ineffective", while "a marginally qualified CISO who appears credible ... may (dangerously) be perceived as highly qualified and effective, thereby triggering a sense of complacency among board members". A board member: when CISOs cannot present, "that is when you get the blank stares and no follow-up questions" (Interview 5). Action 5 (pp. 245 to 246) turns this into advice: "Board members should not simply take executive reporting at face value or treat the perceived credibility of the CISO as sufficient", with a board member's confession that management "is always on audition. And if the person giving the presentation ... comes across as credible and on top of their game, the board is kind of done with the issue. It becomes a relatively surface-level understanding" (Interview 10). Three details for us. Boards meet the CISO formally a few times a year, "perhaps for as little as 10-15 minutes per session" (Action 5). Confronting an executive in the meeting breaks a norm: "Don't show up the CEO. If you have an issue, take it out of the meeting" (Interview 11), and the authors warn that aggressive or confrontational boards get "a less than constructive partnership in the future". Board handouts use green, yellow and red dashboards, and "handouts dominated with green shading can instill a false sense of protection in the board". One board member wanted comfort "codified by giving a symbol of approval of a third party" (Interview 10). No interviewee mentions a tool the board uses to check the CISO; the ten actions contain none.

**Consequence for our study.** Oliver's point is the main one: the CISO's delivery is a variable in both directions, and both ends would flatten the study. A CISO who comes across as "on top of their game" makes the board "done with the issue", so a flag might not dent trust and H1 fails for the wrong reason; a CISO who cannot present produces blank stares and no questions, so H4a fails for the wrong reason. The CISO must therefore be pitched in the middle, plain and competent without charisma, identical in every condition, and the pilot must show the baseline rating landing mid-scale with room to move both ways. This extends rule 8 from the two answers to the whole briefing. Second, the 10-to-15-minute slot is our briefing length; cite it for realism. Third, "don't show up the CEO" is the norm the checker group is asked to break when they challenge the CISO in the meeting; that is the social cost in rule 7, and the paper's warning about damaged partnerships is a cost of checkers to name as a limit. Fourth, the green-dashboard complacency predicts a small rise in trust after "No issues found"; Abstract v7 already compares the groups at slide 1, and this paper gives the prediction a direction. Fifth, the wish for "a symbol of approval of a third party" is why a board would adopt a checker at all; cite it in the motivation next to Opdenbusch et al. and Lowry et al.

**Open.** Pilot the CISO video for a mid-scale baseline before the main run. Check the appendix for the role counts if the review needs them.

## 15. Du, Liu & Xian (2026), Frontiers in Psychology. Automation bias in teachers' evaluation of student writing. (read 2026-10-19)

**Oliver's reading.** Highly related: our flag marks something that is completely correct, as their report did. We have not yet put any colour scheme into the checker's design, and this paper says the colour matters.

**What the full text says.** A 2 x 2 between-subjects online experiment with 214 university teachers in China (232 recruited, 18 removed by pre-set rules; 53 to 55 per cell), mostly social sciences, 4 to 10 years of teaching, 7.5 percent had ever used an AI-detection tool. All read the same medium-quality social-science course paper with a report from a fictitious system, "AcademicCheck", that showed an AI-detection rate of 7 or 87 percent, with or without a "report-presentation package": four target sentences highlighted in red plus wording that pointed to "review-worthy passages". The authors are explicit that the second factor is colour plus wording, not colour alone (limitation 3). The procedure does not mention telling teachers that the detector could be wrong. Measures: overall quality 1 to 10, a 0 to 100 score, three 7-point dimensions (originality, language, logical structure), intention to intervene (require revision, remind the student about AI norms, ask for an explanation of the writing process), perceived AI authorship, and how credible the report itself seemed. Results (Table, p. 7): the warning alone cut the overall rating of the identical paper from 6.83 to 5.02 on the 10-point scale, and the score from 73.3 to 60.6; red highlighting cut it further, to 4.26 and 51.6. Intention to intervene rose from 3.40 to 4.89 with the warning and to 5.66 with red highlighting (7-point scale). Red highlighting also made the report itself seem more credible (3.17 to 3.96 at the low rate, 5.30 to 6.08 at the high rate) and raised suspicion even at the 7 percent rate (perceived AI authorship 2.23 to 2.92). The student never answered, and no trust in the student was measured. Limits the authors give: a fictitious interface, one paper whose medium quality was not validated, some scales near their endpoints, and colour confounded with wording.

**Consequence for our study.** First, this is H1 in another domain with the same structure: an evaluator with power over the person evaluated, a wrong automated flag, identical work rated lower. Cite it beside Madanay et al. (paper 3) as the two closest experiments, and note that both lacked the evaluated person's answer, which we add. Second, the drop of 1.8 points on a 10-point scale from the warning alone is the nearest benchmark for the size of H1 on our 0-to-10 rating; use it for the power estimate, with the caveat that it is a grade of a paper, not trust in a person. Third, Oliver's point: the presentation of the flag is a factor the design has not settled. The paper says that red highlighting of the flagged passage makes the flag itself seem more credible, enlarges the drop, and raises the urge to intervene, which is our H4a act. The decision: pick one presentation, hold it identical across every flag, and say in the review that it was fixed, not varied. The recommendation is to use what real tools and board dashboards use (Proudfoot et al., paper 13): a red flag with the flagged slide region marked on flagged slides, and a calm green "No issues found" on slides 1 and 5. This is the stronger cue, so H1 and H4a will be measured at their upper end; state that as a limit. The paper's own limitation applies to us as well: colour and wording are confounded in a single panel, so no claim about colour alone. Fourth, their "ask for an explanation of the writing process" is the analogue of our "ask the CISO to explain", which supports H4a's wording.

**Open.** Fix the flag panel's design (colour, marked region, wording) before the pilot, and keep the "No issues found" panel visually quiet. Supplementary Table B1 has the exact prompt wording if the review quotes it.

## 16. Lu, Li, Wang & Yin (2025), CHI. AI-based credibility indicators when people are influenced by peers and experts. (read 2026-10-19)

**Oliver's reading.** First worry: does it take our novelty away? After checking, no; it supports us, because it does something similar and shows that an AI highlighting things that are not wrong is still believed. This has to be built into the study design, the evaluation and the interpretation: we can assume that even though the flagged content is correct, the flag will cause a negative view that persists, because participants without education in cybersecurity will believe the LLM checker.

**What the full text says.** Three pre-registered randomised experiments on Amazon Mechanical Turk with 40 health-related news headlines (20 true, 20 false) that people judged correctly only 50 to 60 percent of the time on their own. The "expert" was not a person: a two-phase design collected sequences of peer votes, then the authors appended one artificial expert vote, marked with a graduation-cap icon and a note that the voter has expertise in medicine, nursing, biology or pharmacy, agreeing with the AI in one copy and disagreeing in another. In Experiments 1 and 2 the AI was always right, so whenever it disagreed with the expert the expert was wrong; Experiment 2 added a platform-style "verified" badge to the expert. Experiment 3 (644 workers, 373 in the analysed phase) used a real GPT-3.5 classifier that was right 80 percent of the time and a weak classifier right 55 percent of the time. Outcomes, all about the news item: judgement accuracy, truth discernment, sharing intention and sharing discernment. Nothing about the expert was measured; the sentence that participants "lowered their trust in the verified expert" is the authors' reading of a regression coefficient. Results: an accurate indicator improved judgement and cut sharing of false items against both peer and expert opinion, most when it disagreed with the expert. In Experiment 3, wrong predictions were followed: a wrong flag pushed judgements the wrong way (truth discernment d = 1.09 with the weak AI), and when the better AI marked a true headline as false, people became less willing to share it (d = 0.27). The authors conclude that "people lack the capability to differentiate correct and incorrect AI predictions, regardless of the level of performance of the AI". In the exploratory regressions the AI's effect on accuracy was larger than the expert's in all three experiments, though a verified expert weighed more than a self-claimed one. The authors call for tests in domains "where experts are generally perceived as significantly more or less credible than those in health".

**Consequence for our study.** First, the novelty claim survives in the form the reviewers already proposed after Kim et al. (2026): their expert was a vote, their outcome was belief in the claim, their expert never answered, and nobody held power; ours is a wrong flag on a correct expert, a checker held by the more powerful party, the expert's answer as the repair step, and trust in the expert rated at each step. Second, Oliver's assumption becomes a stated prediction with a moderator: the flag will be believed and will lower trust (H1), and more so for participants with low self-rated cybersecurity knowledge, which Abstract v7 already collects; report H1 split by that rating. Third, persistence: this paper is one-shot and does not test whether the effect of a wrong flag survives a correction. Our after-answer ratings do, and the prediction to state is that, for low-knowledge participants, trust after the reasoned answer stays below baseline, with the gap shrinking for those who rate their knowledge higher. That is the "persisting negative view" written as a test rather than an assumption, and it is where H2's "above the start" is least likely to hold. Fourth, interpretation: if trust falls after a flag that the participant cannot evaluate, the fall is a measure of default belief in the checker, not of the CISO's slide; say so when reading the H1 results, and cite this paper and Horne and Nevo (2025) as the reason the default is belief.

**Open.** Decide whether self-rated cybersecurity knowledge is a planned moderator for H1 and H2 (recommended) or exploratory. If the review needs the AI-versus-expert numbers, Table 3 has the regression coefficients.

## 17. Loconte, Monaro, Pietrini, Verschuere & Kleinberg (2026), Computers in Human Behavior. Humans incorrectly reject confident accusatory AI judgments. (read 2026-10-19)

**Oliver's reading.** A very recent and good paper for the study design: the simulated LLM messages in the checker must not go to extremes, such as calling the CISO a liar. At the same time, our participants will not know whether the AI is highly accurate or not, because we will not show the model itself.

**What the full text says.** A pre-registered online experiment on Prolific: 493 recruited, 373 kept after attention checks (mean age 39, general population). Design 2 x 5 x 2: the model's overall accuracy (54 or 89 percent, between subjects, and told to participants on every screen; the shown figure matched the model's real hit rate), the model's confidence on each statement (five levels, within subjects, shown as a slider from -50 to +50), and the model's verdict (truthful or deceptive, within subjects). Participants read ten written autobiographical statements (hospital stays, car accidents, missed deadlines), each with a stated motive to lie, saw the AI's verdict and confidence, and gave their own verdict on an identical slider. The accused was the anonymous author of a text; nobody was rated as a person and nobody could answer. Results: participants followed the 89 percent model more than the 54 percent one, but the more confident the model was, the more they moved away from it, most when it called a statement a lie, and they moved toward "truthful"; when the model confidently called a statement truthful they moved toward "deceptive". Their adjustments made the combined accuracy worse or no better. Participants overrated human lie-detection ability. The authors set this against von Schenk et al. (2024), which is in Abstract v7: there, having a lie detector available raised the accusation rate; here, "a transparency layer on the model's performance and uncertainty can discourage users from blindly following an accusatory AI judgment". Limits the authors give: isolated written statements, the AI always present, and a call for "live interviews or other settings in which individuals engage directly with a communication partner".

**Consequence for our study.** First, Oliver's wording rule: every flag is a competence-type observation about the slide ("Numbers do not add up", "Architecture likely wrong"), never a statement about the CISO's honesty or intent, and never at the extreme ("definitely false"); the resistance in this paper was strongest for confident accusations of lying. Second, the display rule, and its consequence: the resistance here was produced by a visible accuracy figure and a confidence slider. We show neither, so our checker sits on the von Schenk side of the pair, where the tool is opaque and people follow it; that supports rule 19's default-belief prediction and should be said in the review as a boundary condition: if the checker showed confidence, the Loconte effect could appear. Third, because participants are told nothing about accuracy, they bring their own prior about LLMs; Abstract v7 already records own AI use, which is the proxy for that prior and should be reported next to self-rated cybersecurity knowledge as a moderator of H1. Fourth, the paper's call for settings where people "engage directly with a communication partner" is our design in one phrase. Fifth, for the review it is the counter-result to H1 with a stated boundary: observers resist a confident accusation of lying delivered with its accuracy on screen; our flags are competence-type, opaque and about a slide.

**Open.** Write the flag texts and check each against the two wording limits (no honesty, no extreme). Decide whether a short post-session question on how accurate the participant assumed the checker to be is allowed under rule 10; it would replace own AI use as the proxy.

## 18. Horne & Nevo (2025), Scientific Reports. People adhere to content warning labels even when they are wrong. (read 2026-10-19)

**Oliver's reading.** The last one to add. It correlates: the warning label is very similar to what the checker will do, less precise, but the results can be compared with this study. It also connects the LLM topic with false positives and false negatives, since our checker adds hints that something could be more precise or might be wrong. Politics is not a good reference for our situation, but this paper's point does not rest on politics.

**What the full text says.** Three between-subjects experiments on Prolific with US residents, 1,313 participants in all, collected from October 2023, framed as the move from human fact-checkers to AI labelling. Study 1 (n = 366; 100 control, 123 correct labels, 116 wrong labels; ten headlines each): in the correct condition every false headline carried a label and no true one did; in the error condition every true headline carried a label and no false one did. Every label read "Warning: An AI Tool Says This Story is False". Headlines came from recent fact-checks and from Reuters, AP and NPR, on any topic, "not limited to politics or health"; no source was shown; nobody was told the labels could be wrong and there was no training. Trust per headline on a five-point scale mapped to -1 to +1. A wrong label on a true headline cut trust by 0.30 against control; a correct label on a false headline cut it by 0.17; unlabelled headlines did not differ from control. The authors note the wrong-label effect was "nearly double" the correct-label effect, partly a floor effect, since true headlines start with more room to fall. Study 2: a habituation feed of 50 headlines where labels were mostly right or mostly wrong, then an evaluation phase in which every true headline was mislabelled; those habituated to mostly correct labels trusted the mislabelled true headlines less (a small effect). People learn the label heuristic from exposure. Study 3 used signal detection: sensitivity (d') was 0.76 with correct labels, 0.44 with none, 0.25 with random labels and -0.09 with all-wrong labels, which is worse than chance; wrong labels cost more (0.53) than correct labels gained (0.32). The discussion names a "rare failure effect": "the harm from a highly accurate system's infrequent errors may be as bad as the harm from a less accurate system with more errors", and says adaptation to an error-prone labeller takes exposure. Trust in the label was the outcome; no person was rated and nothing could answer.

**Consequence for our study.** First, this is the closest numerical analogue to H1: an opaque AI label, no accuracy stated, no training, on a true item, and trust falls. Their 0.30 on a two-point range is about 1.5 points on our 0-to-10 rating, next to the 1.8 from Du et al. (paper 15); both go into the power estimate and both are grades or content trust, not trust in a person, so the review says they bound the expectation rather than set it. Second, the comparison Oliver wants is a planned analysis: our drop after a wrong flag on a correct slide against their wrong-label effect, and our change after "No issues found" against their supporting labels in Study 3, which raised discernment; rule 16 already predicts a small rise. Third, the rare failure effect is our setting in one phrase: three wrong flags in a ten-minute briefing fall inside the window before anyone adapts, so participants cannot learn that the checker is wrong except through the CISO's answer. That is the mechanism behind rule 19's "persists" and should be stated as a design assumption, with the CISO's reasoned answer as the only corrective signal. Fourth, their asymmetry warns about floors and ceilings: a wrong label hurt more partly because true items had room to fall; our baseline must sit mid-scale (rule 15) so that both the fall and the recovery can show. Fifth, the paper answers the politics reservation: the headlines were from any topic and the effect did not depend on the topic.

**Open.** Convert the two benchmarks (1.5 and 1.8 points) into the sample size for H1 once the design is final. Decide whether slides 1 and 5 should say "No issues found" or carry a supporting label of the paper's kind ("Checked: consistent with the figures"), which is the stronger version of rule 16.

## Design rules collected so far

1. Take the three-part trust rating (competence, benevolence, integrity) from METI's subscales, one item each, not the fourteen-pair inventory. Results then compare with Kim et al. (2026) in construct, not in instrument. (Papers 1 and 4)
2. Word H3 as "a denial without a reason restores less trust than a reasoned answer". A bare denial is not the denial Kim et al. (2004) tested. (Paper 2)
3. The CISO's reasoned answer must always give more inputs and more proposals than the checker's flag; only the denial condition offers less. (Paper 3)
4. Our flags should make the CISO look less careful than the checker, which is the direction that moved trust in Madanay et al. (2025); never the reverse. (Paper 3)
5. State in the review what each closest design lacks that we add: wrong flags, the expert's answer, the listener's power. (Papers 1 to 3)
6. Keep every rating point to four quick responses: 0-to-10 trust plus the three items. Participants should react, not deliberate. Run the study first with the Abstract v7 measures; the full METI, if ever, once at the end. (Paper 4)
7. Describe power in two parts: the board holds formal power (budget, oversight) and the CISO holds informational power (what reaches the board). Frame the checker as giving the board member an external, face-saving reason to ask; that is the mechanism behind H4a. (Paper 5)
8. Match the reasoned answer and the bare denial on delivery (same speaker, tone and confidence), so that the presence of a reason is the only difference. Non-expert boards judge the presenter's credibility, not the substance, so delivery would otherwise confound H2 and H3. (Paper 8)
9. Ask once, at the end of the session, whether the participant believes CISOs shade their reports to boards. Report it as a moderator of H1. A second exploratory item on the participant's confidence in their own questions is optional. (Paper 8)
10. Scope: the study is about human-to-human trust when one human is augmented with an AI fact-checker. Trust in the AI is not measured. This closes the attribution question raised in the topic map (M5): the outcome is trust in the CISO only. (Oliver, on paper 9)
11. Plan a comparison with Ferrin et al. (2007): our reasoned answer against bare denial should reproduce their best reply against reticence (competence condition: gaps of 0.7 to 1.1 on a 7-point scale, d about 0.5 to 0.9). Use the neutral group to separate the flag's credibility from the answer's quality. If the ordering and gaps match, the board member treated an AI flag as at least as credible as unverified hearsay. (Paper 10)
12. Analyse the three trust items separately and predict in advance that the CISO's answer moves the integrity item and the overall rating, not the competence item; in Ferrin et al. (2007) no reply moved perceived competence. (Paper 10)
13. Treat the budget decision as Mayer et al.'s risk taking in the relationship: an act taken when trust exceeds the perceived risk of the act. Keep the stakes of the decision identical across groups and visible on the slide, and report the decision by group as the test of whether a change in trust reaches an act. (Paper 11)
14. In a first meeting the baseline rating is propensity to trust, and integrity and competence carry the judgement while benevolence stays near flat (Mayer et al., Propositions 1, 3 and 4). State this in advance so a flat benevolence item is a prediction, not a surprise. (Papers 10 and 11)
15. Pitch the CISO's delivery in the middle, plain and competent without charisma, identical in every condition, and pilot the video so the baseline rating lands mid-scale with room to move both ways. A CISO who seems "on top of their game" leaves the board "done with the issue"; one who cannot present gets "blank stares and no follow-up questions". Either end flattens H1 or H4a for the wrong reason. (Paper 13, extends rule 8)
16. Predict a small rise in trust after "No issues found" in the checker group (green-dashboard complacency), and test it at slide 1 against the neutral group. (Paper 13)
17. Fix the flag panel's presentation before the pilot and hold it identical across every flag: a red flag with the flagged slide region marked on flagged slides, a calm green "No issues found" on slides 1 and 5. Red highlighting makes a flag seem more credible, enlarges the drop and raises the urge to act, so H1 and H4a are measured at their upper end; say so, and make no claim about colour alone, since colour and wording are confounded in one panel. (Paper 15, with 13)
18. Use the warning-only drop in Du et al. (2026), 1.8 points on a 10-point scale for identical work, as the benchmark for H1's expected size in the power estimate, with the caveat that it is a grade, not trust in a person. (Paper 15)
19. Treat self-rated cybersecurity knowledge as a planned moderator of H1 and H2, and state the prediction in advance: participants with low knowledge believe the checker by default, so their trust falls more after a flag and stays below baseline after the reasoned answer; the gap shrinks with higher knowledge. When reading H1, say that a fall after an unevaluable flag measures default belief in the checker, not the slide. (Paper 16, with 15 and 18)
20. Flag wording and display: every flag is a competence-type observation about the slide, never about the CISO's honesty or intent, and never at the extreme. The checker shows no accuracy figure and no confidence score. State in the review that this is the opaque condition in which people follow the tool (von Schenk et al., 2024), and that a visible confidence display could produce the resistance Loconte et al. (2026) found. Report own AI use beside self-rated cybersecurity knowledge as the proxy for the participant's assumed accuracy. (Paper 17, with 19)
21. Plan two comparisons with Horne and Nevo (2025): the drop after a wrong flag on a correct slide against their wrong-label effect (0.30 on a two-point range, about 1.5 points on 0-to-10), and the change after "No issues found" against their supporting labels. Use 1.5 (Horne and Nevo) and 1.8 (Du et al.) as the bounds for H1 in the power estimate. State the rare failure effect as a design assumption: three wrong flags in ten minutes fall before anyone adapts, so the CISO's answer is the only corrective signal. (Paper 18, with 15, 16 and 19)
