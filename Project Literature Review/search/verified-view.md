## 1. Kim, Daneshi, Draper & Sreedharan (2026), AIES. Asymmetric trust effects of corrective AI in expert advisory workflows under epistemic dependence. (read 2026-10-18)

**Oliver's reading.** Highly connected to what we do, although it is more workflow-related. It uses the METI scale.

**What the full text adds.** The connection is in the roles: a non-expert who cannot verify the advice, a human expert, and an AI that monitors and flags the expert, with trust in the expert as the outcome. The workflow focus is the manipulation: the AI is absent, invoked by the advisor, or monitors automatically, and that factor changed nothing. What moved trust was whether the advisor had erred. The AI was always right, the advisor never replied, and the student held no power over the advisor. Trust was measured with METI (expertise, integrity, benevolence), an entity trust scale, a 100-point rating and intention to reuse the advisor. The authors name the AI's constant correctness as a limitation and call for work that varies AI uncertainty or failure.

**Consequence for our study.** Three things make ours different and should be said in the review: the flags are wrong, the expert answers, and the listener holds the power. METI is the measure to adopt for the three-part trust rating (competence, benevolence, integrity), which also makes our results comparable with theirs. Their null for "how the AI is involved" cuts against H4b.

**Open.** Pull the cell means for the no-error monitoring condition to see whether the checker's presence alone moved trust (bears on our "No issues found" slides).

## 2. Kim, Ferrin, Cooper & Dirks (2004), J. Applied Psychology. Removing the shadow of suspicion: Apology versus denial. (read 2026-10-18)

**Oliver's reading.** The paper seemed to be about CEO pay or trust; the connection to our study is only in the finding that a denial of wrongdoing followed by evidence of innocence will probably not damage trust.

**What the full text says.** The paper is not about CEO pay. Participants acted as a hiring manager and watched a videotaped interview of a job applicant for an accounting position who was accused of a past violation: filing an incorrect tax return through lack of knowledge (competence) or on purpose (integrity). The candidate apologised or denied. Study 1 (N = 200) found apology repaired trust better after the competence violation and denial after the integrity violation, on perceived integrity and on the intentions (willingness to risk, hiring, job responsibilities), not on perceived competence. Study 2 (N = 444) added a memo from HR reporting a state board ruling of guilty or innocent. Evidence of innocence raised every measure, and a response that did not fit the later evidence cost trust: apology followed by innocence did worse than denial followed by innocence. Oliver's takeaway is correct for that part. Two details matter for us: their denial was not bare (the candidate denied, blamed office politics at the previous firm, and gave an assurance), and the innocence came from a third party, not from the accused.

**Consequence for our study.** Our "denial with no reason" is weaker than their denial, which carried an account. The paper therefore does not predict that a bare denial repairs trust; it predicts that an innocent party who denies with an account does better than one who apologises. H3 should be worded as "a denial without a reason restores less trust than a reasoned answer", and Ferrin et al. (2007) on reticence is the closer test of a reply with no information. The paper also shows why our participants never learn that the CISO was right: in their design the clearing came from a board ruling, and in ours the CISO's reason has to do that work alone.

**Open.** None on the design. Table 6 (p. 113) has the cell means if a number is needed.

## 3. Madanay, O'Donohue & Zikmund-Fisher (2025), JMIR. Patient reactions to AI–clinician discrepancies. (read 2026-10-18)

**Oliver's reading.** It crosses an interesting point: the penalty appeared only when the human's advice fell short of the AI's. In our case, even if the checker lists an issue, the outcome should differ. We need to design the study so that we do not prove the same thing, and make sure the human always has more inputs than the AI and more suggestions than the AI gives.

**What the full text says.** 1,606 US adults imagined a lung-cancer screening CT. The radiologist who recommended less testing than the AI was agreed with less (mean 4.01 against 4.55 to 4.63), was least likely to be recommended to family and friends, and was rated the worst provider. The radiologist who recommended more testing than the AI paid no penalty. Patients who prefer aggressive care sided with the radiologist who overcalled and against the one who undercalled. The AI never explained itself and the radiologist never answered.

**Consequence for our study.** Oliver's design rule: the CISO's answer must always contain more than the flag. A flag is one line ("Numbers do not add up"); the reasoned answer should give the company's reason, the figure the checker misread, and what the CISO already did about it, so that the human visibly exceeds the AI in inputs and in proposals. The denial condition is then the only case where the CISO offers less than the checker, which is the contrast H3 needs. Second rule from the asymmetry: a flag that makes the CISO look less careful than the checker ("Architecture likely wrong") is the Madanay direction and should move trust; a flag that makes the CISO look more careful than needed would not, and we do not use one. Say in the review that Madanay's observers never heard the expert answer, which is what we add.

**Open.** Check whether the published version reports the three outcome measures separately by condition, for the review's numbers.

## Design rules collected so far

1. Adopt METI (expertise or competence, integrity, benevolence) for the three-part trust rating, so results compare with Kim et al. (2026). (Paper 1)
2. Word H3 as "a denial without a reason restores less trust than a reasoned answer". A bare denial is not the denial Kim et al. (2004) tested. (Paper 2)
3. The CISO's reasoned answer must always give more inputs and more proposals than the checker's flag; only the denial condition offers less. (Paper 3)
4. Our flags should make the CISO look less careful than the checker, which is the direction that moved trust in Madanay et al. (2025); never the reverse. (Paper 3)
5. State in the review what each closest design lacks that we add: wrong flags, the expert's answer, the listener's power. (Papers 1 to 3)
