# Novelty review B: Abstract v7 (VLM checker, board member, CISO)

Reviewer B, 2026-10-19. Written alone; reviews A and C were not opened.

## What the abstract claims to add (step 1, my own words)

A lay person who holds power over an expert (a board member deciding a CISO's budget) runs an AI slide checker during the expert's live briefing. Every flag the checker raises is wrong for this company, though plausible "by the textbook". After each flag the expert either gives the company-specific reason or a bare denial. Trust in the expert (0 to 10) is rated at baseline, after each flagged slide and after each answer, and compared with a neutral group that sees the same slides and hears the same answers without flags. The claimed additions over the cited work are: the checker is wrong rather than right, it is in the hands of the powerful lay listener rather than the expert, the expert gets to answer, and trust is tracked step by step instead of once at the end.

## A. Preliminary verdict (written after step 3, before reading project notes; not changed afterwards)

Preliminary verdict: (b), novel only if the claim is narrowed.

Reasoning from the papers alone:

- No paper I read combines the four elements. The nearest AI-plus-expert studies (Kim et al. 2026 AIES; Madanay et al. 2025 JMIR; Chen et al. 2026 per references/reviews/chen.md) all let the AI challenge or correct an expert in front of a lay recipient, but in all three the recipient does not hold the tool, the expert never answers the AI, and trust is measured once, between subjects. In Kim et al. 2026 the AI flags are correct (the advisor really erred; "all such errors were corrected before the final schedule", Section 2 and 4.1). In Madanay et al. and Chen et al. the ground truth of the AI's divergent opinion is left undefined; neither study tells the participant that the AI was wrong.
- Each element taken separately has precedent:
  - Wrong AI flags: Horne and Nevo 2025 (false warning labels on true headlines, trust in the headline drops), Gupta et al. 2026 (one deliberate false positive per video in a real-time wearable fact-checker; participants became more sceptical of true claims), Lu et al. 2025 Experiment 3 (AI credibility indicator at 55 percent accuracy, contradicting a correct "expert" judgement in some cells). None of these measures trust in the person who made the claim; outcomes are belief in, or veracity judgements of, the content.
  - Powerful evaluator holding the tool: Du et al. 2026 (teachers, who grade, receive a fictitious AI-detection report on an unchanged student paper; a high score lowered quality ratings), von Schenk et al. 2024 (accuser consults a lie detector). But here the evaluator is the domain expert and the checked person the lay one, the reverse of v7.
  - Accused party answers, reasoned versus other responses: Kim et al. 2004 (hiring manager role; applicant apologises or denies, with a reason, "bad office politics"; Study 2 adds later evidence of innocence) and Ferrin et al. 2007 (adds reticence). This is the trust-repair paradigm almost exactly, including the power relation (participant decides on hiring). The accuser is a human reference, not an AI, and the denial in Kim 2004 carries an explanation, so v7's "reason versus bare denial" is a contrast inside the denial family that these papers did not run, but it is a small step from them.
  - Repeated trust ratings in one session: Greitemeyer and Sagioglou 2015 and Mickelberg et al. 2025 (rated before and after clearing), Kim 2004 pilot (before and after the allegation).
  - Fact-checking a person and measuring evaluation of that person: Prike et al. 2025 (human checker, correct), Chae and Tewksbury 2026 (AI-labelled fact-checks of Biden and Trump, correct; no effect on feeling thermometer or competence).
- Board and CISO: Lowry et al. 2026, Opdenbusch et al. 2025, Proudfoot et al. 2023 and Aamir et al. 2026 are field or interview studies. They document the power imbalance and that non-expert directors fear asking questions, but none tests an intervention and none involves AI tools. So no experiment in this setting exists in what I read.

Preliminary conclusion: the study is a new combination of known paradigms (AI-versus-expert discrepancy, false-positive labels, trust repair after accusation) moved into a new setting. What is new is narrow: (1) an AI flag that is known to be wrong, aimed at a correct expert, with trust in that expert as the outcome; (2) the expert's own reply to the AI flag as the manipulated repair step; (3) the lay, powerful listener as the one who holds and can voice the checker. Claims that "AI checking erodes trust in experts" or that "a reasoned answer restores trust" are not new on their own; Kim 2026, Madanay 2025 and Chen 2026 cover the first and Kim 2004 and Ferrin 2007 cover the second. The abstract as written does not cite Kim 2026, Madanay 2025, Kim 2004 or Ferrin 2007, and its claim that Chen et al. 2026 is "the closest design" is no longer correct on my reading; Kim et al. 2026 is at least as close.

## B. Final answers

### What changed after reading the project notes and searching (step 4 and 5)

The project's own gap statement ("Literature Review - Candidate papers.md", section "The closest designs and the gap") matches my preliminary verdict in substance, and I could not find a paper that breaks it. Three things in the notes sharpen or qualify it, and the project underweights them:

1. **The expert's stated reason has already been manipulated, in the AI setting.** Pezzo et al. (2022, Medical Decision Making; results-4, Sub-theme A) varied the physician's stated reason for not consulting a diagnostic aid and found the reason changed competence ratings ("I knew what it would say" helped, "I don't trust it" hurt). Only one selection reviewer picked it. It is the nearest published precedent for E3 with an AI in the loop, although the aid there never accused the physician and the reason was given in a vignette, not in reply to a flag.
2. **"Wrong" is invisible to the participant.** Every flag in v7 is wrong for the company, but the participant cannot know this until the debrief; during the session it is an unresolved accusation followed by an account. From the participant's side, the manipulation is therefore "an unverified AI allegation, then the accused's account or bare denial", which is Kim et al. (2004, Study 1) with an AI as the source of the allegation. Kim 2004's denial itself carried an account ("bad office politics at the previous firm", p. 108, Manipulations). The project's verified-view.md (paper 10 entry, line 95 to 97) reached the same reading for Ferrin 2007. The true novelty of E1 is therefore the source (an AI that is fallible in a known, textbook-versus-context way), not the wrongness as experienced.
3. **Within-session repeated measurement is more covered than the abstract implies.** Dobbs et al. (2026) rated a doctor's credibility before and after a correction; Nurek and Kostopoulou (2023) rated satisfaction with a doctor at two time points; Kim et al. (2026, Section 3.1) cite Han and Ko (2025) on trust that drops at an error and is partly repaired by later corrective interaction. Repeated ratings alone are not a contribution.

The notes also contain one claim I would test rather than adopt: the Candidate papers file lists Lu et al. (2025) as showing "AI indicator weighs more when it disagrees with an expert". That is true for news veracity judgements, but Lu et al. never measured trust in the expert (I found no such measure in the full text), so it is not evidence on E1 for trust in a person.

The web search (section C) found one relevant paper the project has not seen (Q7) and no study that combines E1 to E4. The search result confirms but does not prove absence: web search is weak on 2026 conference proceedings.

### Q1. Verdict: (b) novel only if the claim is narrowed

The abstract as written frames the question as "how does trust in the CISO change when a board member uses a VLM checker". That broad question is already answered in direction by Kim et al. (2026), Madanay et al. (2025), Chen et al. (2026), Du et al. (2026), Dobbs et al. (2026) and Hendriks et al. (2016, per the project notes): an AI or other check that contradicts an expert lowers lay evaluation of that expert. The repair half (H2, H3) is the trust-repair paradigm of Kim et al. (2004) and Ferrin et al. (2007), whose participants also held power (hiring manager) over the accused. The abstract also still calls Chen et al. (2026) "the closest design" and does not cite Kim et al. (2026), Madanay et al. (2025), Kim et al. (2004), Ferrin et al. (2007) or Pezzo et al. (2022); a reviewer who knows any of them will read the novelty claim as overstated. What no paper does is put the pieces together: an AI flag, fallible in a stated way, raised by the lay party who holds both the tool and the power, followed by the expert's own answer to that flag, with trust tracked across flag and answer. That combination, and specifically the expert's reply to an AI flag as the manipulated repair step, is new. So the study is novel, but only as a combination and as "repair of trust after an AI flag", not as "AI checking affects trust in experts".

### Q2. Element by element

- **E1 (wrong AI flags on a correct expert): partly.** Wrong AI flags are well studied, but on content: Horne and Nevo (2025) wrong warning labels lowered trust in true headlines; Gupta et al. (2026) inserted one false positive per video in a wearable fact-checker and participants grew more sceptical of the true claim; He, Horne and Nevo (2026, results-6) a wrong LLM "false" verdict cost a true headline about one point on a 5-point trust scale; Lu et al. (2025) Experiment 3 had a 55 percent accurate AI that sometimes contradicted a correct "expert" vote, outcome veracity accuracy. On a human expert as the target: Madanay et al. (2025) and Chen et al. (2026) contradict the expert but leave the AI's correctness undefined; Kim et al. (2026) flag real advisor errors (AI correct); Song et al. (2026, medRxiv, results-4) include AI-wrong cells and found lower liability when the AI was wrong, but outcome is legal liability after a bad outcome. No study measures trust in a correct expert after an AI flag known to the experimenter to be wrong; and, as noted, participants in v7 cannot know either.
- **E2 (checker held by the powerful non-expert): partly.** The listener holds the checker in Gupta et al. (2026) and Danry et al. (2020), but nobody has power over the speaker and trust in the speaker is not measured. The power holder holds the flag in Du et al. (2026) (teachers grading an AI-flagged paper) and von Schenk et al. (2024) (accuser consulting a lie detector), but there the holder is the more expert party or a peer, not a non-expert over an expert. Kim et al. (2004) gives the participant power (hiring) but no AI. Chen et al. (2026) and Kim et al. (2026) give the AI to the expert or to the system, not to the lay party. Chen, Li, Meng, Qiu and Yang (2026, arXiv, new, Q7) give patients chatbot access before a physician visit in a field experiment and find lower compliance and satisfaction and a shift of authority, but patients there do not hold power and there is no flag of a specific statement. The exact configuration is not covered.
- **E3 (the expert answers, reasoned answer versus bare denial, as repair): partly.** Kim et al. (2004) apology versus denial-with-account, and Ferrin et al. (2007) adding reticence, are the paradigm, with a human hearsay allegation. Pezzo et al. (2022) varied the physician's stated reason for setting an AI aid aside. Schiff et al. (2025) varied politicians' responses (claiming misinformation, silence, apology) to scandal stories. No study has the expert answer an AI's flag, and none contrasts a reasoned denial with a bare denial (Kim 2004's denial had an account; Ferrin's reticence took no position). Chen et al. (2026, Section 5.1, per reviews/chen.md) explicitly name this as untested.
- **E4 (trust before, after the flag, after the answer, same session): partly.** Kim et al. (2004) pilot measured before and after the allegation; Greitemeyer and Sagioglou (2015) and Mickelberg et al. (2025) after accusation and after clearing; Dobbs et al. (2026) before and after correction; Nurek and Kostopoulou (2023) at two time points. Kim et al. (2004) main studies, Ferrin et al. (2007), Chen, Madanay and Kim 2026 all measure once. Three-point tracking (baseline, flag, answer) repeated over several slides in one session, with an AI flag, is not covered, but the method is standard.
- **E5 (E1 to E4 together): not covered.** No paper read, listed in the 129-entry index, or found in the web search combines them.
- **E6 (board member and CISO): not covered** as an experiment. Lowry et al. (2026), Opdenbusch et al. (2025), Proudfoot et al. (2023), Aamir et al. (2026) and Da Silva and Jensen (2022) are field, interview or design studies of the relationship; none mentions AI checkers (Opdenbusch and Lowry have no AI content in the body; Aamir mentions AI oversight disclosures only). Note that a setting is a weak contribution on its own: the board is the motivating case, while the participants will be lay adults role-playing a director, so the study's evidence is about a lay power holder in a simulated briefing, not about boards.

### Q3. Closest single study

Kim, Daneshi, Draper and Sreedharan (2026, AIES; `Kim-2026-asymmetric-trust-corrective-AI-AIES.pdf`). It is the only study I read in which an AI visibly flags a human expert's work in front of the lay person who depends on that expert, with automatic monitoring ("reactive") as one condition, and with multidimensional trust in the expert (METI expertise, integrity, benevolence, plus a 100-point global trust rating and reuse intention) as the outcome. It found advisor errors lowered every trust measure and the AI's involvement did not change the penalty (Sections 2.1 and 5). What separates it from v7: (1) its AI flags were correct, the advisor really erred (Section 4.1, "all such errors were corrected before the final schedule"); v7's flags are wrong for the company; (2) the AI belonged to the advisory workflow, not to the user; the student never held or invoked it, and held no power over the advisor; (3) the advisor never answered the AI, he simply corrected the schedule; (4) one between-subjects measurement after the task (Section 4.1), not repeated ratings; (5) low-stakes course advising. Chen et al. (2026) is the runner-up: closer on "AI disagrees and the expert says nothing", further on everything else.

### Q4. Strongest case against novelty (hostile reviewer, 150 words max)

This recombines three mature paradigms, each with stronger designs. That AI contradiction lowers lay trust in an expert is shown by Madanay et al. (N = 1,606), Kim et al. 2026 and Chen et al. 2026; that wrong labels are followed by Horne and Nevo and Gupta et al.; that an accused party's account repairs trust, and that its effect depends on whether the allegation is true, by Kim et al. 2004 and Ferrin et al. 2007, with participants already holding hiring power. Participants never learn the flags are wrong, so "wrong flag" reduces to "unverified allegation from an AI", one source swap away from Kim 2004. The board setting is cosmetic: lay volunteers will role-play directors. With 40 unpaid participants, non-random allocation and groups that differ in who asks, the study cannot separate the claimed new mechanism from known ones. The abstract omits the closest prior work.

### Q5. Strongest case for novelty (150 words max)

Every prior study of AI contradicting an expert stops at the contradiction: the expert never answers (Chen 2026, Madanay 2025, Kim 2026), and every trust-repair study uses a human accuser (Kim 2004, Ferrin 2007). Yet the situation now spreading through products, a listener pointing a camera or meeting copilot at a speaker, is precisely one where the accused expert is present and must reply on the spot, and where the tool belongs to the person with power over the expert, not to the expert. No study measures what that reply does after an AI flag, or whether a fallible AI flag shifts the burden of proof (Kempt and Nagel 2022; Chen 2026 Section 5.1 names this as untested) so that the same answer moves trust more than without a flag. The step-by-step design isolates flag and answer within the same person, which between-subjects studies cannot.

### Q6. What the abstract should claim

Suggested contribution: "Prior work shows that an AI that contradicts an expert lowers lay trust in that expert, but in those studies the AI belonged to the expert's own workflow and the expert never replied. We test the reverse arrangement now offered by camera and meeting assistants: the less expert party who holds power over the expert runs the checker, the checker's flags are plausible but wrong for the context, and the expert answers each flag, so that we can measure how much of the loss of trust a reasoned answer, as against a bare denial, recovers." In the related work, Kim et al. (2026) should replace Chen et al. (2026) as "the closest design", and Kim et al. (2004), Ferrin et al. (2007) and Pezzo et al. (2022) should be cited as the repair precedent the study extends to an AI accuser. H2's "above the initial score" should be framed as exploratory, since a gain beyond baseline would be the genuinely surprising result.

### Q7. Web search findings the project has not seen

- **Chen, Y., Li, H., Meng, L., Qiu, X., & Yang, Q. (2026). Directional AI advice: Experimental evidence from healthcare (arXiv:2607.08706).** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.08706 . Preregistered field experiment at a Chinese hospital; patients randomized to chatbot access before outpatient visits; prescriptions fell, testing rose, and survey results show lower compliance and satisfaction with a shift of authority from physicians to patients. Not found anywhere in the project files. Relevance: the lay party holds the AI before meeting the expert (E2 adjacent, real field setting), but no flag of a specific statement, no reply measured, trust not named in the abstract. Worth one sentence in the review as field evidence that lay-held AI shifts authority away from experts.
- **Li, Li and Kuang (2026), "Bridge or barrier?", Internet Research,** https://doi.org/10.1108/INTR-09-2025-1539 . The project has metadata only (trust-topic-map 2.6). The abstract (Emerald) shows it is a cross-sectional survey of 356 Chinese patients analysed with PLS-SEM, not an experiment; it does not threaten novelty.
- Searches on presenter, teacher, advisor, board and repair-after-AI-accusation designs found nothing that combines a lay-held AI flag with the expert's reply. Tangential: a FAccT 2024 paper on repairing trust in AI systems after errors (denial least effective; https://facctconference.org/static/papers24/facct24-39.pdf), where the trustee is the AI, not a human.

### Q8. Confidence: medium

The verdict rests on the absence of any study in which an expert answers an AI flag in front of an evaluator; one such study (most likely in medical-liability work by the Bernstein and Baird group, in CSCW or CHI 2026 proceedings, or in Hendriks et al. 2016, which I could not read) would move the verdict towards (c), while a full-text check of Pezzo et al. (2022) showing its reasons were not replies to the aid would raise my confidence in (b).
