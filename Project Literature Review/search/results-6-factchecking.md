## A. Anchors: what fact-checks do on average, and what persists

### Walter, N., Cohen, J., Holbert, R. L., & Morag, Y. (2020). Fact-checking: A meta-analysis of what works and for whom. *Political Communication*, *37*(3), 350–375. https://doi.org/10.1080/10584609.2019.1668894
- **Type:** communication
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1080/10584609.2019.1668894
- **Design:** Meta-analysis of 30 studies with 20,963 participants testing fact-checks of political misinformation; outcome is belief in the claim.
- **Finding:** Fact-checks move political beliefs toward the truth, with a modest average effect (d = 0.29). The effect weakens when the fact-check uses a truth scale (for example "half true"), refutes only part of a claim, or concerns campaign statements, and it is much weaker for people whose prior beliefs, ideology or knowledge favour the claim.
- **Relation:** Anchor for the corrections literature. Shows the outcome studied is belief in the claim, not trust in the speaker. Our study measures trust in the person who is flagged, and the flag is wrong, which this literature does not cover.
- **Access:** SFU library
- **Score:** 1=2 2=1 3=2 4=4 5=3 total=12/25

### Thorson, E. (2016). Belief echoes: The persistent effects of corrected misinformation. *Political Communication*, *33*(3), 460–480. https://doi.org/10.1080/10584609.2015.1102187
- **Type:** communication
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1080/10584609.2015.1102187
- **Design:** Three experiments in which participants read negative information about a political candidate that was then corrected (in some conditions immediately); attitudes toward the candidate were measured afterwards.
- **Finding:** Negative information kept lowering attitudes toward the candidate after it had been effectively discredited and participants accepted the correction ("belief echoes"). This held even when the correction came immediately, the journalistic gold standard. The echoes can arise by an automatic or a deliberate route.
- **Relation:** Speaker evaluation after a correction; grounds H1 and the limit of H2: a flag that is later answered may still leave a residue on trust in the CISO. Our flag is wrong and the answer comes from the flagged person, not from a third party.
- **Access:** open access https://repository.upenn.edu/bitstreams/fde2b15d-38dd-4d96-9205-6ca7bfb356e2/download
- **Score:** 1=4 2=0 3=2 4=4 5=3 total=13/25

## B. What a fact-check does to evaluation of the checked speaker

### Swire, B., Berinsky, A. J., Lewandowsky, S., & Ecker, U. K. H. (2017). Processing political misinformation: Comprehending the Trump phenomenon. *Royal Society Open Science*, *4*(3), 160802. https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.160802
- **Type:** psychology
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1098/rsos.160802
- **Design:** Two experiments with US adults before the 2016 election. Participants rated belief in true and false statements Trump made, then saw affirmations and retractions; belief was re-rated immediately or after a delay. Experiment 1 varied attribution to Trump; Experiment 2 varied the source of the explanation (own party versus opposing party). Voting preference was also measured.
- **Finding:** Attributing a statement to Trump raised belief among his supporters and lowered it among Democrats. Trump supporters reduced their belief in the false statements after correction but did not change their voting preference. The source of the explanation mattered little; belief updating depended mainly on how credible people found the person who made the original claim.
- **Relation:** Speaker evaluation; the classic "beliefs change, support does not" result, a counter-result to H1. Our speaker is a professional expert judged by a board member, not a partisan figure judged by supporters.
- **Access:** open access https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.160802
- **Score:** 1=4 2=0 3=2 4=4 5=4 total=14/25

### Aird, M. J., Ecker, U. K. H., Swire, B., Berinsky, A. J., & Lewandowsky, S. (2018). Does truth matter to voters? The effects of correcting political misinformation in an Australian sample. *Royal Society Open Science*, *5*(12), 180593. https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.180593
- **Type:** psychology
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1098/rsos.180593
- **Design:** N = 370 Australians; statements by Australian politicians from both sides; the ratio of corrections to affirmations was manipulated. Measured belief in the statements, feelings toward the politician, voting intention and perceived veracity of the politician.
- **Finding:** Fact-checks corrected beliefs. Feelings, voting intention and perceived veracity of the politician dropped only when corrections outnumbered affirmations. Effects were similar on both sides of politics.
- **Relation:** Speaker evaluation; supports H1 with a boundary: trust in the speaker falls when flags dominate. Our design has three flags and two "No issues found" slides, so the ratio itself is a design variable we should name.
- **Access:** open access https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/pdf/10.1098/rsos.180593
- **Score:** 1=5 2=0 3=2 4=4 5=3 total=14/25

### Swire-Thompson, B., Ecker, U. K. H., Lewandowsky, S., & Berinsky, A. J. (2020). They might be a liar but they're my liar: Source evaluation and the prevalence of misinformation. *Political Psychology*, *41*(1), 21–34. https://doi.org/10.1111/pops.12586
- **Type:** psychology
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1111/pops.12586
- **Design:** US participants saw statements from Republican and Democratic politicians, either equal numbers of false and true statements or many more false ones; fact-checks followed, then belief and feelings toward the politician were re-rated.
- **Finding:** With equal numbers of corrections and affirmations, belief in the false claims fell but feelings toward the politician did not. When corrections clearly outnumbered affirmations, feelings toward both Republican and Democratic politicians dropped, but the effect was described by the authors as extremely small.
- **Relation:** Speaker evaluation; H1 counter-result and the "ratio" boundary. Feeling thermometers stand in for trust here, which is a weaker outcome than our trust ratings.
- **Access:** open access https://research-repository.uwa.edu.au/en/publications/958dc452-2288-439e-a34d-822fc1c53aa9
- **Score:** 1=5 2=0 3=2 4=4 5=3 total=14/25

### Agadjanian, A., Bakhru, N., Chi, V., Greenberg, D., Hollander, B., Hurt, A., Kind, J., Lu, R., Ma, A., Nyhan, B., Pham, D., Qian, M., Tan, M., Wang, C., Wasdahl, A., & Woodruff, A. (2019). Counting the Pinocchios: The effect of summary fact-checking data on perceived accuracy and favorability of politicians. *Research & Politics*, *6*(3). https://doi.org/10.1177/2053168019870351
- **Type:** communication (political science)
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1177/2053168019870351
- **Design:** Three survey experiments comparing a negative rating of one statement with a summary of a politician's fact-check record over time; outcomes were favourability of two prominent elected officials and perceived accuracy of their statements.
- **Finding:** Summary fact-check data lowered favourability and perceived accuracy of the politician more than a single negative statement rating. Co-partisans were not consistently more resistant than opponents.
- **Relation:** Speaker evaluation; supports H1 and suggests accumulation: several flags in a row (slides 2 to 4) may weigh more than one. Our flags are wrong and arrive live, one per slide.
- **Access:** open access https://doi.org/10.1177/2053168019870351

### Barrera, O., Guriev, S., Henry, E., & Zhuravskaya, E. (2020). Facts, alternative facts, and fact checking in times of post-truth politics. *Journal of Public Economics*, *182*, 104123. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpubeco.2019.104123
- **Type:** management or economics
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1016/j.jpubeco.2019.104123
- **Design:** Randomized online experiment with 2,480 French voters during the 2017 presidential campaign; exposure to Marine Le Pen's misleading claims about refugees, official facts, or a fact-check; measured factual knowledge, policy conclusions and support for the candidate.
- **Finding:** The misleading claims were highly persuasive. Fact-checking improved factual knowledge but did not change policy conclusions or support for the candidate.
- **Relation:** Speaker evaluation; counter-result to H1 in a high-stakes field setting. Our participant is a decision maker who must act (the budget), which may make trust more sensitive to flags than voting support is.
- **Access:** open access https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpubeco.2019.104123
- **Score:** 1=4 2=0 3=2 4=3 5=3 total=12/25

### Dobbs, M., Butler, L. H., & Swire-Thompson, B. (2026). The differential consequences of correcting misinformation for high and low credibility sources. *Scientific Reports*, *16*, 16044. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-42329-x
- **Type:** psychology
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; full text read at https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-42329-x.pdf
- **Design:** Experiment 1: 648 US adults (Prolific, final sample) read a vignette in which a doctor (high credibility) or a senator (low credibility) made four false health claims; random assignment to correction or no correction. Credibility was rated before and after on trustworthiness and expertise adjectives (Credibility Semantic scale) and belief on 1 to 7 scales. Experiment 2: 789 adults; all corrected, with the inaccuracy framed as unintentional, intentional, or uncertain.
- **Finding:** Doctors started out more credible than politicians. Corrections lowered credibility for both compared with no correction, and the doctor lost more than the politician (d = 2.05 versus d = 1.52; both ended at similar post-correction credibility) (Experiment 1). In Experiment 2 both lost about the same, and the loss was largest when the inaccuracy was framed as intentional. Sources who sounded uncertain were already rated less credible before the correction.
- **Relation:** Speaker evaluation; the closest finding to H1 for an expert: a correction cuts credibility, and more so for a trusted professional. The framing result supports measuring integrity separately from competence. Our correction is wrong, comes from a machine, and the expert can answer it.
- **Access:** open access https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-42329-x
- **Score:** 1=5 2=0 3=4 4=5 5=5 total=19/25

### Dan, V. (2025). Deepfakes as a democratic threat: Experimental evidence shows noxious effects that are reducible through journalistic fact checks. *The International Journal of Press/Politics*, *31*(3), 525–550. https://doi.org/10.1177/19401612251317766
- **Type:** communication
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18 (online 2025, issue 2026); abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1177/19401612251317766
- **Design:** Multiple-message experiment, N = 2,085; fake videos or texts alleging a sex, corruption or prejudice scandal about an innocent politician, with or without a journalistic fact-check; measured attitudes, emotions and voting intention toward the politician.
- **Finding:** Fake videos (cheap or deep), but not text-only fakes, caused substantial reputational damage to an innocent politician. A journalistic fact-check substantially reduced and in some cases eliminated that damage.
- **Relation:** Speaker evaluation; an exoneration analogue for H2: a false accusation hurts, and a credible rebuttal can restore standing. In our study the rebuttal comes from the accused CISO, not from a third party, which is a weaker source.
- **Access:** open access https://doi.org/10.1177/19401612251317766
- **Score:** 1=4 2=1 3=2 4=3 5=3 total=13/25

## C. Wrong, partial or over-broad flags (implied truth, tainted truth)

### Pennycook, G., Bear, A., Collins, E. T., & Rand, D. G. (2020). The implied truth effect: Attaching warnings to a subset of fake news headlines increases perceived accuracy of headlines without warnings. *Management Science*, *66*(11), 4944–4957. https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2019.3478
- **Type:** management or economics
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1287/mnsc.2019.3478
- **Design:** Study 1: 5,271 MTurk workers rated accuracy of headlines, some false ones tagged as disputed by fact-checkers. Study 2: 1,568 MTurk workers, sharing intentions. A further condition also tagged some true headlines as verified.
- **Finding:** Warnings modestly lowered perceived accuracy of tagged false headlines, but untagged false headlines were then seen as more accurate than in the control (implied truth). Adding "verified" tags to some true headlines removed and slightly reversed this effect.
- **Relation:** Wrong or partial flags; design element: our checker says "No issues found" on slides 1 and 5, which is an explicit verification and so may raise trust on those slides by contrast. Supports interpreting the flag pattern as a whole, not slide by slide.
- **Access:** open access https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/pdf/10.1287/mnsc.2019.3478
- **Score:** 1=0 2=2 3=1 4=4 5=4 total=11/25

### Freeze, M., Baumgartner, M., Bruno, P., Gunderson, J. R., Olin, J., Ross, M. Q., & Szafran, J. (2021). Fake claims of fake news: Political misinformation, warnings, and the tainted truth effect. *Political Behavior*, *43*(4), 1433–1465. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11109-020-09597-3
- **Type:** communication (political science)
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18 (online 2020); abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1007/s11109-020-09597-3 (full text blocked by the publisher's login redirect, so N and procedure not checked)
- **Design:** Experiments adapted from forensic psychology: participants read political news and then received a retrospective warning that was either valid (the news was misleading) or invalid (accurate news wrongly labelled inaccurate); measured credibility of the news and memory for its content.
- **Finding:** Valid warnings helped people discard misleading information, though weakly. False warnings lowered the credibility of accurate news, led people to discard true information and impaired political memory (tainted truth). The authors advise against general, indiscriminate warnings.
- **Relation:** Wrong flags; the direct precedent for our design, where every flag is wrong. Supports H1 (a wrong flag still costs credibility). The target here is a news story, not a person who can answer back.
- **Access:** open access https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s11109-020-09597-3.pdf
- **Score:** 1=1 2=1 3=1 4=5 5=3 total=11/25

### Clayton, K., Blair, S., Busam, J. A., Forstner, S., Glance, J., Green, G., Kawata, A., Kovvuri, A., Martin, J., Morgan, E., Sandhu, M., Sang, R., Scholz-Bright, R., Welch, A. T., Wolff, A. G., Zhou, A., & Nyhan, B. (2020). Real solutions for fake news? ### He, J., Horne, B. D., & Nevo, D. (2026). *On the effectiveness of fact checking information from politically congruent and incongruent large language models* (arXiv:2607.15364) [Preprint]. arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.15364
- **Type:** other HCI (preprint, listed by the authors as accepted to ICWSM 2027)
- **Verified:** DOI unverified (none issued); arXiv page https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.15364; full text read at https://arxiv.org/html/2607.15364
- **Design:** Two within-subjects experiments on Prolific (412 and 293 recruited, 705 in total). LLM chatbots configured with different political leanings fact-checked true and false political headlines; trust in each headline rated on a 5-point scale (completely distrust to completely trust), compared with a no-check control.
- **Finding:** The chatbot fact-checks changed trust in headlines even when the chatbot's leaning differed from the participant's. When the chatbot was wrong, it still moved trust: a true headline labelled false lost about 1 point of trust on the 5-point scale (0.96 and 1.02 in the two studies), and a false headline labelled true gained 0.27 to 0.45 points. The authors conclude LLM checkers can "taint the truth at scale".
- **Relation:** Wrong AI flags; supports H1 for content: a wrong AI verdict lowers trust in correct material by about a quarter of the scale. Extends Freeze et al. to LLM checkers. Our target is the person presenting, not a headline.
- **Access:** open access https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.15364
- **Score:** 1=0 2=4 3=1 4=4 5=4 total=13/25

## D. AI-labelled checkers and how a checked human is judged

### Kim, D., Daneshi, R., Draper, B., & Sreedharan, S. (2026). Asymmetric trust effects of corrective AI in expert advisory workflows under epistemic dependence. *Proceedings of the AAAI/ACM Conference on AI, Ethics, and Society*, *9*(3), 1514–1525. https://doi.org/10.1609/aies.v9i3.43244
- **Type:** other HCI (AIES)
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; full text read at https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIES/article/download/43244/50804 (also arXiv:2602.11522)
- **Design:** N = 157 Prolific participants played students receiving course-planning advice from a human advisor (3 x 2 between subjects): advisor performance (errors or no errors) by AI involvement (no AI, AI invoked by the advisor, AI that automatically monitored and flagged errors). All workflows ended with a correct schedule. Measured trust in the advisor with the METI scale (expertise, integrity, benevolence; Hendriks et al. 2015), a trust scale by Riedl et al. 2024, a 100-point global trust rating and intention to reuse the advisor.
- **Finding:** Advisor errors lowered trust in the human advisor on every measure: perceived expertise, integrity and benevolence, general trust and willingness to reuse (effects of 4 to 15 percent of variance). Visible AI correction did not raise trust in the AI assistant or the human-AI team, and how the AI was involved (absent, invoked, or monitoring) did not change the trust penalty for the advisor. Trust stayed anchored to the human expert.
- **Relation:** AI checks a human expert in front of a non-expert who cannot verify; the nearest design to ours, and a measure (METI with three dimensions) we can reuse. Supports H1 when the AI's flag is right. Our flags are wrong, the expert answers, and the non-expert holds the formal power.
- **Access:** open access https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIES/article/download/43244/50804
- **Score:** 1=5 2=5 3=4 4=5 5=5 total=24/25

### Wang, R., & Ophir, Y. (2026). Can professional or AI fact-checking protect trust in journalism from political attacks? The complex roles of source, transparency, ideology, and the machine heuristic. *Computers in Human Behavior*, *176*, 108872. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108872

Transparency about how the fact-check was done narrowed partisan gaps in fact-checker credibility.

### Amaddio, K. M., Goebel, J. T., Clark, J. K., Wegener, D. T., Garrett, R. K., Susmann, M. W., & Parthasarathy, S. (2026). Prior beliefs & automated fact checking: Limits on the effectiveness of AI-based corrections. *PLOS ONE*, *21*(2), e0342332. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0342332
- **Type:** psychology
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0342332
- **Design:** Two studies, N = 1,372; gun-control misinformation followed by a correction from an automated fact-checker. Study 2 told participants the system was 67% or 97% accurate. Measured continued belief and perceived quality and reliability of the system.
- **Finding:** People's default views of how accurate an automated checker is varied widely. At the stated 67% accuracy (close to what people expect of current AI), people kept believing misinformation that matched their prior views; at 97% this bias shrank. Prior beliefs also shaped how reliable people judged the system, most strongly when it was described as not highly accurate.
- **Relation:** AI checker credibility; design element: whether we state the checker's accuracy will change how far participants follow its flags (H4). Our participants have no prior stake in the CISO's claims, so motivated reasoning should be weaker.
- **Access:** open access https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0342332

### Drolsbach, C. P., Solovev, K., & Pröllochs, N. (2024). Community notes increase trust in fact-checking on social media. *PNAS Nexus*, *3*(7), pgae217. https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgae217

## E. The checked party's response and behaviour

### Schiff, K. J., Schiff, D. S., & Bueno, N. S. (2025). The liar's dividend: Can politicians claim misinformation to evade accountability? *American Political Science Review*, *119*(1), 71–90. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003055423001454
- **Type:** communication (political science)
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18 (online 2024-02-20, issue 2025); abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1017/s0003055423001454
- **Design:** Five survey experiments with over 15,000 American adults; stories about real politician scandals followed by hypothetical politician responses: claiming the story is fake news or a deepfake (by raising uncertainty or rallying supporters), staying silent, or apologising; measured support for the politician and trust in media.
- **Finding:** False claims that a story was misinformation raised support for the politician across partisan groups, and did so more than staying silent or apologising. They worked against text reports but largely not against video evidence, and they did not lower general trust in media.
- **Relation:** Response of the checked party; a counter-result to H3: an unsupported denial ("it is fake") can help the accused, at least when evidence is text. Our CISO's denial with no reason is the truthful party denying a wrong flag, and the audience is a board member, not a supporter.
- **Access:** open access https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/687FEE54DBD7ED0C96D72B26606AA073/S0003055423001454a.pdf

## Gap found: live or second-screen fact-checking

Searches for experiments with fact-checks shown during a live debate or speech, beyond the known Wintersieck (2017) and Garrett and Weeks (2013), found no new controlled study measuring viewers' evaluation of the speaker. What exists is practice and systems: Duke Reporters' Lab's FactPopUp debate overlay (2016, a usage test; https://reporterslab.org/2016/10/17/reporters-lab-experiment-pop-fact-checking-debate/, accessed 2026-10-18), moderator corrections on air in 2024 (ABC and CBS debates), and LiveFC (known). This supports a gap statement: no study has measured trust in a live presenter while an audience member holds an automated checker, and none where the checker is wrong.

## Notes on known papers

- **Chae & Tewksbury (2026, New Media & Society).** The abstract (read at https://comm.ucla.edu/2024/12/08/ai-intervention-and-of-fact-checking) says three experiments, N = 3,978, and ends: neither AI-generated nor human fact-checks significantly affected participants' feelings toward or their perceptions of the competence of the targeted politicians. This is a direct H1 counter-result for speaker evaluation (competence), worth stating in the review, alongside its main finding that AI involvement did not weaken truth discernment.
- **Mattozzi, Nocito & Sobbrio.** A 2026 IZA Discussion Paper version (DP 18534) exists; the summary reports that fact-checked Italian politicians cut incorrect statements for several weeks and replaced them with no statements or unverifiable ones. Cite the latest version.
