# Results, Agent 1: SIGCHI and HCI, AI tools that check, verify or contradict a human in front of another human

Searched and verified 2026-10-18. 21 entries. Every DOI below was resolved on api.crossref.org on 2026-10-18 (one arXiv DOI resolved via OpenAlex and arxiv.org, marked). Abstracts read via Semantic Scholar, OpenAlex, arXiv or the publisher page as stated.

Overall finding for the gap statement: in the SIGCHI literature the checker almost always judges *content* (headlines, statements, arguments) and the measured outcome is belief in the content or trust in the AI. Only the AI-mediated communication strand (Liu et al. 2022; Navajas Fernández et al. 2026; Mieczkowski et al. 2021) measures trust in the *person*, and there the AI acts on the speaker's side, not in the listener's hand. No study found here gives the listener a checker that flags a speaker in a live, power-asymmetric professional setting and measures trust in the speaker before and after the speaker's reply.

## A. AI-mediated communication: AI involvement changes how a person is judged

### Hancock, J. T., Naaman, M., & Levy, K. (2020). AI-mediated communication: Definition, research agenda, and ethical considerations. *Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication*, *25*(1), 89–100. https://doi.org/10.1093/jcmc/zmz022
- **Type:** communication (core reference for HCI AI-MC work)
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1093/jcmc/zmz022
- **Design:** Conceptual paper, no study.
- **Finding:** Defines AI-mediated communication as interpersonal communication in which an intelligent agent acts on behalf of a communicator by modifying, augmenting or generating messages, and sets out a research agenda on its psychological, relational and ethical effects, including how it may change how communicators are perceived.
- **Relation:** Sub-theme A; definition. Our checker is a variant the definition does not cover: the agent acts for the *receiver* (the board member) and comments on the sender's message instead of producing it. One sentence for the review: we extend AI-MC from sender-side to receiver-side mediation.
- **Access:** SFU library (Oxford Academic, may be free on the publisher page)
- **Score:** 1=2 2=2 3=1 4=4 5=3 total=12/25

### Mieczkowski, H., Hancock, J. T., Naaman, M., Jung, M., & Hohenstein, J. (2021). AI-mediated communication: Language use and interpersonal effects in a referential communication task. *Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction*, *5*(CSCW1), 1–14. https://doi.org/10.1145/3449091
- **Type:** SIGCHI
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18 (Crossref lists the short title "AI-Mediated Communication"; full subtitle from the ACM DL); abstract read at https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1145/3449091
- **Design:** Between-subjects experiment with pairs doing a text-based referential communication task, with or without Google-style smart-reply suggestions; measured language use and the partners' interpersonal perceptions (including social attraction).
- **Finding:** AI suggested replies made language more positive, and there was preliminary, mixed evidence that AI-generated language lowered some parts of how partners perceived each other, such as social attraction.
- **Relation:** Sub-theme A; context for H1 (an AI in the channel can lower interpersonal perception even when it is not aimed at the person). Our study differs because the AI openly contradicts the speaker instead of quietly shaping their words.
- **Access:** SFU library
- **Score:** 1=3 2=2 3=1 4=2 5=3 total=11/25

### Liu, Y., Mittal, A., Yang, D., & Bruckman, A. (2022). Will AI console me when I lose my pet? Understanding perceptions of AI-mediated email writing. In *Proceedings of the 2022 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems* (pp. 1–13). Association for Computing Machinery. https://doi.org/10.1145/3491102.3517731
- **Type:** SIGCHI
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1145/3491102.3517731
- **Design:** Large-scale surveys and in-depth interviews; participants read emails and were told AI was or was not involved in writing them; messages varied from transactional to interpersonal; measured trust in the email writer.
- **Finding:** Participants trusted the email writer less when told AI was involved in writing. Unexpectedly, the drop was smaller (trust was higher) when AI helped with more interpersonal emails than with transactional ones.
- **Relation:** Sub-theme A; H1 context and a measure (trust in the human sender as outcome). It shows AI involvement alone lowers trust in a person; our study asks whether an AI *verdict against* the person does the same and whether the person's reply repairs it.
- **Access:** open access https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3491102.3517731
- **Score:** 1=5 2=3 3=1 4=3 5=3 total=15/25

### Navajas Fernández, N., Hancock, J., & Jakesch, M. (2026). Through the looking-glass: AI-mediated video communication reduces trust and confidence in judgement. In *Proceedings of the 2026 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems* (pp. 1–19). Association for Computing Machinery. https://doi.org/10.1145/3772318.3790845
- **Type:** SIGCHI
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1145/3772318.3790845 and https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18868
- **Design:** Two preregistered online experiments, N = 2,000 in total. Participants watched short videos of speakers making truthful or deceptive statements under three levels of AI mediation (retouching, background replacement, avatars). Measured trust in the speaker, lie-detection accuracy and confidence in one's own judgement.
- **Finding:** Participants trusted speakers less and were less confident in their own judgements when the video was AI-mediated, most of all when some speakers used avatars and others did not. Their accuracy at telling truth from lies did not change, and they did not suspect AI users of lying more often.
- **Relation:** Sub-theme A; H1 and a design element (trust in a speaker on video, separate from accuracy). Closest recent CHI study of trust in a speaking person when AI is in the loop; our study differs because the AI is an explicit checker in the listener's hand, not a filter on the speaker.
- **Access:** open access https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.18868
- **Score:** 1=5 2=3 3=1 4=4 5=3 total=16/25

### Sobieraj, S., Eimler, S., & Rinkenauer, G. (2023). Can smart glasses change how people evaluate healthcare professionals? A mixed-method approach to using smart glasses in hospitals. *International Journal of Human-Computer Studies*, *178*, Article 103081. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhcs.2023.103081
- **Type:** SIGCHI (IJHCS)
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1016/j.ijhcs.2023.103081
- **Design:** Online experiment, 7 (glasses design) x 2 (sex) x 2 (social status), N = 415, followed by a focus group. Participants rated a healthcare professional pictured with or without smart glasses.
- **Finding:** The design of the glasses mattered most for how the professional was perceived. In the focus group the glasses caused as much confusion as curiosity, and were seen as something attached to the body rather than part of it.
- **Relation:** Sub-theme A; context for wearable or camera devices in expert-layperson encounters. Here the expert wears the device; in our study the non-expert holds the camera, the reverse arrangement.
- **Access:** open access https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1071581923000903
- **Score:** 1=4 2=2 3=4 4=2 5=3 total=15/25

## B. Machine heuristic: why a checker's verdict carries weight

### Sundar, S. S., & Kim, J. (2019). Machine heuristic: When we trust computers more than humans with our personal information. In *Proceedings of the 2019 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems* (pp. 1–9). Association for Computing Machinery. https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300768
- **Type:** SIGCHI
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18 (Crossref short title "Machine Heuristic"); abstract read at https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1145/3290605.3300768
- **Design:** Experiment, N = 160, airline booking with a machine or human agent; measured willingness to disclose credit card details and the accessibility of the belief that machines are more secure and trustworthy.
- **Finding:** Participants were more willing to give their credit card information to a machine agent than to a human agent. Those who more strongly held the belief that machines are more trustworthy disclosed more to the machine, but not more to the human.
- **Relation:** Sub-theme B; theory anchor for H1 and H4: a non-expert may give an automated flag more weight than a human expert's word. Our study tests this in the reverse direction (machine against human).
- **Access:** SFU library
- **Score:** 1=1 2=2 3=1 4=4 5=3 total=11/25

### Yang, H., & Sundar, S. S. (2024). Machine heuristic: Concept explication and development of a measurement scale. *Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication*, *29*(6), Article zmae019. https://doi.org/10.1093/jcmc/zmae019
- **Type:** communication
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1093/jcmc/zmae019
- **Design:** Three studies, combined N = 1,129: open-ended survey (N = 270), exploratory factor analysis (N = 448), confirmatory factor analysis (N = 411).
- **Finding:** A validated seven-item scale of how strongly a person believes machines are superior, and six descriptive labels people attach to machines (expert, efficient, rigid, superfluous, fair, complex).
- **Relation:** Sub-theme B; measure. A short pre-study covariate that could explain who follows the checker (H4 amplification). Not a trust-in-person study.
- **Access:** open access https://academic.oup.com/jcmc/article-pdf/29/6/zmae019/59341760/zmae019.pdf

## C. Checkers in the listener's hand: wearable and interactive reasoning aids

### Danry, V., Pataranutaporn, P., Mao, Y., & Maes, P. (2020). Wearable Reasoner: Towards enhanced human rationality through a wearable device with an explainable AI assistant. In *Proceedings of the Augmented Humans International Conference* (pp. 1–12). Association for Computing Machinery. https://doi.org/10.1145/3384657.3384799
- **Type:** SIGCHI (ACM Augmented Humans)
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18 (Crossref short title "Wearable Reasoner"); abstract read at https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1145/3384657.3384799
- **Design:** Proof-of-concept wearable that runs argument mining on heard speech and tells the wearer whether a claim is backed by evidence. Experiment on evaluating verbal statements with no feedback, feedback, and explainable feedback; measured rated reasonableness of and agreement with the statements; plus interviews.
- **Finding:** With explainable feedback, users rated statements backed by evidence as more reasonable and agreed with them more than statements without evidence, compared with no feedback. Interviews showed users reflecting on the feedback when judging the speaker's arguments.
- **Relation:** Sub-theme C; the closest design precedent to our listener-held checker (a wearable that judges what someone says). It measured judgements of statements, not trust in the speaker, which is our gap.
- **Access:** open access https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3384657.3384799
- **Score:** 1=2 2=5 3=1 4=4 5=3 total=15/25

### Danry, V., Pataranutaporn, P., Mao, Y., & Maes, P. (2023). Don't just tell me, ask me: AI systems that intelligently frame explanations as questions improve human logical discernment accuracy over causal AI explanations. In *Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems* (pp. 1–13). Association for Computing Machinery. https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3580672
- **Type:** SIGCHI
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1145/3544548.3580672
- **Design:** Experiment, N = 204, judging the logical validity of socially divisive statements with no feedback, a causal AI explanation, or AI-framed questions.
- **Finding:** Turning the AI's reasoning into questions improved participants' detection of logically flawed statements more than no feedback and more than causal explanations from an always-correct AI.
- **Relation:** Sub-theme C; design element. A checker that asks instead of asserting ("Do the numbers add up?") is a design alternative to our assertive flags; relevant to H4, since question framing may push the board member to ask the CISO rather than to challenge.
- **Access:** open access https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3544548.3580672
- **Score:** 1=1 2=4 3=1 4=3 5=3 total=12/25

### Danry, V., Pataranutaporn, P., Groh, M., & Epstein, Z. (2025). Deceptive explanations by large language models lead people to change their beliefs about misinformation more often than honest explanations. In *Proceedings of the 2025 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems* (pp. 1–31). Association for Computing Machinery. https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713408
- **Type:** SIGCHI
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.00024 (preprint version; OpenAlex has no abstract for the CHI version)
- **Design:** Preregistered online experiment, N = 1,192 (23,840 observations). Participants judged true and false headlines with an AI that gave honest or deceptive explanations, or only a true/false label.
- **Finding:** Deceptive AI explanations were more persuasive than honest ones: they raised belief in false headlines and lowered belief in true ones more than a bare label did. Cognitive reflection and trust in AI did not reliably protect people. Explanations whose logic did not support the AI's verdict were seen as less credible.
- **Relation:** Sub-theme C; H1 and H4. Our flags are wrong but sound plausible ("right by the textbook"), which is close to this paper's deceptive explanations; we add the step where the human expert answers back.
- **Access:** open access https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.00024 (CHI version listed as open at https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713408)
- **Score:** 1=1 2=4 3=1 4=4 5=3 total=13/25

### Nguyen, A. T., Kharosekar, A., Krishnan, S., Krishnan, S., Tate, E., Wallace, B. C., & Lease, M. (2018). Believe it or not: Designing a human-AI partnership for mixed-initiative fact-checking. In *Proceedings of the 31st Annual ACM Symposium on User Interface Software and Technology* (pp. 189–199). Association for Computing Machinery. https://doi.org/10.1145/3242587.3242666
- **Type:** SIGCHI (UIST)
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18 (Crossref short title "Believe it or not"); abstract read at https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1145/3242587.3242666
- **Design:** Built a mixed-initiative fact-checking interface; user study in which participants judged claims with the model's predictions shown, with or without the option to interact with the evidence.
- **Finding:** Participants tended to trust the system: their accuracy rose when the model was right and often fell when the model was wrong. Participants who could interact with wrong predictions often recovered some accuracy.
- **Relation:** Sub-theme C; H1 and H4 (users follow a wrong checker), and a design precedent for a UIST-style checker. Claims had no human speaker who could answer back, which is what our design adds.
- **Access:** SFU library
- **Score:** 1=1 2=4 3=1 4=3 5=3 total=12/25

## D. AI credibility flags, and flags that are wrong

### Lu, Z., Li, P., Wang, W., & Yin, M. (2022). The effects of AI-based credibility indicators on the detection and spread of misinformation under social influence. *Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction*, *6*(CSCW2), 1–27. https://doi.org/10.1145/3555562
- **Type:** SIGCHI
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1145/3555562
- **Design:** Two randomized online experiments; news items with or without AI credibility indicators, with and without other people's judgements shown; measured perceived veracity and engagement.
- **Finding:** AI indicators pulled people's beliefs toward the AI's verdict whether the AI was right or wrong. The effect was larger when other people's opinions were also shown and when the indicator came before people formed their own judgement.
- **Relation:** Sub-theme D; H1 and H4 (people align with a wrong AI; showing the flag before one's own judgement strengthens it, as in our design where the flag appears with the slide). Predecessor of the known Lu et al. CHI 2025 paper. Content only, no speaker.
- **Access:** open access https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3555562
- **Score:** 1=1 2=4 3=1 4=4 5=3 total=13/25

### Seo, H., Xiong, A., & Lee, D. (2019). Trust it or not: Effects of machine-learning warnings in helping individuals mitigate misinformation. In *Proceedings of the 10th ACM Conference on Web Science* (pp. 265–274). Association for Computing Machinery. https://doi.org/10.1145/3292522.3326012
- **Type:** other HCI (ACM WebSci)
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18 (Crossref short title "Trust It or Not"); abstract read at https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1145/3292522.3326012
- **Design:** Two online experiments comparing a fact-checking warning, machine-learning warnings and a control on fake news recognition, detection and sharing; post-session trust in each warning.
- **Finding:** In Experiment 1 only the fact-checking warning improved detection, and participants trusted it more than the machine-learning warnings. In Experiment 2 a transparent machine-learning warning with a graph improved discrimination between fake and real news most, yet participants still trusted it less than the fact-checking warning.
- **Relation:** Sub-theme D; counter-result to the machine heuristic: an automated label can be less trusted than a human fact-check, which bears on how much weight a board member gives the checker in H1.
- **Access:** SFU library
- **Score:** 1=1 2=4 3=1 4=3 5=3 total=12/25

### Horne, B. D. (2025). Does the source of a warning matter? Examining the effectiveness of veracity warning labels across warners. *Proceedings of the International AAAI Conference on Web and Social Media*, *19*, 823–836. https://doi.org/10.1609/icwsm.v19i1.35848
- **Type:** other HCI (ICWSM)
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1609/icwsm.v19i1.35848
- **Design:** Online between-subjects experiment, N = 2,049; warning labels attributed to the platform, other users, AI, or fact-checkers, versus no label; measured trust in the information and intent to share.
- **Finding:** All four warners lowered trust in false information; AI warnings were slightly more effective than the others, and much more effective among participants with low trust in news organizations. All except other users' warnings lowered sharing intent.
- **Relation:** Sub-theme D; supports H1 (an AI-sourced warning is at least as persuasive as a human one) and suggests a moderator (prior institutional trust). Target is content, not a speaker.
- **Access:** open access https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICWSM/article/download/35848/38002
- **Score:** 1=1 2=4 3=1 4=3 5=3 total=12/25

### Horne, B. D., & Nevo, D. (2025). People adhere to content warning labels even when they are wrong due to ecologically rational adaptations. *Scientific Reports*, *15*(1), Article 13896. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-98221-7
- **Type:** other HCI (published in a general science journal)
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1038/s41598-025-98221-7
- **Design:** Three between-subjects experiments, N = 1,313; news headlines with correct or wrongly placed warning labels; measured trust in the news.
- **Finding:** People relied on warning labels when deciding whether to trust news even when the labels were wrong. The authors explain this as a learned shortcut: labels have usually been human-made and correct, so people carry that habit over to systems where labels are often wrong.
- **Relation:** Sub-theme D; direct support for H1 with wrong flags, which is our manipulation. Our study adds a human target who can rebut the wrong flag (H2, H3).
- **Access:** open access https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-98221-7.pdf
- **Score:** 1=1 2=4 3=1 4=5 5=3 total=14/25

## E. AI that judges whether a person is lying

### Loconte, R., Monaro, M., Pietrini, P., Verschuere, B., & Kleinberg, B. (2026). Humans incorrectly reject confident accusatory AI judgments. *Computers in Human Behavior*, *182*, Article 109019. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2026.109019
- **Type:** other HCI (CHB)
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1016/j.chb.2026.109019
- **Design:** Experiment, N = 373; participants saw an AI's truth or lie verdicts on people's statements, with the model's overall accuracy (high or low) and its confidence per statement varied; measured whether participants adopted the AI's verdict.
- **Finding:** Participants followed a highly accurate model more than a less accurate one, but the more confident the model was, the more they departed from it, especially when it said the person was lying. Human adjustments made the combined accuracy worse or no better. The authors link this to people's default assumption that others tell the truth and to the social cost of accusing someone.
- **Relation:** Sub-theme E; counter-result to H1: observers may resist a confident AI accusation against a person. Close to our flags, which imply the CISO is wrong; our study measures trust in the person rather than agreement with the verdict.
- **Access:** open access https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2026.109019
- **Score:** 1=3 2=5 3=1 4=5 5=3 total=17/25

### Serra-Garcia, M., & Gneezy, U. (2025). Improving human deception detection using algorithmic feedback. *Management Science*, *71*(12), 10289–10307. https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2023.02792
- **Type:** management or economics
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1287/mnsc.2023.02792
- **Design:** Participants watched pre-play talk by contestants in the TV show Golden Balls and predicted whether each would cooperate; an algorithm flagged videos as likely cooperation or defection, shown before or after the video.
- **Finding:** Participants on their own predicted contestants' behaviour poorly, while the algorithm did much better. Participants relied much more on flags shown before they watched the video than on flags shown after.
- **Relation:** Sub-theme E; design element for our flag timing (flag shown while the slide is presented, before the board member forms a view) and H1. Judgement of a speaker's honesty under a flag, in a high-stakes but non-professional setting.
- **Access:** open access preprint https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.4495855 ; journal version SFU library
- **Score:** 1=3 2=5 3=2 4=4 5=3 total=17/25

## F. AI as challenger or second opinion in groups, and power

### Chiang, C.-W., Lu, Z., Li, Z., & Yin, M. (2024). Enhancing AI-assisted group decision making through LLM-powered devil's advocate. In *Proceedings of the 29th International Conference on Intelligent User Interfaces* (pp. 103–119). Association for Computing Machinery. https://doi.org/10.1145/3640543.3645199
- **Type:** SIGCHI (IUI)
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1145/3640543.3645199
- **Design:** Randomized experiment with groups making AI-assisted decisions; four LLM devil's advocate styles varied by interactivity and by target (the AI recommendation or the group majority); measured reliance on AI, workload and perceived group process.
- **Finding:** A devil's advocate that argued against the AI's recommendation helped groups rely on the AI more appropriately; it did not add much workload; interactive versions were seen as more collaborative and of higher quality.
- **Relation:** Sub-theme F; design element (an LLM that objects in a group setting). Our checker objects to a human presenter instead of to an AI, and we measure trust in that human.
- **Access:** open access https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3640543.3645199
- **Score:** 1=1 2=4 3=2 4=3 5=3 total=13/25

### de Brito Duarte, R., Abreu, M. C., Campos, J., & Paiva, A. (2025). The amplifying effect of explainability in AI-assisted decision-making in groups. In *Proceedings of the 2025 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems* (pp. 1–15). Association for Computing Machinery. https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713534
- **Type:** SIGCHI
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1145/3706598.3713534
- **Design:** Controlled experiment, N = 89, mushroom edibility classification, individuals versus two-person groups, AI recommendations with or without explanations; measured reliance on correct and incorrect AI advice.
- **Finding:** Explanations mattered more for groups than for individuals: with explanations, groups relied less on wrong AI advice than individuals did; without explanations, groups relied more on wrong AI advice than individuals did.
- **Relation:** Sub-theme F; H4 (amplification). An outside example that the social setting can magnify both good and bad responses to the same AI input, as H4 predicts for the checker group. No human target is evaluated.
- **Access:** open access https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3706598.3713534
- **Score:** 1=1 2=3 3=1 4=4 5=3 total=12/25

### Lu, Z., Wang, D., & Yin, M. (2024). Does more advice help? The effects of second opinions in AI-assisted decision making. *Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction*, *8*(CSCW1), 1–31. https://doi.org/10.1145/3653708
- **Type:** SIGCHI
- **Verified:** Crossref 2026-10-18; abstract read at https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1145/3653708
- **Design:** Three preregistered randomized experiments; decision makers saw an AI recommendation with or without a second opinion from a peer or another AI, shown always or on request; measured over- and under-reliance on the AI.
- **Finding:** When a second opinion was always shown, people relied less on the AI when it was wrong but also more often ignored it when it was right, whether the second opinion came from a peer or an AI. Asking for a second opinion only when they wanted one reduced over-reliance without raising under-reliance in some cases.
- **Relation:** Sub-theme F; context for how a second opinion shifts reliance between two sources. Here the AI is the primary advisor and the human the second opinion; our study reverses this (human expert first, AI checker second) and measures trust in the human.
- **Access:** open access https://doi.org/10.1145/3653708
- **Score:** 1=1 2=4 3=1 4=3 5=3 total=12/25

### Lee, S., & Lee, K. (2026). Rethinking AI-mediated minority support in power-imbalanced group decision-making: From anonymity to authenticity [Position paper, CHI 2026 Workshop on Restoring Human Authenticity in AI-Mediated Communication]. arXiv. https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2604.22319
- **Type:** SIGCHI (CHI workshop paper; earlier system paper: Lee, S., Kim, M., Hwang, S., Kim, D., & Lee, K. (2025). Amplifying minority voices: AI-mediated devil's advocate system for inclusive group decision-making. In *Companion Proceedings of the 30th International Conference on Intelligent User Interfaces* (pp. 17–21). https://doi.org/10.1145/3708557.3716334)
- **Verified:** arXiv DOI not in Crossref (DataCite); resolved via https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.48550/arxiv.2604.22319 and https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22319 on 2026-10-18; the IUI 2025 companion paper verified in Crossref 2026-10-18; both abstracts read via OpenAlex
- **Design:** Position paper drawing on an ongoing empirical study of hierarchical groups comparing two LLM strategies: relaying minority members' input anonymously through the AI, or having the AI generate its own counterarguments.
- **Finding:** Relaying minority input anonymously raised participation but lowered psychological safety and satisfaction; AI-generated counterarguments raised satisfaction and reduced the feeling of being sidelined. The authors warn that power asymmetry can reverse the intended effect of an AI that speaks up in a group.
- **Relation:** Sub-theme F; context for power asymmetry. In our study the lower-expertise but higher-power party (board member) uses the AI to challenge the expert; this paper is one of the few HCI works on AI challengers in hierarchies. Preliminary, workshop-level evidence only.
- **Access:** open access https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22319
- **Score:** 1=1 2=3 3=4 4=3 5=3 total=14/25

## Notes on known papers

- **Gupta et al. (CHI 2026), Feeling the Facts.** The wearable checker was deliberately imperfect (reported system accuracy 87.5%, N = 34, participants watched pre-fact-checked videos), and trust was measured as trust in the AI-provided information, not in the speaker in the video (from the arXiv HTML, https://arxiv.org/html/2603.27289, search snippet; not re-read in full). This sharpens the gap statement: the nearest real-time wearable checker study does not measure trust in the person being checked.
- **Lu et al. (CHI 2025).** Its predecessor, Lu et al. (CSCW 2022, entry above), already showed that people align with an AI indicator whether right or wrong and that the effect grows when the indicator appears before people form their own view; the CHI 2025 paper should be cited together with it.
