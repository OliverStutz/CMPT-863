## 3. Synthesis versus summary

Paragraphs 1 and 3 synthesise: paragraph 1 sets up two theories that the evidence is then read against, and paragraph 3 groups trust-repair psychology and HCI checker studies into two camps and places the project between them. Paragraph 2 is mostly summary in sequence: Lowry (two sentences), Opdenbusch (one), comparison (one), Aamir (one), Schilke (one). One-paper summaries a marker would notice:

- "Lowry et al. (2026) interviewed directors, CISOs and consultants and found non-expert directors' oversight mostly symbolic..." (66 words, one study, method plus finding).
- "In Australia, directors challenged management more confidently where external assurance was routine; without cyber literacy, boards often accepted management's account (Aamir et al., 2026)." Fine content, but it is not connected to anything; it is actually the best evidence that an independent view helps, and the paragraph does not say so.
- "Power itself works against trust: in exchange experiments, the party with better alternatives trusted its partner less (Schilke et al., 2015)." A stand-alone fact from another field; the reader must guess that the board is the powerful party.

The comparison sentence ("Lowry et al. (2026) read the same deference as a gap in expertise rather than power; the two studies agree that the CISO controls what the board hears") is genuine synthesis and should lead the paragraph, not sit in its middle. Fix 1 restructures paragraph 2 around it.

## Appendix: condensed section with the fixes applied (about 530 words)

Boards must oversee cybersecurity, yet few directors have the expertise to judge it (Lowry et al., 2026). The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC, 2023) requires public companies to describe how the board oversees cyber risk, but it dropped a proposed disclosure of director cyber expertise, reasoning that directors with broad risk skills often oversee technical matters without it (p. 85). Lowry et al. (2026) frame such oversight with two theories: agency theory expects the board to check managers whose interests may differ from its own, and institutional theory expects boards without expertise to adopt practices that look legitimate.

Field studies favour the institutional reading for non-expert boards, but the agency problem remains. Lowry et al. (2026) found non-expert directors' oversight mostly symbolic even when they did what expert directors did: they believed their business experience let them ask the right questions, but expert participants saw them unable to assess the answers. The study's CISO survey put the median share of CISOs who filter board reports to look better at 40%; most non-expert directors thought filtering unlikely. In the United Kingdom, directors held back questions rather than expose their lack of knowledge, the budget was by most accounts their only cyber decision, and CISOs decided what reached the board (Opdenbusch et al., 2025). Lowry et al. trace this deference to expertise rather than power; both studies find that the CISO controls what the board hears. In Australia, boards without cyber literacy often accepted management's account; routine external assurance made directors more confident to challenge it (Aamir et al., 2026).

Three things are missing. First, this evidence comes from interviews and surveys; we found no study that put a checking tool in a director's hand. Second, the two relevant fields do not meet. Trust-repair research has a human accuse and a human reply, with no tool involved (Ferrin et al., 2007; P. H. Kim et al., 2004). HCI research on checkers measures belief in claims or trust in the AI (Danry et al., 2020; Lu et al., 2025); the few studies that measured trust in the checked person had the expert consult the AI or let it run on its own (Chen et al., 2026; D. Kim et al., 2026), never a listener holding it. Third, the effect on the board-CISO relationship is unknown. A board that cannot judge the substance and does not expect filtering is likely to give a flag weight whether or not it is right, and power itself works against trust: in exchange experiments, the party with better alternatives trusted its partner less (Schilke et al., 2015). A wrong flag may cost a correct CISO the board's trust and the budget; a well-answered flag may strengthen it.

This study adds the experiment to the board research and the tool to the trust research. A participant plays a board member who decides the CISO's budget and holds a checker during the briefing. Every flag is right by the textbook but wrong for this company, and the CISO answers with a reason or a bare denial. Trust in the CISO, rated after every flag and answer and compared with a group without a checker, is the measure neither field has taken.
