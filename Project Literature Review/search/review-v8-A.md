Condensed version (502 words, all four answers kept, all citations kept, inferences hedged):

> Boards must oversee cybersecurity, and most do so without the expertise to judge it. The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC, 2023) requires listed companies to describe that oversight but dropped the plan to disclose director expertise, reasoning that directors with broad risk skills often oversee technical matters without it (p. 85). Lowry et al. (2026) frame oversight with two theories: agency theory expects the board to monitor executives whose interests may differ from the firm's; institutional theory expects uncertain boards to adopt practices that look legitimate. Tools for an independent view are on the market: smart glasses answer questions about what the wearer sees (Meta, 2024), and Copilot in Teams will say where the holes in a meeting participant's argument lie (Microsoft, n.d.).
>
> Field studies agree that directors without expertise ask but cannot judge the answers. Non-expert directors believed their business experience let them ask the right questions, but expert directors and consultants saw them unable to interpret the answers; their oversight was mostly symbolic, and expertise decided which theory fit (Lowry et al., 2026). They also thought CISO filtering unlikely, although surveyed CISOs put its share at a median of 40%. In the United Kingdom, some directors held back questions rather than expose their lack of knowledge, and CISOs decided what reached the board (Opdenbusch et al., 2025). Opdenbusch et al. call this CISO power, Lowry et al. a gap in expertise; both find that the CISO controls what the board hears. Australian directors challenged management more confidently where external assurance was routine and otherwise often accepted management's account (Aamir et al., 2026). In exchange experiments the more powerful party trusted its partner less (Schilke et al., 2015), yet these boards, which hold the budget, rarely doubted the CISO.
>
> Three things are missing. First, this evidence comes from interviews and surveys; we found no study that put a checking tool in a director's hand. Second, the fields that could answer the question do not meet: trust research studies a human accuser and a human reply (Ferrin et al., 2007; P. H. Kim et al., 2004), and HCI studies of checkers mostly measure belief in claims or trust in the AI (Danry et al., 2020; Lu et al., 2025). The few that measured trust in the person checked had the expert consult the AI or let it run on its own (Chen et al., 2026; D. Kim et al., 2026), never a listener holding it. Third, a board with little to weigh a flag against may give it weight whether or not it is right: a wrong flag may cost a correct CISO trust and budget, and a well-answered flag may strengthen trust.
>
> This study adds the experiment to the board research and the tool to the trust research. A participant acting as a board member holds a checker whose flags are right by the textbook but wrong for this company; the CISO answers each with a reason or a bare denial, and trust is rated after every flag and answer, against a group without a checker.

Notes on the condensed version: "yet these boards ... rarely doubted the CISO" is a synthesis of Lowry p. 796 (filtering thought unlikely) and Aamir p. 15 (accepted management's account), not a new claim; it should be listed in the working notes as the study's own reading. The budget-only point (Opdenbusch p. 5) is dropped; if Oliver wants the budget stake sourced, keep "and the budget was, by most participants' account, the board's only cyber decision" (+13 words).
