## C2. Readability for a CHI reviewer (compliance item 6)

- Reads as one argument: yes, mostly. Section titles state claims, sections 2 and 3 open with a thesis sentence ("Such checkers are often wrong", "Wrong flags are believed", "The person flagged pays"), and section 3 ends with limits. The "sources disagree on power" paragraph is the strongest synthesis in the draft.
- Still lists papers: section 1 paragraph 1 (SEC, Lowry, Proudfoot, Opdenbusch, Aamir, one quote each); section 3 paragraph 2 (Strickland, Prike, Madanay, Chen, Reif, one per sentence); section 4 paragraph 2 (Kim 2004, Ferrin, Greitemeyer, Mickelberg, Sharma, one per sentence). Each could open with the shared finding (for example "Replies repair trust, but rarely beyond the start") so the citations support a claim rather than form a list.
- Vague or dangling: "Proudfoot et al. (2023) agree" (agree on what: that boards judge the presenter, not the substance); the Pezzo sentence (Fix 7); "Hence H2 ... H3" names hypotheses a reader of a related-work section has not seen yet, unless the paper defines them earlier; "Two results set limits" is clear. The politician sentence is terse but does its job.
- Section 4 is the longest (375 words) and carries three strands (definition, human-accuser repair, machine-accuser burden); a reviewer would follow it, but the transition "In these studies the accuser is human" is the only link between the last two.
