# Results 7: regulation that assigns cybersecurity oversight to boards, and the reach of AI tools into boardrooms

Brief: `search/_regulation-brief.md`. Searched and verified 2026-10-20. Every entry below was read in its primary source (PDF or official web page) on that date unless the entry says otherwise. Page numbers are the printed page numbers of the document named. Access date for all URLs: October 20, 2026.

## 1. Summary

- **What section 1 can say.** Regulators agree that cyber risk is a board matter but disagree on whether directors need cyber expertise. The U.S. SEC (2023) requires listed companies to describe the board's oversight of cybersecurity risk (Item 106(c)(1)) but dropped its proposal to disclose directors' cyber expertise (Item 407(j)), reasoning that directors with broad-based skills "often effectively oversee management's efforts without specific subject matter expertise" (p. 85). The EU takes the opposite line: NIS2 Article 20(2) requires members of management bodies "to follow training" so that they can "identify risks and assess cybersecurity risk-management practices". The UK Cyber Governance Code of Practice (2025) asks directors to "Undertake training to improve your own cyber literacy" (Action C3), and the 2026 NACD handbook asks boards to define the cyber expertise they need and close gaps.
- **Why a U.S. anchor is defensible.** The SEC release is the only regulatory text found that explicitly weighs and rejects a director-expertise requirement, with reasons, so it states the premise of the study (non-expert directors overseeing an expert) most plainly. Canada has no board-level cyber disclosure rule or expertise requirement. CSA guidance (Staff Notice 11-332, 2016; Multilateral Staff Notice 51-347, 2017) only says it is "relevant to disclose governance issues", OSFI Guideline B-13 (2022, in force January 1, 2024) puts cyber accountability on senior management, and the new federal *Critical Cyber Systems Protection Act* (royal assent June 15, 2026) makes directors liable only if they took part in a violation. One Canadian link is worth a clause: Canadian issuers listed in the U.S. that file under the Multijurisdictional Disclosure System (Form 40-F) are outside Item 106 (SEC, 2023, p. 87).
- **Recommended citations (two or three).**
  1. **SEC (2023), Release 33-11216**: the anchor. Gives the board duty (Item 106(c)(1), p. 171) and the expertise reasoning (p. 85). It also gives the CISO briefing example used in the study scenario (p. 69, fn. 247).
  2. **NIS2 Directive (2022), Article 20**: the clearest contrast. It is binding law that requires training for management bodies, so the review can say "regulators differ" with one citation.
  3. **Nolen (2026), Diligent Institute / Corporate Board Member Director Confidence Index**: for the AI sentence. Of 104 U.S. public company directors, 82% had used generative AI in their board work in the past six months, up from 66% in September 2025. Back it with PwC (2025) if a larger sample is wanted (638 directors, but it measures board-level use: 35%).
- **Suggested sentence for the AI claim:** "Generative AI has already reached board work: 82% of 104 U.S. public company directors reported using it for board work in the past six months (Nolen, 2026), and board portals now ship AI summaries and suggested questions for directors (Diligent, 2025)." This supports "within reach"; it does not show that directors use AI to check a CISO's claims live.

Verified entries per question: Q1 SEC 2023: 3 (the release itself, its Federal Register record, and the April 2026 trade-association letter on its status); Q2 Canada: 7; Q3 other jurisdictions: 5 (NIS2, UK Code, NACD 2026, ASIC speech, ASIC good-practice page); Q4 AI in boardrooms: 8 (4 surveys, 1 handbook-cited survey figure, 2 products, 1 scholarly article).

### What is required of boards: Item 106(c)(1), verbatim

Codified rule text (17 CFR 229.106(c)), Release p. 171 (also p. 180 for Form 20-F Item 16K; 88 FR 51942 in the Federal Register):

> "(c) Governance. (1) Describe the board of directors' oversight of risks from cybersecurity threats. If applicable, identify any board committee or subcommittee responsible for the oversight of risks from cybersecurity threats and describe the processes by which the board or such committee is informed about such risks."

Notes from the discussion (Release pp. 68 to 70):
- The adopted Item 106(c) is "less granular than proposed" (p. 68). The proposal had asked for the frequency of board discussions and whether the board integrates cyber into strategy; both were removed (pp. 68 to 69).
- No materiality qualifier on 106(c)(1), because "if a board of directors determines to oversee a particular risk, the fact of such oversight being exercised by the board is material to investors" (p. 69, fn. 250).
- Footnote 247 (p. 69) gives the CISO briefing case directly: "if the board or committee relies on periodic (e.g., quarterly) presentations by the registrant's chief information security officer to inform its consideration of risks from cybersecurity threats, the registrant may, in the course of describing those presentations, also note their frequency." Useful for the study's CISO-briefing scenario.
- Item 106(c)(2) asks for the relevant expertise of management (for example the CISO), "in such detail as necessary to fully describe the nature of the expertise" (p. 69 to 70, rule text p. 171). Expertise disclosure was kept for management and dropped for directors.
- The release's own paraphrase on p. 68 reads "[d]escribe the board's oversight"; the codified text on p. 171 reads "board of directors' oversight". Quote the codified text.

### What was proposed and dropped: Item 407(j)

- Proposed: "the Commission proposed adding 17 CFR 229.407(j) (Regulation S-K "Item 407(j)") to require disclosure about the cybersecurity expertise, if any, of a registrant's board members" (p. 81). The proposal did not define expertise but listed criteria "such as prior work experience, certifications, and the like" (p. 81), and included a safe harbour so that named experts would not carry greater liability (Item 407(j)(2), p. 82).
- Comments: pp. 82 to 84. Supporters included NACD, CII, CalPERS, PRI and "Prof. Lowry et al." (p. 82, fn. 292; the Lowry letter submitted an academic study, p. 83, fn. 295). Opponents argued cyber risk "is not intrinsically different from other risks" and that directors need "broad-based skills in risk and management oversight, rather than subject matter expertise in one particular type of risk" (ABA letter, p. 83), and that there is not enough cyber talent for every board (p. 83).
- Decision, p. 85 (88 FR 51918), verbatim:

> "After considering the comments, we are not adopting proposed Item 407(j). We are persuaded that effective cybersecurity processes are designed and administered largely at the management level, and that directors with broad-based skills in risk management and strategy often effectively oversee management's efforts without specific subject matter expertise, as they do with other sophisticated technical matters."

  The paragraph continues: a registrant that has determined that board-level expertise is necessary "would likely provide that disclosure pursuant to Items 106(b) and (c)" (p. 85).

### Canada inside the SEC rule

- p. 85, fn. 309 and p. 87: the Commission did not amend Form 40-F. Verbatim (p. 87): "we do not find reason to adopt prescriptive cybersecurity disclosure requirements for Form 40-F filers, given that the MJDS generally permits eligible Canadian FPIs to use Canadian disclosure standards and documents to satisfy the Commission's registration and disclosure requirements." So eligible Canadian cross-listed issuers follow Canadian rules, which have no Item 106 equivalent.

### Status in 2026

- Item 106 is still in force as far as could be verified. On April 10, 2026 five trade associations (ABA, BPI, SIFMA, ICBA, IIB) wrote to the SEC in its Regulation S-K review (File No. CLL-15): "we urge the Commission to rescind Item 106" (SIFMA joint trades letter, read in full). No SEC proposal to amend or rescind Item 106 was found; sec.gov rulemaking pages could not be checked directly (see section 6). Worth one clause at most ("the rule remains contested").

- Supports: "In Canada, the banking regulator places technology and cyber accountability on senior management and leaves the board to general governance expectations (OSFI, 2022)."

## 4. Other jurisdictions (Q3)

**O1. EU NIS2 Directive, Article 20 (Governance).** Directive (EU) 2022/2555 of 14 December 2022, OJ L 333, 27.12.2022, pp. 80 to 152. ## 5. AI tools in boardrooms (Q4)

**A1. Diligent Institute and Corporate Board Member, Director Confidence Index, June 2026.** Article by Melanie Nolen (Research Editor, Chief Executive Group), June 17, 2026. Surveyed: 104 U.S. public company directors.
- "82 percent have used generative AI in their board work in the past six months", up from 66% in September 2025.
