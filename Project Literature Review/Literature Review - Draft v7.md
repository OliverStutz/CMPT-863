## Cybersecurity oversight by non-expert boards

Boards must oversee the cybersecurity professionals who serve them, whether employed or contracted, and most do so without the expertise to judge their work. The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC, 2023) made such oversight mandatory for listed companies, which must describe how the board oversees risks from cybersecurity threats, but it withdrew its proposal that companies disclose whether any director has cybersecurity expertise, reasoning that broadly skilled directors "often effectively oversee management's efforts without specific subject matter expertise" (p. 85). Boards therefore oversee with or without subject-matter expertise, and field evidence shows what oversight without it looks like. Lowry et al. (2026) found non-expert directors' oversight "mostly symbolic" (p. 783) even when they did what expert directors did: they believed their general experience let them ask the right questions, whereas expert directors and executives judged that they could not interpret the answers, so that, as one expert director put it, "all they can do is assess the credibility of the executives that are put in front of them" (p. 798). Opdenbusch et al. (2025) report that directors would be "too shy to ask questions" (p. 6) for fear of exposing their lack of knowledge, and that the budget is the only cybersecurity decision most boards make. Where external assurance was routine, directors reported more confidence in challenging management; where literacy was missing, boards often accepted management's account (Aamir et al., 2026). A board in this position has an evident need for a check that does not depend on its own expertise, and in the age of AI such a check is within reach of almost every director: consumer tools already answer questions about whatever a camera is pointed at (Meta, 2024) and suggest, during a meeting, where the holes in a participant's argument lie (Microsoft, n.d.), although none is as explicit a checker as the one simulated in this study. Whether such a tool helps directors ask better questions is secondary to the question this study asks: what a checker in one person's hand does to that person's trust in the person being checked.

The relationship between the two makes this question critical rather than incidental. The board holds the formal power of oversight and the budget; the CISO holds informational power. Opdenbusch et al. (2025) conclude that "the CISOs are the ones in power" (p. 2) because they decide what reaches the board, and survey respondents in Lowry et al. (2026) estimated, at the median, that 40% of CISOs filter their reports to the board to look better, a risk most non-expert directors considered unlikely (p. 796). A non-expert board that does not expect filtering has no view of what it is not told beyond the one a checker gives it, so the checker's verdicts are likely to carry weight whether or not they are right. Power itself also bears on trust: in exchange experiments, the party with better alternatives trusted its partner less (Schilke et al., 2015), although there power meant having alternatives rather than authority. The consequences must therefore be clear before boards adopt such tools: a flag that is wrong for the company may cost a correct CISO the board's trust, and with it the budget decision that rests on that trust, while a flag that is answered well may strengthen it. All of this evidence comes from interviews, with neither a checking tool nor a measure of trust, and the experimental literature reviewed in the sections that follow does not fill the gap. Studies of trust between people are psychological and purely interpersonal: a human accuses, a human replies, and no tool is involved (e.g., P. H. Kim et al., 2004; Ferrin et al., 2007). Studies of checking tools in human-computer interaction examine the tool and its content, and measure belief in claims or trust in the AI rather than trust in the person checked (e.g., Danry et al., 2020; Lu et al., 2025). The augmented human of HCI research, a person who checks another person with a tool in hand, falls between the two, and what the tool does to the trust between them has not been examined directly. The present study examines exactly this scenario. A participant acting as a board member, with the budget decision in their hands, uses a checker during a CISO's briefing. Each flag the checker raises is right by the textbook but wrong for this company, and the CISO answers it. Trust in the CISO is rated after every flag and every answer, and compared with the ratings of board members who follow the same briefing without a checker.
