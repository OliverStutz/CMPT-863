  Like: A phicon is both a container and a handle

  A model of MIT's Great Dome placed on the metaDESK summons the campus map beneath it, and moving the model moves the map, so one object acts "both as a container of bits" and "as a handle for manipulating the map". A second model turns the pair into two handles, for two hands or two people, and the authors set this against the mouse's single point of control: the interaction is "constrained by the physics of the physical environment". Figure 9 extends the move to every GUI element, window to lens, icon to phicon, menu to tray, handle to phandle, widget to instrument. The price of "container" is that the Dome model means the MIT campus and nothing else, so the tangible interface trades the mouse's generality for the object's specificity. Zooids recovers some of that generality: a zooid is a phicon the computer can drive to wherever the data says, and sweeping a row of them with the side of the arm is this many-handles idea on a table full of elements. What Geospace cannot do, and Zooids can, is let the computer move the handle back.

  Like: Build the expectation into the object

  One design decision I would steal is small: with two phicons on the desk, rotating one or both independently has no clear meaning, and the software simply ignores the conflict. Rather than pick an interpretation in software, the authors designed a rotation-constraint instrument for this one case, two cylinders coupled by a sliding bar, whose mechanics make the ambiguous input impossible. I read the discussion of optical metaphors as the same principle: an interface metaphor that stays consistent with the physics of real space is legible, because "people know what to expect of a flashlight, know what to expect of lenses". This is the complement to what I presented from Wobbrock, Morris and Wilson in Week 4, where 44 percent of the gestures people invented were physical, treating shapes on glass as if they were objects. Wobbrock and colleagues elicited a gesture set from users because designers could not predict that expectation. Here the object is physical, so the designer can build the expectation into the object, and nobody needs to be asked.

  Like: Not ubiquitous computers, but awakened objects

  The paragraph on Weiser draws the clearest line in the paper. The authors take Ubiquitous Computing as inspiration and then part ways with it, because Tabs, Pads and Boards export "a GUI-style interaction metaphor to large and small computer terminals". Tangible Bits, in their words, "is not about making 'computers' ubiquitous per se, but rather about awakening richly-afforded physical objects, instruments, surfaces, and spaces to computational mediation". Read in 2026, the Tab, Pad and Board are the phone, the tablet and the wall display, so the industry took the path the authors set aside, and their diagnosis of "painted bits" on rectangular screens still holds. The abacus that closes the paper is their illustration of the other path: a computing device that was also a musical instrument, an imaginary toy train and a back scratcher, and whose sound told the young Ishii his mother was busy. That one object carries the whole paper: foreground manipulation and peripheral awareness from the same artefact.

Summary (not for submission)

Ishii and Ullmer, CHI 1997, printed pages 234 to 241. A vision paper: it is "not intended to propose a solution to any one single problem" but to "propose a new view of interface and raise a set of new research questions to go beyond GUI". There is no formal evaluation; the paper reports only informal observations (the rain sounds were at times distracting, the water-tank display was received enthusiastically by visitors, the barcode wand was cumbersome).

The argument

- We live between the physical environment and cyberspace with no seamless coupling between them. GUIs confine interaction to rectangular screens ("painted bits"), bias toward graphical output, and neglect both haptic skills (Post-it notes spatially arranged on a wall) and peripheral senses (knowing the weather from ambient light).
- Inspiration: the oak and brass scientific instruments at Harvard's Collection of Historic Scientific Instruments, and what was lost when personal computers replaced them.
- Three key concepts: Interactive Surfaces (walls, desktops, ceilings, doors, windows become active interfaces); Coupling of Bits and Atoms (cards, books, models coupled with their digital information); Ambient Media (sound, light, airflow, water movement at the periphery of perception).
- Center and periphery (Figure 3): graspable media in the foreground, ambient media in the background. The smooth transition of attention between the two is named "a key challenge of Tangible Bits". Information moves to the foreground either when it becomes anomalous or when there is a lull in the foreground task.
- Ultimate aim: turn each state of matter, solids, liquids and gases, within architectural space into an interface.

Positioning against related work

- Weiser's Ubiquitous Computing: inspiration, but Tabs, Pads and Boards export a GUI-style metaphor to terminals. Tangible Bits instead awakens richly-afforded physical objects, borrowing more from pre-computer forms than from the present.
- Augmented Reality (DigitalDesk as the pioneer): Tangible Bits differs from the common visual-overlay approach by taking graspable objects as input and by combining them with ambient media.
- ClearBoard: a wall as a collaboration medium, which led to the idea that all surfaces become active.
- Hinckley's passive props for neurosurgery: differs by integrating input and output rather than props plus a screen.
- Bricks (Fitzmaurice, Ishii and Buxton): space-multiplexed input, two-handed manipulation. The direct ancestor of the graspable focus.
- Marble Answering Machine (Durrell Bishop): voice messages as marbles you drop into the machine to play or onto the phone to call back.
- Live Wire (Natalie Jeremijenko): network packets as twitches of a hanging cord. Inspired ambient media as a general mechanism.
- Fields and Thresholds (Dunne and Raby): two steel benches in different cities that warm and open a sound channel when someone sits. Inspired the ambientROOM.

Three prototypes (Figure 7)

- metaDESK: a back-projected desk with optical, mechanical and electromagnetic sensors. Elements: activeLENS (arm-mounted LCD, a physical window, modelled on a jeweller's magnifying lens), passiveLENS (transparent plexiglass brought to life by the back-projection), phicons (physical icons), trays, phandles and instruments. Figure 9 maps them to window, icon, menu, handle and widget. Application: Tangible Geospace. A model of the Great Dome summons the MIT campus map beneath it; the phicon is both container and handle; the activeLENS shows a 3D view; a second phicon (the Media Lab) lets the user scale, rotate and translate with two hands, or lets two users share control. Independent rotation of two phicons is ambiguous and the software ignores it, so they designed a rotation-constraint instrument (two cylinders on a sliding bar, Figure 12) that mechanically prevents it.
- ambientROOM: a Steelcase Personal Harbor unit, 6 by 8 feet, with MIDI-controlled light, shadow, sound, airflow and water. Scenario: a toy car phicon stands for web hits on the car's page; placed near a speaker it plays raindrops; heavy rain means many visits; a change in the rain pulls attention and the user carries the phicon to the metaDESK for detail. Prototyped with passive phicons, infrared proximity sensors and recorded rainfall; the paper does not say whether the hand-off to the metaDESK was built. They found the display compelling but the rain sounds sometimes distracting, so they built a water tank with a solenoid-driven float and lit it so the ripples show on the ceiling. Framing: the brain as parallel processor and attention manager. A new project, Ghostly Presence, is announced for remote awareness.
- transBOARD: a SoftBoard whiteboard that tracks tagged pens and erasers with a scanning infrared laser. Strokes are served to remote viewers through a Java applet. hyperCARDs (barcode-tagged, magnet-backed paper cards) store a session's strokes on a web server, the barcode acting as the URL, so placing the card on a screen recalls the drawing. The barcode wand was cumbersome; they were moving to RFID. The transBOARD is a one-way filter that absorbs bits; the metaDESK is bi-directional.

Discussion: optical metaphors

- Light, shadow and optics as the metaphor that spans all three platforms. The activeLENS came first and invoked the metaphor implicitly; then the passiveLENS; then digital shadows (phicons project information about their contents); then a flashlight, begun but not finished, that would project "semantic light", so a building's shadow could also reflect publications or sponsorship, or shift rendering from photorealistic to impressionistic.
- The stated value of the metaphor is its consistency with the physics people already expect, which maximises legibility.

Conclusion

- Change "painted bits" into "tangible bits" by using multiple senses. The abacus anecdote: Ishii met it at age two; it was a computing device, musical instrument, imaginary toy train and back scratcher; the sound of his mother's abacus told him she was busy.

What the paper does not do

- No formal evaluation of any prototype; only informal observations.
- The ambientROOM to metaDESK hand-off is a scenario; the paper does not say whether it was built.
- transBOARD's display is admitted to be limited; an activeLENS for it is planned.
- Ghostly Presence and the semantic-light flashlight are announced, not reported.
