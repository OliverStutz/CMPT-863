# Reviewer report: Jian, Bisantz, Drury & Llinas, "Foundations for an empirically determined scale of trust in automated systems" (AFRL-HE-WP-TR-2000-0102, DTIC ADA395339)

Source read in full: tesseract OCR of the DTIC scan (1,722 lines, about 8,900 words). Locations below give the report's printed page number and then the scan page in brackets. Printed page = scan page − 12, so "p. 30 [42]" means printed page 30, scan page 42.

### 1. The paper in one paragraph

This is a US Air Force Research Laboratory technical report from the University at Buffalo. The title page is dated February 1998 and reads "Interim report for the period April 1996 to February 1997" (scan pp. 1, 3). It has four authors, including Llinas, who is not an author of the 2000 journal article we cite. The report never mentions the journal article. The report is not an experiment on trust in any person or system. It is a three-phase study of trust-related words, used to build a questionnaire. Nobody used an automated system, and nobody rated trust in a person or a system. The only manipulation was which kind of trust participants were told to think about: trust in general, trust between people, or trust between people and automated systems. In phase 2 there was a second manipulation: participants rated words against either "trust" or "distrust".

- **Phase 1, word elicitation (pp. 6–9 [18–21]).** 7 graduate students in Linguistics or English, all native English speakers, wrote descriptions of trust and distrust. They also sorted 138 candidate words as positively related to trust, not related, negatively related, or don't know. 60 words were dropped and 38 new words added, which left 96 words ("Set-1").
- **Phase 2, questionnaire (pp. 10–16 [22–28]).** 120 native-English university students (45 graduate, 75 undergraduate; 50 men, 70 women) were split into six between-subject conditions: trust or distrust, crossed with the three kinds of trust. The number per condition is not stated; it would be 20 if the groups were equal. Each participant rated how related each of the 96 words was to trust, or to distrust, on a 7-point scale from "positively related" to "negatively related". Averaged per word, the trust ratings and the distrust ratings were almost exact mirror images. A word rated strongly related to trust was rated just as strongly negatively related to distrust. The correlations across words were r = −.96 for general trust, −.95 for trust between people, and −.95 for trust in automation (p. 11 [23]). The slopes of distrust on trust were −0.96, −0.79 and −1.01. The people-to-people slope was flatter than the other two (t = 4.78 and t = 5.68, df = 220). In other words, participants rated words less extremely for distrust between people than for trust between people (pp. 11–12 [23–24], Figure 1). The top-rated words overlapped heavily across the three kinds of trust; trustworthy, loyalty and reliability appeared in all three top-five lists (Table 2, p. 14 [26]). The words from the top-10 lists, 15 trust words and 15 distrust words, became "Set-2".
- **Phase 3, paired comparison (pp. 17–19 [29–31]).** 30 native-English students (12 graduate, 18 undergraduate; 14 men, 16 women), about 10 per kind of trust (Table 3), rated the similarity of all 435 pairs of the 30 words. They used a 7-point scale from "Totally different" to "Almost the same", and a session took 1 to 2 hours. The average ratings stopped changing much after 8 or 9 participants (Table 3, p. 19 [31]).
- **Scale development (pp. 20–30 [32–42]).** A factor analysis of the phase-2 ratings gave 9, 6 and 8 factors, explaining 77%, 77% and 79% of the variance. The authors found these factors "difficult to interpret" (p. 23 [35]). A cluster analysis of the phase-3 similarity ratings split the words into one large trust cluster and one large distrust cluster for all three kinds of trust. The order of the words was similar across the three kinds (rank correlations .84, .88 and .89; pp. 24–25 [36–37]). The clusters for trust in automation were turned into 12 questionnaire items (Table 5, p. 29 [41]; Figure 8, p. 30 [42]).

The 12-item scale was never given to anyone or tested in this report. The authors call it "proposed" and say it "should be validated" (pp. 32–33 [44–45]).

### 2. Verdict per hypothesis

The report does not bear on any of H1–H4. It has no presenter, no checker, no flag, no question or answer, and no trust rating of any person or system over time. Its only use to us is as the source of the trust-in-checker measure.

**H1 (trust in the CISO drops when a VLM claim is shown)**
- **Verdict:** Silent
- **Evidence:** None. No one saw a claim about another person, and no rating was taken before and after an event.
- **How their situation differs from ours:** Participants rated how related words were to trust, or how similar two words were. Nobody checked anybody. There was no power relation, nobody could reply, and there was no correct or incorrect check. What was measured was word meaning, not trust in a target.
- **Quote:** None bearing on H1.

**H2 (trust recovers or rises after the CISO explains)**
- **Verdict:** Silent
- **Evidence:** None. There were no answers, no explanations and no repeated ratings.
- **How their situation differs from ours:** The same as for H1.
- **Quote:** None bearing on H2.

**H3 (trust falls further after a denial or non-answer)**
- **Verdict:** Silent
- **Evidence:** None on denials or non-answers. One side finding could be misread as relevant. Participants rated words less extremely for distrust between people than for trust between people (slope −0.79, against −0.96 for general trust and −1.01 for trust in automation; pp. 11–12 [23–24]). This is about how people rate word meanings. It says nothing about how far trust in a real person falls, so it neither supports nor contradicts H3.
- **How their situation differs from ours:** The same as for H1.
- **Quote:** None bearing on H3.

**H4 (trust change depends on the participant's actions and the CISO's responses)**
- **Verdict:** Silent
- **Evidence:** None. No actions were chosen and no responses were heard. The authors only propose, for future work, that ratings of trust in a system should fall as the system's quality falls, and reliance with them (pp. 32–33 [44–45]). That is about trust in the automation, not in the person being checked (see section 3).
- **How their situation differs from ours:** The same as for H1.
- **Quote:** None bearing on H4.

### 3. Other relevant content

**3.1 The 12 items exactly as printed (Figure 8, p. 30 [42])**

Title of the form: "Checklist for Trust between People and Automation".

Instructions, verbatim, including the original grammar: "Below is a list of statement for evaluating trust between people and automation. There are several scales for you to rate intensity of your feeling of trust, or your impression of the system while operating a machine. Please mark an "x" on each line at the point which best describes your feeling or your impression. (Note: not at all=1; extremely=7)"

1. The system is deceptive
2. The system behaves in an underhanded manner
3. I am suspicious of the system's intent, action, or outputs
4. I am wary of the system
5. The system's actions will have a harmful or injurious outcome
6. I am confident in the system
7. The system provides security
8. The system has integrity
9. The system is dependable
10. The system is reliable
11. I can trust the system
12. I am familiar with the system

Items 1–5 are worded toward distrust and items 6–12 toward trust. The form lists all five distrust items first. The report says nothing about the order of the items or about mixing them.

OCR notes:
- The OCR renders the word "I" as "|", "1" or "T" ("3 1 am suspicious", "4 | am wary", "1 | can trust" for item 11, "Tam suspicious" in Table 5). I read all of these as "I".
- Table 5 (p. 29 [41]) words two items slightly differently from Figure 8. Item 3 ends "…intent, action, or output" (singular), and item 5 reads "The system's action will have a harmful or injurious outcome" (singular). Figure 8 is the version the authors say "might be presented to participants" (p. 25 [37]), so use the Figure 8 wording.

**3.2 Response format**

Under each item is a line with seven points numbered 1 to 7. The only labels are at the ends, printed once above item 1: "not at all" = 1 and "extremely" = 7. Points 2 to 6 have no labels, and there is no labelled midpoint. The participant marks an "x" on the line.

The OCR of the numbers 1–7 is clean under all 12 items. The tick marks on the lines are garbled, but they do not change the reading. The authors present this as a proposal, not a tested format: "Figure 8 shows how the proposed scale might be presented to participants" (p. 25 [37]).

**3.3 How the items were derived (Table 5, p. 29 [41])**

Each item was written from one cluster of words in the cluster analysis for trust in automation (Figure 7, p. 28 [40]). The clusters were cut at a level chosen to give roughly the 11–13 word groups that the factor analysis suggested (p. 24 [36]).

| Item | Word cluster it was based on |
|---|---|
| 1 deceptive | deception, lie, falsity, betray, misleading, phony, cheat |
| 2 underhanded | sneaky, steal |
| 3 suspicious | mistrust, suspicion, distrust |
| 4 wary | beware |
| 5 harmful outcome | cruel, harm |
| 6 confident | assurance, confidence |
| 7 security | security |
| 8 integrity | honor, integrity |
| 9 dependable | fidelity, loyalty |
| 10 reliable | honesty, promise, reliability, trustworthy, friendship, love |
| 11 can trust | entrust |
| 12 familiar | familiarity |

The items are the authors' own phrasings of the clusters. No participant rated the items themselves.

**3.4 Trust and distrust: opposites or separate? (matters for scoring)**

- **Finding.** "the high negative correlations of ratings of trust and distrust indicate that these concepts can be treated as opposites, lying along a single dimension of trust. In previous studies, this has been assumed, but not empirically tested. In practical terms, this implies that it is not necessary to develop questionnaires to measure high and low levels of distrust, separately from high and low levels of trust." (Discussion, p. 31 [43]). The same conclusion is in the abstract (scan p. 3) and the Conclusions (p. 34 [46]). In the cluster analysis, the words also fell into one trust cluster and one distrust cluster at the top level (p. 24 [36]).
- **What the evidence actually is.** The correlation of about −.95 is across words. Each word's average "related to trust" rating, from one group of participants, was correlated with its average "related to distrust" rating, from a different group (p. 11 [23]). This shows the words mean opposite things. It does not show that one person's answers to items 1–5 and to items 6–12 will move together when rating a real system. That was never tested, because the items were never administered.
- **Scoring rule.** The report gives none. It has no reverse-scoring instruction, no total or average, no subscales, no reliability figure and no norms. I searched the OCR for "score", "reverse", "alpha" and "reliab" in a scoring sense and found nothing.
- **Our inference, not in the report.** To get one trust-in-checker score, reverse-score items 1–5 (8 − answer) and average all 12, giving a score from 1 to 7. The report's single-dimension finding supports this. Because the finding comes from word ratings, also report the trust items (6–12) and the distrust items (1–5) separately, and check in our own data how strongly they agree.
- **Background knowledge (not in this report, and not verified here).** As far as I recall, later studies that actually gave people these 12 items reported that the distrust-worded and trust-worded items can split into two factors. Treat this only as a reason to run the check above, not as a fact to cite.
- **Nuance for trust in people.** "people seemed to consider human-human trust more in terms of trust than distrust" (p. 34 [46]; the basis is on pp. 11–12 [23–24]). At most, this is a weak side note in favour of asking about the CISO with a trust-framed rating, as our 0–10 item does. It is not evidence for any hypothesis.

**3.5 Item 12 ("I am familiar with the system") behaves differently**

In phase 1 some participants rated "familiarity" as not related to trust. It was kept only "to provide continuity with the existing literature" (p. 7 [19]). It formed a factor of its own in all three kinds of trust (pp. 20–21 [32–33]) and a cluster of its own in all three (p. 25 [37]). After a 20-minute session, every participant will be about equally unfamiliar with our checker, so this item will mostly measure exposure, not trust. Suggestion (ours): report the score with and without item 12.

**3.6 What the scale was built for, against how we use it**

"the scale was developed with respect to a non-directed feeling of trust in automated systems, rather than trust in a specific system which the participants had experienced … However, the scale was not developed to measure a general personality trait of being trusting, but was focused on trust in a specific type of system." (p. 32 [44]). Our use is trust in one specific checker that the participant has just used, which is outside the setting the items were built for. It is, however, the kind of use the authors propose for validating the scale (see 3.7).

Wording must be adapted:
- Replace "the system" with "the checker" (or "the content checker").
- Rewrite "while operating a machine" in the instructions.
- Say "adapted from" in the draft.

**3.7 Authors' recommended future work that our checker group partly carries out**

"participants' actions regarding the use of an automated control system or information source could be captured, as the quality of those systems changes. As the system performance or information source degrades, one would expect participants to rely on the system or information less, and also to rate the system lower on factors of some trust on the proposed trust scale." (pp. 32–33 [44–45]). They also suggest checking reliability by giving the scale to "the same participants over time" (p. 33 [45]).

Our checker is an information source whose flags are all wrong for this company. The action "challenge the CISO with the checker's finding" is an act of relying on it. So we could relate the end-of-study checker-trust score to how often each participant chose "challenge". Because we give the scale only once, at the end, we cannot show the change over time that the authors describe. We should say so.

**3.8 Things to note or avoid**

- **No numbers for a power calculation.** The report has no effect sizes for any change in trust and no reliability for the 12 items.
- **Language.** All participants in all three phases were native English speakers (pp. 6, 10, 17 [18, 22, 29]). Our sample is all SFU students, and some will not be native speakers. Words such as "underhanded", "injurious" and "wary" may be hard for them. Consider a short gloss, or at least note it as a limitation.
- **Human and machine trust are similar concepts.** The authors argue that trust between people and trust in automation are similar concepts, so "results from studies of human-human trust … may indeed have applicability to situations of trust between humans and automated systems" (p. 31 [43]). This could be cited if we want to say that trust in the CISO and trust in the checker are comparable constructs, but it is a weak argument: it rests on word ratings only.
- **Citation.** The report is Jian, Bisantz, Drury & Llinas: four authors, title page dated 1998, report number TR-2000-0102. The journal article is Jian, Bisantz & Drury (2000), three authors. If we cite the report for the 7-point format, it needs its own reference entry.

**3.9 OCR and report defects**

- Figure 3 (p. 16 [28]) is rotated and unreadable in the OCR.
- Table 1 (pp. 8–9 [20–21]) has scrambled columns.
- R² values appear as "R?" in the Figure 1 OCR.
- The report's text says "Figure 5 shows the groupings of trust-related words for each factor" (p. 20 [32]) but means Table 4.
- The word counts do not add up: 138 − 60 + 38 = 116, but Set-1 is reported as 96 words (p. 7 [19]). The report does not explain the difference.

None of these defects affects the 12 items or the response format, which are clean in Figure 8.

### 4. Check of our current sentence

**Draft: "Trust in the checker (Jian et al., 2000), checker group only."**
- **Accurate in substance.** Jian et al. is a scale for trust in an automated system, so it fits a VLM checker.
- **Incomplete.**
  - The items refer to "the system", so our version is an adaptation.
  - The sentence gives no item count or response format.
  - The source calls the scale "proposed" and does not validate it (pp. 32–33 [44–45]).
  - The scale was built for a "non-directed" feeling of trust in a type of system, not for one system just experienced (p. 32 [44]).
- **Suggested wording:** "Trust in the checker, checker group only, at the end: the 12-item checklist for trust between people and automation (Jian et al., 2000), with "the system" replaced by "the checker", each item rated from 1 (not at all) to 7 (extremely)."

**Notes: "Jian et al. (2000) has 12 items."**
- **Accurate.** Figure 8 (p. 30 [42]) and Table 5 (p. 29 [41]) both list 12 items, and the abstract refers to "12 potential factors" (scan p. 3).
- **Worth adding:** five items are worded toward distrust (1–5) and seven toward trust (6–12).

**Notes: "The 7-point format was confirmed through secondary sources only."**
- **No longer accurate.** The report itself confirms the 7-point format. Figure 8 (p. 30 [42]) prints the numbers 1 to 7 under every item, with the note "(Note: not at all=1; extremely=7)".
- **Two limits:**
  - This is the technical-report version (four authors, 1998/2000). This file cannot show that the 2000 journal article prints the same figure.
  - The authors present the format as how the scale "might be presented" (p. 25 [37]), not as a tested format.
- **Suggested wording:** "Jian et al. (2000) has 12 items (five worded toward distrust, seven toward trust), each rated on a 7-point scale from 1 = "not at all" to 7 = "extremely". This format is confirmed in the technical-report version (Jian, Bisantz, Drury, & Llinas, AFRL-HE-WP-TR-2000-0102, Figure 8); the journal version was not checked. The report gives no scoring rule."

### 5. Bottom line

This report is worth citing only as the source of the trust-in-checker measure. It says nothing on H1–H4 and should not be cited for them. The fact that matters most for us: the source gives the 12 items and the 1 (not at all) to 7 (extremely) format, but no scoring rule. Its claim that trust and distrust are opposites rests on word ratings, not on answers to the items. So reverse-score items 1–5 before averaging, and check in our own data that the distrust and trust items agree.
