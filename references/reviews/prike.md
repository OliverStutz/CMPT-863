# Reviewer report: Prike, Baker & Ecker (2025), Political Psychology 46(5)

Source: pdftotext of the accepted manuscript. Page numbers below are the manuscript's own page numbers ("p."), not journal pages. Extraction problems that matter: (a) Tables 1 and 2 are scrambled in the text file (columns merged, statements separated from their fact-checks). I matched each statement to its fact-check by content. (b) Figures 1 to 6 are images and are missing from the text. The per-candidate mean ratings on the 0 to 10 scales (Figs. 2 to 4) are therefore not available, so **the drop in scale points cannot be given from this file**. Only effect sizes on difference scores are in the text.

### 1. The paper in one paragraph

This is a single online experiment with 5 groups, each participant in one group only (a 2 × 2 design plus a control group). Participants were 406 politically moderate U.S. adults recruited on Prolific. 455 were recruited, and the authors removed 8 who said they recognized a candidate, 1 repeat attempt, and 40 with strong left or right views. Mean age was 38.7; 218 men, 187 women, 1 non-binary. The session took about 15 minutes, paid US$2.50, and ran in August 2022 (p. 9, p. 16). Participants imagined they had moved to Malta and were deciding how to vote. They met two fictional centrist candidates, one slightly left-leaning and one slightly right-leaning, through a short profile (name, party, headshot) and then a social-media simulator showing 16 generic posts (all groups). The four experimental groups also saw 8 false statements favouring one candidate (the "misinformation candidate") and 4 true statements, 2 per candidate (p. 8, Table 1 p. 10). Two factors were manipulated: which candidate the misinformation favoured (left or right), and whether a fictional fact-checker, "Fact Checks Malta", "introduced as nonpartisan and objective", commented on every true and false statement with "TRUE." or "FALSE." and a one-sentence explanation (p. 8, Table 2 pp. 13–14). All ratings were taken **once, at the end**. Competence and morality of each candidate were rated on 0 to 10 sliders, warmth on a 0 to 10 thermometer, then a forced-choice vote, then belief in each statement on a 0 to 10 scale (pp. 12, 15). The analysis used the left-minus-right difference score and compared each experimental group with the control group (p. 16). **Main result:** misinformation that was not checked helped a candidate a little, mostly the right-leaning one. When the misinformation was fact-checked, participants rated the misinforming candidate as much less competent than his opponent, compared with control (d = 0.90 and 1.14), much less moral (d = 1.11 and 1.31) and much less warm (d = 0.89 and 1.12). In each case the ratings fell "to well below control levels". Participants were 4.85 and 5.42 times more likely (odds) to vote for the opponent than in control (pp. 17–23). Fact-checks lowered belief in the false statements strongly (d = 2.32 and 2.44) and raised belief in the true statements (d = 1.02 and 1.03) (pp. 24–26). There is one study only.

### 2. Verdict per hypothesis

#### H1: trust drops when a flag is shown

- **Verdict:** Indirect support (by analogy)
- **Evidence:** One ANOVA per rating across the 5 groups: competence F(4, 401) = 51.31, p < .001, ηp² = .34 (p. 17); morality F(4, 401) = 66.58, p < .001, ηp² = .40 (p. 18); warmth F(4, 401) = 46.08, p < .001, ηp² = .31 (p. 21). The planned comparisons of each fact-check group with control (Holm-corrected) are below. In each, the fact-checked candidate was rated lower relative to his opponent than in control:
  - Competence: left fact-check t(401) = 5.79, p < .001, d = 0.90 [0.59, 1.21]; right fact-check t(401) = 7.24, p < .001, d = 1.14 [0.82, 1.46] (pp. 17–18).
  - Morality: left t(401) = 7.14, d = 1.11 [0.80, 1.43]; right t(401) = 8.32, d = 1.31 [0.99, 1.63], both p < .001 (p. 19).
  - Warmth: left t(401) = 5.72, d = 0.89 [0.58, 1.20]; right t(401) = 7.11, d = 1.12 [0.80, 1.44], both p < .001 (p. 22).
  - Vote: the odds of voting for the non-misinforming candidate were 4.85 times the control odds (95% CI [2.00, 11.79]) and 5.42 times (95% CI [2.03, 14.46]), both p < .001 (p. 23).

  Two caveats on how these numbers map to our H1:
  1. The control group saw only the 16 generic posts. It never saw the true or false statements (Table 1). The comparison that isolates the fact-check alone, "same statements, with versus without fact-checks" (which matches our "only the flag differs"), is **not reported**. Unchecked misinformation raised the candidate slightly above control (competence d = 0.31 and 0.58, morality d = 0.38 and 0.44, warmth d = 0.26 and 0.41). So the effect of the fact-check alone is probably larger than the d values against control. This is my inference; the paper did not test it.
  2. The d values are for the gap between two candidates, not for one person's rating, and between groups, not a before/after drop within a participant.
- **How their situation differs from ours:**
  - *Who checks whom:* a third-party human organization, introduced as nonpartisan and objective, checks the candidates' public posts and newspaper posts about them. The check appears publicly as a comment. In our study, an AI tool held by the rater checks the presenter.
  - *Power:* the participant is a voter who picks between two candidates. There is no expert–board relationship and no expertise gap.
  - *Reply:* the candidate could not reply. Nothing in the paper shows a candidate response to any fact-check.
  - *Correctness:* the checks were correct. The flagged statements were false by design, and each fact-check gave the correct facts. Ours are wrong for the company.
  - *Dose:* 8 statements flagged FALSE for one candidate, against 2 affirmed TRUE (the 4:1 ratio, p. 7). We show 3 flags on 5 slides.
  - *Measure:* competence, morality, warmth and vote, not trust. Competence and morality are defined on p. 7. My background note, not from the paper: these are close to "ability" and "integrity" in Mayer and colleagues' trust model.
  - *Timing:* measured once at the end, not right after each flag.
- **Quote:** "When statements were fact-checked, participants viewed candidates who spread misinformation much less favorably than their political opponents, perceiving them as less competent and less moral, and having reduced feelings of warmth toward them." (Discussion, p. 26)

#### H2: trust recovers or rises after a knowledgeable answer

- **Verdict:** Silent
- **Evidence:** No condition lets the checked person answer, and no rating is taken after any answer. The closest material does not test H2:
  1. *Confirming fact-checks.* Each candidate had 2 true statements that the fact-checker confirmed ("TRUE. According to parliamentary records, Candidate X has a strong record of voting in favor of anti-corruption laws.", Table 2, p. 13). These raised **belief in the true statements** compared with a control group that had never seen them: d = 1.02 [0.71, 1.34] and d = 1.03 [0.71, 1.34], p < .001. Seeing the statements without confirmation did not raise belief (d = 0.16, p = .302; d = 0.26, p = .197) (pp. 25–26). But this is belief in a statement, not a rating of the person. In the fact-check groups **both** candidates received 2 confirmations each, so any effect of confirmation on candidate ratings cancels out of the difference score. The paper cannot say whether a confirmation raises a person's rating. Also, two of the confirmations shrink the claim ("Crime rates dropped by around 2% ... making them marginally lower", p. 13).
  2. *False attacks that were cleared.* By content, two of the 8 false statements attack the opponent: he "loaned millions of Euros to foreign interest groups" and "was once a member of a communist student organization". In the fact-check groups these were corrected, so the opponent was cleared: "Candidate X did not hand out loans ..." and "... was never affiliated with them" (Table 2, p. 13). This is the nearest thing in the paper to "falsely accused, then cleared". But the opponent's own rating is not reported apart from the difference score, and Figs. 2 to 4, which would show whether the opponent ended above control, are images missing from the text file. Worth checking in the PDF figures.
  3. *Background the paper reports from earlier work (Aird et al., 2018), not its own finding:* in an Australian sample, fact-checks moved warmth and voting intention only when the ratio of false to true statements was 4:1, not when it was 4:4 (p. 5). This suggests that enough confirmations can offset the corrections.
- **How their situation differs from ours:** the confirmation came from the third-party checker, not from the checked person. It was about a claim the person made, not a defence against an accusation. The person never spoke after the check. The paper measured belief in the claim, not a rating of the person after the confirmation.
- **Quote (on statement belief only, not person ratings):** "In sum, mere exposure to true statements did not increase belief in them relative to control, but fact-checks slightly but significantly increased belief relative to control." (Results, True-Statement Beliefs, p. 26). Note that the paper calls this "slightly" although d is about 1.0 against control. The raw difference in Fig. 6 is not in the text file.

#### H3: trust falls further after a denial or non-answer

- **Verdict:** Silent
- **Evidence:** No reply of any kind, so no denial or non-answer condition. The only related contrast is that misinformation left unchecked sometimes helped the candidate. That is not a denial after a check.
- **How their situation differs from ours:** the candidate had no voice after the check. The checks were correct, so a denial would have been a false denial, which is the opposite of our set-up, where the CISO is right.
- **Quote:** none exists.

#### H4: trust change depends on the participant's action and the answers heard

- **Verdict:** Silent
- **Evidence:** Participants could "like", "dislike", comment on or skip posts, but "These interactions were included for the sake of realism and were not analyzed" (Materials, p. 12). Their actions did not change what they saw, and candidates never answered. The only moderators tested were the candidate's leaning (misinformation helped the right-leaning candidate more; pp. 17–24, 27) and, in robustness checks, age, gender and political ideology, which did not change the results (p. 16–17).
- **How their situation differs from ours:** in the paper the participant is a passive viewer and every participant in a group sees the same material. In ours, the action chosen at each flag decides whether an answer follows.
- **Quote:** none that bears on H4.

### 3. Other relevant content

**How the person was introduced (borrow):**
- Profiles showed name, party and headshot. The headshots were photos of real European politicians who are little known in the U.S., checked by a Google Trends analysis (Robert Abela, Borut Pahor) (p. 11).
- Names, parties, photos and the order of introduction were fully counterbalanced, giving 80 survey versions (p. 11). For us: counterbalance CISO name, photo and gender, and which 3 of the 5 slides carry a flag.
- Participants who said they recognized a candidate were excluded (n = 8, p. 9). For us: ask whether the CISO or the company seems familiar.
- Before the vote, the two profiles were shown again "to remind them of each candidate's name, face, and party" (p. 12). This could be copied before our budget decision.
- The authors argue that unfamiliar people are where checks bite hardest: "This supports the notion that one reason prior studies have found little or no attitudinal change is due to strong pre-existing attitudes." (p. 28). Our profile deliberately builds a positive prior (experience), so the first trust rating is a natural moderator of the flag effect. This moderation was argued in the paper, not tested.

**How ratings were taken (borrow or adapt):**
- Exact wording (p. 12):
  - "Considering Roberto Camilleri as a candidate for prime minister, how would you rate his competence [morality]?", from "0, Very incompetent" to "10, Very competent" ("0, Very immoral" to "10, Very moral").
  - "Please use the thermometer below to indicate how warmly you feel about Roberto Camilleri", from "0, Very cold" to "10, Very warm".
  - The same 0 to 10 format as our single trust rating.
- "Sliders were initially anchored at the scale midpoint." (p. 12). For repeated ratings we need to decide where the slider starts (midpoint, the previous value, or unset). The paper does not discuss this; my note: starting at the previous value may bias toward no change.
- Ratings were taken once, after all posts (p. 16), and posts appeared in random order. The paper has no data on how ratings move over time. Our repeated ratings after each flag and answer go beyond it.
- Belief in each statement was rated at the end on 0 to 10 ("0, Entirely untruthful" to "10, Entirely truthful"), **including by the control group, who had never seen the statements, as a baseline** (p. 15). For us: at the end, ask both groups how true each flag's claim is ("the architecture is wrong"). In the checker group this checks whether the CISO's answer undid the flag. In the neutral group it gives a baseline.
- The vote was a behavioural measure "to go beyond the self-report measures" (p. 6). Our budget share plays the same role.

**How the check was presented (borrow or contrast):**
- The fact-check appeared as a comment under the post, at the same time as the post, with a verdict word and one sentence of correct facts: "FALSE. Travel records show that Candidate X has only travelled to two out of the five summits held since he entered parliament." (Table 2, p. 13; Fig. 1, p. 15). Our flags ("Architecture likely wrong") name a problem but give no correct facts. That is a difference worth stating.
- The source was framed as credible ("introduced as nonpartisan and objective", p. 8). How we introduce the VLM checker is a comparable choice, and it will affect both the flag's effect and trust in the checker (Jian et al.).

**Pilot of materials (borrow):** 42 candidate statements were pretested (N = 52) for plausibility and political leaning on −2 to +2 scales, and for how much they would shift perceived competence and morality on −5 to +5 sliders. Statements with mean plausibility below 0.5 were dropped, and those with the largest shifts were kept (p. 11). For us: pilot the flags and the CISO answers for plausibility and for how far they move trust.

**Effect sizes for our power calculation:**
- Fact-check against control, on the between-candidate gap: competence d = 0.90 to 1.14, morality d = 1.11 to 1.31, warmth d = 0.89 to 1.12 (pp. 18–22). Unchecked misinformation against control: d = 0.26 to 0.58.
- Group sizes: about 81 per group (406 / 5, my arithmetic; the paper only says "approximately equal group sizes", p. 9). Their a-priori power target was f = 0.20, α = .05, power .80, giving a minimum N of 310 (p. 9).
- My own calculation (normal approximation, not from the paper): with 30 per group, two-sided α = .05, power is about 0.93 for d = 0.9, 0.60 for d = 0.58 and 0.47 for d = 0.5. The smallest d detectable with 80% power is about 0.73.
- Our effect is likely smaller than theirs: 3 flags rather than 8, a profile that builds a positive prior, and answers that can undo the flag. So their d values are best read as an upper reference, not a planning value. This is my inference.

**Moderators seen or argued:**
- Candidate leaning: misinformation helped only or mainly the right-leaning candidate. The authors explain this by plausibility, since the statements read as "populist" (p. 27).
- Absence of prior attitudes (p. 28).
- Age, gender and ideology did not change the results (robustness, pp. 16–17). One competence contrast (misinformation-left against control) was p = .050 in the main analysis and p = .027 without exclusions (p. 17).

**Stated limitations:**
- A single session "with minimal delay between exposure to misinformation, fact-checks, and measures", so real-world effects will be smaller (p. 29).
- Fictional materials limit generalisability (p. 29).
- Politically moderate participants and only mildly partisan candidates (p. 29).
- The study was not pre-registered. Data and materials are on OSF (Declarations, p. 41).

**Authors' recommendations for future work:** multi-session simulated elections, real but unfamiliar politicians, and a wider range of political views (pp. 29–30). None of these is what our study does. Our additions (an AI checker that is wrong, a checked person who can reply, a rater who chooses an action, and repeated ratings) are not named in the paper as future work, so we should not claim to answer a call from these authors.

**Errors inside the manuscript (not extraction):**
- After the warmth fact-check contrasts it says "H1AWarm was thus supported"; from context this should be H1BWarm (p. 22).
- "Slightly" is used for a d of about 1.0 (p. 26).

**Leads from the reference list (titles only; I have not read them, so check before citing):**
- Ecker & Rodricks (2020), "Do false allegations persist? Retracted misinformation does not continue to influence explicit person impressions" (cited p. 5 for the impression-formation method). The title suggests person impressions recover after a false allegation is retracted, which bears directly on H2.
- Nyhan et al. (2020), "Taking fact-checks literally but not seriously?", on fact-checks changing beliefs more than candidate favourability.

**Suggested wording:**
> In Prike et al. (2025), 406 politically moderate U.S. adults formed impressions of two unfamiliar, fictional election candidates from a short profile and a series of social-media posts, and rated each candidate once at the end. When a fact-checking organization, introduced as nonpartisan, labelled eight statements favouring one candidate as false, participants rated that candidate as much less competent, less moral and less warm than his opponent, and fewer voted for him, compared with a group that saw no fact-checks. We borrow the unfamiliar person who is introduced by a profile and rated on a 0 to 10 scale. Unlike Prike et al., our flags come from an AI tool held by the rater, they are wrong for this company, the CISO can reply, and we rate trust before and after each flag. This informs H1.

### 5. Bottom line

Cite this paper for H1 only, as indirect support and as the justification for the design choice of an unfamiliar person introduced by a profile and rated on 0 to 10 scales. It is not the closest design: the checker was a human, was correct, and the checked person could not reply. It gives no evidence for H2, H3 or H4. The fact that matters most: when raters had no prior attitude toward a person, correct fact-checks from a neutral source cut that person's competence, morality and warmth ratings by a large amount (d about 0.9 to 1.3 against control) and cut votes about fivefold in odds. Because our flags are fewer, wrong and answerable, this is a ceiling for the H1 drop, not a prediction of it.
