# Reviewer report: Greitemeyer & Sagioglou (2015), PLOS ONE 10(5), e0126316

"Does exonerating an accused researcher restore the researcher's credibility?" I read the whole file (pdftotext, 12 pages). Page numbers below are the PDF pages (the "n / 12" markers in the text). Anything marked **[reviewer calc]** is my own arithmetic from the reported means, SDs and n. It is not a number printed in the paper.

## 1. The paper in one paragraph

The paper reports two online experiments on Amazon Mechanical Turk (US $0.25 per person, random assignment). They test whether clearing a researcher accused of misconduct restores how people see him. Everyone first read that a "famous social psychologist ... at a prestigious university" studies some topic. The accused groups then read, in bold, that the researcher "was accused of reporting inconclusive data and that the ethics committee of his university demanded the retraction of a scientific article" (the paper's description of the stimulus, p. 4). All participants then rated the researcher on three items, each from −5 to +5 and averaged: is he a trustworthy researcher, do you believe his published findings, and should he receive further financing. After some filler items, the cleared group read, in bold, "that it turned out that Sanna and his colleagues were wrongly accused. None of the allegations were confirmed and so, eventually, the article was not retracted" (again the paper's description, p. 4). Then everyone answered the same three items again.

- **Study 1** (N = 185 adults; 117 women, 68 men; mean age 37.9) used a real case (Lawrence Sanna) and had three groups: accused then cleared (n = 68), accused and never cleared (n = 46), never accused (n = 71). The never-accused group also did not read the article summary. The accusation lowered the first rating: never-accused +1.54 against −0.16 and −0.30, p < .001. After clearing, the cleared group rose to +1.85. That is level with the never-accused group (+1.63, p = .536) and well above the not-cleared group (−0.28, p < .001) (pp. 5–6).
- **Study 2** (N = 358; 148 women, 210 men; mean age 32.1) used a fictitious researcher (Robert Miller). It added a second never-accused group that read the same article summary, so that this group differed from the accused groups only in the accusation. The cleared group went from +0.38 to +2.47. That is level with the matched never-accused group (+2.27, p = .508). It is also above the not-cleared group (+0.27) and above the never-accused group that got no article information (+0.96) (pp. 8–9).

The authors had predicted that clearing would restore the researcher only partly. Instead they found full restoration, and in neither study did the cleared researcher end reliably above the matched never-accused researcher.

### H1: trust drops when a flag is shown

- **Verdict:** Indirect support (by analogy)
- **Evidence:** Right after reading the accusation, people rated the researcher lower than people who read no accusation.
  - Study 2, clean comparison with the matched control: +2.35 (never accused, read the article) vs +0.38 and +0.15 (the two accused groups). That is about 2 points lower on the 11-point scale; d ≈ 0.97 and 1.06 **[reviewer calc]**. Contrast t(354) = 6.27, p < .001; F(3, 354) = 19.29, ηp² = .14 (p. 8). The accused groups were even below the never-accused group with no article information (+0.97).
  - Study 1: +1.54 vs −0.16 and −0.30, t(182) = 5.27, p < .001, ηp² = .13 (p. 5). The control here also lacked the article information, so this comparison is less clean.
- **How their situation differs from ours:**
  - The drop is between groups only. There is no rating before the accusation, so it is not a within-person drop as in our H1.
  - The accuser is a university ethics committee, a human institution with authority that had already demanded a retraction. Our accuser is an AI tool held by the rater, and it says "likely wrong".
  - The doubt is about honesty (misconduct), not about whether the content is correct.
  - The raters are anonymous online adults with no power over the researcher. One item asks whether he "should receive financing", a loose parallel to our budget.
  - In the story the accusation is later shown to be wrong, as our flags are, but participants did not know this at Rating 1.
  - What was measured was a 3-item attitude (trustworthy researcher, believe his findings, should be funded), which the authors call credibility. It is not a single trust item.
- **Quote:** "First, it is important to note that the manipulation of the accusation worked in that learning about academic misconduct led to a negative attitude toward the researcher." (General Discussion, p. 10)

### H2: trust recovers, and may "increase" above a never-flagged level, after a knowledgeable answer

Your two questions: should this paper ground H2, and does it argue against the "increase" part?

- **Verdict, recovery part** (trust rises above the post-flag level): **Indirect support (by analogy).** This is the strongest and closest-design evidence in the paper.
- **Verdict, "increase" part** (ends above a never-flagged person): **no support, and no contradiction.** None of the five labels fits exactly. The paper tested this comparison and found no reliable difference either way, and the cleared group was slightly higher in both studies. "Silent" would be wrong because they did measure it; "Contradicts" would be too strong.
- **Evidence, recovery:**
  - Only the cleared group changed between the two ratings. Study 1 went from −0.16 to +1.85 (+2.01 points), F(1, 67) = 37.99, p < .001, ηp² = .36 (p. 6). Study 2 went from +0.38 to +2.47 (+2.09 points), F(1, 93) = 57.55, p < .001, ηp² = .38 (p. 9).
  - As a within-person effect, that is dz ≈ 0.75 (Study 1) and 0.78 (Study 2) **[reviewer calc]**.
  - At Rating 2 the cleared group was far above the never-cleared group: Study 1 +1.85 vs −0.28, d ≈ 0.91; Study 2 +2.47 vs +0.27, d ≈ 0.95 **[reviewer calc]** (contrast tests on pp. 6 and 9).
- **Evidence, "increase":**
  - Cleared vs never accused at Rating 2: Study 1 was +1.85 vs +1.63, a difference of +0.22 points, p = .536 (p. 6). Study 2, against the matched control, was +2.47 vs +2.27, a difference of +0.20 points, t(354) = 0.66, p = .508 (p. 9).
  - Both gaps are about d ≈ 0.11 **[reviewer calc]**. The 95% range for the Study 2 gap is about −0.33 to +0.73 points **[reviewer calc]**. So a third-party clearing alone produced, at most, a small overshoot. An overshoot larger than about 0.7 points on the 11-point scale is unlikely under their conditions.
  - The authors tested the opposite direction: they expected the cleared researcher to end *below* the never-accused one ("this restoration, however, should be insufficient", p. 3), and were surprised to find equality.
  - **Trap for citation.** In Study 2 the cleared researcher *did* end well above the never-accused group without article information (+2.47 vs +0.96; contrast t(354) = 5.67, p < .001). The Study 2 Discussion states that the cleared group "had a more favorable post-exoneration attitude ... than participants in the ... no-article control conditions" (p. 9). The authors attribute this gap to the extra information (being an author of a published article), not to the clearing. The article alone raised ratings by about 1.4 points: +2.35 vs +0.97 at Rating 1, t(354) = 4.49, p < .001, d ≈ 0.89 **[reviewer calc]**. Citing this as "a cleared person ended above a never-accused one" would be a misreading.
  - The authors say the same about Study 1's small edge: "Yet, in Study 1 participants in the exoneration condition did tend to have a more favorable attitude toward the researcher than participants in the control condition. If the sample size was bigger and given greater statistical power, results might have revealed this finding to be significant." (p. 10). They read this as the missing article information in that control, not as a boost from being cleared.
- **What this means for our "increase" clause** (reviewer inference, not a finding of the paper): the paper's own data show how a "cleared" person can end above a never-flagged one, and it is not the clearing. It is new positive information that the comparison group did not get. In our design, the CISO's answer both clears the flag and adds evidence of company-specific knowledge, and the neutral group never hears that evidence. If our checker group ends above the neutral group, this paper predicts that the added information is the likely cause, not the flag-and-clear sequence itself.
- **How their situation differs from ours:**
  - **Who cleared:** an unnamed outside outcome ("None of the allegations were confirmed ... the article was not retracted"). The accused never replied. In ours, the accused (the CISO) clears himself with an explanation. The authors suggest their full recovery may be due to the believability of the correction's source (Guillory & Geraci, ref 17; p. 10). A self-explanation from the accused may be less believed than a neutral finding (reviewer inference).
  - **Who asked:** nobody. Participants were passive readers who took no action. In ours, the participant chooses to ask or to challenge.
  - **Content of the clearing:** it removed the charge and added nothing positive. Ours removes the charge *and* adds competence evidence.
  - **Kind of doubt:** honesty (misconduct), not correctness of content.
  - **Measure:** the 3-item attitude on −5 to +5, not a 0–10 trust rating. Both scales happen to have 11 points.
  - **Timing:** a single accusation and a single clearing within minutes, separated only by filler items. Ours has three flags across five slides.
  - **Correctness:** the accusation is wrong within the cleared-group story, as our flags are.
- **Quote (recovery):** "Overall, these analyses suggest that being an author of a scientific article increases the credibility of the researcher and that exonerating an accused researcher fully restores the researcher's credibility." (Study 2 Results, p. 9)
- **Quote ("increase" part):** "Unexpectedly, however, the post-exoneration attitude toward the researcher was very similar in the exoneration and the control condition." (General Discussion, p. 9)

### H3: denial or a non-answer makes trust fall further

- **Verdict:** Silent
- **Evidence:** No group heard a denial or a non-answer from the accused, who never speaks. The closest group is "accused, never cleared": the accusation simply stood with no reply. That group's rating stayed down but did not fall further over the few minutes between ratings. Study 1 went from −0.30 to −0.28, F(1, 45) = 0.02, p = .887 (p. 6). Study 2 went from +0.15 to +0.27, F(1, 77) = 1.94, p = .168 (p. 9). Denial appears only as an anecdote about the real Förster case ("Förster has denied that he manipulated any data", Introduction, p. 2), with no data.
- **How their situation differs from ours:** their "never cleared" group is an accusation left alone, not a question met with a bad answer. It is a possible reference point for our "say nothing" path: an unanswered doubt held steady, it did not deepen. But our "say nothing" group chooses itself, while theirs was assigned at random.
- **Quote:** none on denial or non-answers.

### H4: the change over the briefing depends on the participant's action and on the answers heard

- **Verdict:** Indirect support (by analogy), for the "answers heard" half only. Silent on the participant-action half.
- **Evidence:** Whether the accusation was followed by a clearing decided the direction of change. Only the cleared group moved, by about +2 points; the other groups stayed flat (tables above). Group × time interaction: Study 1 ηp² = .22 (printed as F(1, 182) = 25.32, p < .001, p. 5); Study 2 F(3, 354) = 47.27, p < .001, ηp² = .29 (p. 8). Participants chose nothing, so there is no evidence on the effect of the rater's own action.
- **How their situation differs from ours:** the information came from the experimenter's text, not from a reply to the participant's question. The participant was a passive reader, not a board member choosing to ask or to challenge.
- **Quote:** "Results revealed that the exoneration effectively worked, in that participants in the exoneration condition had a more favorable attitude (post-exoneration attitude) toward the researcher than did participants in the uncorrected accusation condition." (Abstract, p. 1)

## 3. Other relevant content

**The design is the closest template we have for H2.** The same people are rated after the doubt and again after the resolution, alongside two comparison groups: a doubt that is never resolved, and no doubt at all (pp. 3–4). Their Rating 1 is exactly our "after flag" rating, and their Rating 2 is our "after answer" rating. Our design is stronger in one way: we also have a rating before the flag (after the profile), which they do not.

**Lesson from Study 1 → Study 2: the comparison group must get the same information.** In Study 1 the never-accused group did not read the article summary. Study 2 showed that reading the article summary alone raised ratings by about 1.4 points (p. 8). The authors call this "an important inconsistency" (p. 10).

For us: the neutral group sees the same profile and slides, which is good. But the checker group also hears three CISO explanations that show company knowledge, and the neutral group never hears these. For the "ends above neutral" part of H2 this is the same confound (reviewer inference). One option, if the team wants to test "increase" cleanly, is a neutral-group version in which the same company-reason explanations appear without a preceding flag. That would be the analogue of their "article control".

**Measure wording** (Study 1 Method, p. 4). Three items, each from −5 to +5, averaged:

- "To what extent do you think that Lawrence Sanna is a trustworthy researcher? (anchors: not trustworthy, very trustworthy)"
- "To what degree do you believe empirical findings that are published by Lawrence Sanna? (anchors: don't believe at all, believe very much)"
- "To what extent should Lawrence Sanna receive financing for his future research? (anchors: no further financing, definitely further financing)"

Reliability α = .92 before / .94 after (Study 1) and .90 / .91 after (Study 2). The Study 2 post value is garbled in the extraction: "α = . pre-exoneration attitude toward the91" (p. 8). The funding item moved together with the trust and belief items. This suggests our budget-approval share may track trust closely (reviewer inference), so analyse budget share separately and do not fold it into trust.

**Stability of repeated ratings.** Groups that got no new information did not drift between two ratings a few minutes apart: changes of −0.08 to +0.12 points, all p > .11 (pp. 6, 9). This is useful support for taking repeated single ratings in our neutral group.

**The accusation made people disagree more.** SDs were about 2.2–2.7 in the accused groups vs about 1.4–1.8 in the never-accused groups (tables above). Expect larger spread in our checker group, which affects power.

**Effect sizes for our power calculation** (all **[reviewer calc]** from the reported numbers):

| Effect | Size |
|---|---|
| Accusation drop, between groups (Study 2, matched control) | about 2 points on the 11-point scale, d ≈ 1.0 |
| Recovery after clearing, within person | about +2 points, dz ≈ 0.75–0.78; about 16 people give 80% power in a within-person test |
| Cleared vs matched never-accused at the end | +0.2 points, d ≈ 0.11 |

With 30 per group, our study can detect only between-group differences of about d = 0.74 or larger (80% power, two-sided .05). Detecting d = 0.11 needs about 1,300 per group. So if the "ends above the neutral group" effect is anything like this paper's, our N cannot test it. The recovery part can be tested well.

**Procedure details worth copying:**

- Key information printed in bold (pp. 4, 7).
- Filler items between the two ratings, for example "How much do you generally hold on to your opinions?" (p. 4).
- A funnel question at the end, "what they thought this experiment was trying to study"; no one guessed (p. 4).
- A check for prior knowledge of the person (p. 4).
- A full debriefing that tells the truth (p. 5).
- The statement "there were no data exclusions, and all manipulations and all measures analyzed are reported" (p. 3).

**Explanations and moderators the authors discuss** (pp. 10–11):

- *Believability of the correction's source:* "That participants did not persevere in their beliefs might be due to the credibility of the source of the correcting feedback." In our study the correction comes from the accused himself (reviewer inference: this could make recovery weaker than theirs).
- *Vague accusation:* "the accusation of academic misconduct that was given to participants in our studies was rather vague ... so that the ethics committee's decision to demand a retraction might have been questioned." Our flags are also short and vague.
- *Explaining the accusation to oneself:* people who are prompted to explain why the accused did it hold on to the belief more (refs 10, 18). Their participants were not prompted. Reviewer inference: our "challenge" action, where the participant voices the checker's finding, may work like this and slow recovery. That is a testable moderator for H4.
- *Domain knowledge:* people inside academia may hold on to the accusation more. "Testing this possibility is an important avenue for future research." (p. 11). Our self-rated cybersecurity knowledge measure can test the analogous question.

**Stated limitations** (p. 11):

- An online sample.
- No attention check (in their earlier work under 1% failed). We should include one; the paper does not say this, it is my recommendation.
- Possible demand effects: "Participants in our samples were aware that the study in which they participated was carried out by researchers who—they might have assumed—expect or wish an exoneration to fully restore an accused researcher's credibility."
- Sample size was set from their previous study (at least 158), not by a power analysis (p. 4).

**Background they cite** (relevant to our notes sentence, not checked by me): their Introduction summarizes prior work as "corrections do successfully reduce beliefs in misinformation, although often insufficiently" (p. 3). Examples are ref 15 (Greitemeyer, 2014, on retracted findings, where debriefed readers ended between never-told and not-debriefed readers), the debriefing paradigm (refs 10–12), and the continued-influence effect (refs 5–9). These studies are mostly about beliefs in facts, not ratings of a person.

**Reporting errors** (none change the conclusions):

- The Study 1 interaction df (see above).
- The Study 2 no-article F/p mismatch (see above).
- The values labelled "MSE" in the within-group tests appear to be the effect mean squares, not error mean squares. For example, 68 × 2.01² / 2 ≈ 137 vs the printed 136.67 **[reviewer calc]**. Do not use them for power.
- The figures are not in the text file. All numbers above come from the text.

## 4. Check of our current sentences

**Sentence A (earlier draft):** "a researcher who was accused and then cleared was rated as credible as one never accused (Greitemeyer & Sagioglou, 2015)"

- "accused and then cleared": **accurate**, with one missing detail that matters for us. The clearing was a report that the allegations were not confirmed and the article was not retracted (p. 4). The researcher himself never replied.
- "rated as credible": **acceptable.** "Credibility" is the authors' own word (title, p. 9, p. 11). What was rated is a 3-item attitude: trustworthy as a researcher, belief in his findings, and whether he should get further funding (p. 4).
- "as one never accused": **accurate** in both studies, with no significant difference. Study 1: +1.85 vs +1.63, p = .536. Study 2 against the matched never-accused group: +2.47 vs +2.27, p = .508. The cleared group was slightly higher both times, not reliably. The sentence leaves out that this was a recovery: the cleared group had been about 2 points lower right after the accusation.
- **Suggested wording:** "In two online experiments (N = 185 and 358), a researcher accused of misconduct and then cleared by a report that the allegations were not confirmed recovered fully. He was rated as trustworthy, as believable and as deserving of funding as a researcher never accused, but not reliably higher (Greitemeyer & Sagioglou, 2015)."

**Sentence B (working notes):** "Prior work found that a cleared person is rated at best as before."

- **Not accurate as a description of this paper.** This paper found the cleared person rated the *same* as a never-accused one, slightly higher in both studies (+0.22 and +0.20 points on an 11-point scale, not significant). "At best" implies a proven ceiling, but the paper only failed to find a difference; it did not show that ending higher is impossible. Also, "as before" does not fit: the paper has no rating from before the accusation. Its comparison is with a separate never-accused group.
- **Partly right as a summary of the older work this paper reviews.** That work says corrections reduce the effect of false information "although often insufficiently" (p. 3), which is "below before", not "at best as before". That work is mostly about beliefs in facts, not ratings of a person. This paper is the counter-example: full recovery.
- **Suggested wording:** "Earlier work found that corrections usually weaken the effect of a false claim but often do not remove it (reviewed in Greitemeyer & Sagioglou, 2015). In their own two experiments, a researcher cleared of misconduct by an outside finding was rated as favorably as a never-accused researcher, but not reliably more favorably."

## 5. Bottom line

Yes, bring this paper back as the grounding for the *recovery* part of H2 and as the closest design to ours. It rates people after a doubt and again after it is resolved, with a random-assigned "doubt never resolved" group and a "never doubted" group. It also gives indirect support for H1 and for the "answers heard" half of H4.

It does not argue against the "increase" part of H2, but it gives no support for it either. A third-party clearing alone brought the rating back to the never-accused level (Study 2: +2.47 vs +2.27, p = .508), with no reliable overshoot. The only time a cleared researcher beat a never-accused one was when the comparison group lacked positive information (the article) that the cleared group had.

The one fact that matters most for us: after the clearing, ratings rose by about 2 points on an 11-point scale, and the clearing came from an outside finding, not from the accused. Our CISO clears himself *and* adds new evidence of competence. Any "increase" we find therefore has to be separated from that added information, and with 30 per group we can detect only a large one.
