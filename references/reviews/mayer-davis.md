# Reviewer report: Mayer & Davis (1999)

Mayer, R. C., & Davis, J. H. (1999). The effect of the performance appraisal system on trust for management: A field quasi-experiment. *Journal of Applied Psychology, 84*(1), 123–136.

Source: pdftotext of a scan (about 11,000 words), read in full, including Tables 1–7 and the Appendix. Page numbers are journal pages. The OCR errors that affect the numbers are flagged where they occur. The main ones: Greek letters are garbled (t appears as "7", "f" or "r"; β appears as "j3"), two tables are scrambled, and the Appendix is printed in two columns, so the scale headings are interleaved.

### 1. The paper in one paragraph

This is one 14-month field study in a small non-union plastics manufacturer in the rural US Midwest. Production employees and supervisors filled in paper surveys during work time in three waves, with 166, 170 and 193 respondents (68%, 75% and 82% of production staff). Mean age was 36.8, mean tenure 3.7 years, and 45% were women (p. 126). Wave 2 came 5 months after Wave 1. Between Wave 2 and Wave 3, which were 9 months apart, top management replaced the performance appraisal system. The new one was an off-the-shelf system that added a self-appraisal and a required meeting where the supervisor and employee reconcile their two appraisals (p. 127). Employees are appraised on the anniversary of their hire date, so month of hire decided who had been appraised under the new system by Wave 3. That gave an "experienced" group (n = 22) and an "inexperienced" control group (n = 57), N = 79 people with usable surveys in both waves (pp. 127, 129). The authors call this assignment close to random.

Measures, all on a 1–5 disagree/agree scale:
- trust for top management, defined as willingness to be vulnerable (4 items)
- perceived ability (6), benevolence (5) and integrity (6) of top management
- propensity to trust (8)
- perceived accuracy of the appraisal (8) and outcome instrumentality, i.e. the belief that performance leads to pay and promotion (3)

Main results:
- Across the whole workforce, trust rose modestly from 2.85 at Wave 2 to 3.04 at Wave 3, t(359) = 2.66, p < .01, d = .28 (p. 129; means in Table 1, p. 128).
- In the quasi-experiment, trust in the experienced group rose from 2.87 to 3.24. In the control group it went from 2.94 to 2.97 (Table 4, p. 130).
- Group membership predicted Wave 3 trust with Wave 2 trust, age, tenure, gender and propensity held constant: β = .20, p < .05. The experienced group ended about 0.32 points higher (B = .324; Table 6, p. 131).
- No single trustworthiness factor differed significantly between the groups (ability ns; benevolence p < .07; integrity p < .08; pp. 130–131).
- In a path analysis on Wave 3 data (n = 185), the effect of appraisal perceptions on trust ran entirely through ability, benevolence and integrity. This is full mediation (pp. 131–132, Table 7).

### 2. Verdict per hypothesis

#### H1: trust drops when a VLM flag is shown

- **Verdict:** Silent
- **Evidence:** The paper never induces or measures a drop in trust. No event lowers perceived ability, benevolence or integrity, and no trust decline is reported between any waves. The only related content is theory the authors summarise from Mayer et al. (1995). Trustworthiness is reassessed downward when the trustor feels "let down". Separately, "exogenous" incidents that prompt a reappraisal of ability, benevolence or integrity change trustworthiness (p. 124). This is framing only and is not tested in the paper.
- **How their situation differs from ours:**
  - Nobody checks the trustee (top management). The only check in the study is management's appraisal of employees, which runs the opposite way.
  - The trustors are low-power employees and the trustee is a high-power group. In our study the trustor (board member) holds the formal power over the trustee (CISO).
  - No third party makes any claim, correct or wrong.
  - Trust is measured over months, not minutes.
- **Quote (theory, not a finding):** "Because trustworthiness is affected by three distinct factors (ability, benevolence, and integrity), incidents or developments that prompt a reappraisal of any of these perceptions of the trustee will impact trustworthiness." (p. 124)

#### H2: trust recovers and can increase after the CISO's knowledgeable answer

- **Verdict:** Indirect support (by analogy), weak.
- **Evidence:** trust means (1 = disagree strongly, 5 = agree strongly; 3 = "neither agree nor disagree").

| Wave 2 (month 5) | all respondents (Table 1 uses pairwise deletion) | 2.85 (SD 0.68) | Table 1, p. 128 |

| Wave 2 → Wave 3 | control ("inexperienced"), n = 57 | 2.94 → 2.97 (change +.03) | Table 4 and text, p. 130 |
| Wave 2 → Wave 3 | experienced, n = 22 | 2.87 → 3.24 (change +.36 in the text; +.37 from the rounded table means) | Table 4 and text, p. 130; Figure 1 |

  - What changed between Wave 2 and Wave 3: in focus groups before the change, employees said the old appraisal system did not reflect their real performance and that performance was not linked to pay or promotion (pp. 126–127). Top management chose a new system without involving employees. It added a self-appraisal, a supervisor–employee reconciliation meeting, and discussion of the specific behaviours being rated (p. 127).
  - The change worked on the targeted perceptions. With Wave 2 levels held constant, the experienced group rated accuracy higher (t(75) = 2.75, p < .01) and instrumentality higher (t(75) = 3.22, p < .01) at Wave 3 (Table 3, p. 130). Group means: accuracy 3.48 → 3.57 (experienced) against 3.56 → 3.30 (control); instrumentality 3.19 → 3.64 against 3.05 → 2.98 (Table 4).
  - Before the change, the groups did not differ on trust (2.94 against 2.87, t(77) = 0.37) or on any other variable (Table 5, p. 130). Among 58 people also present at Wave 1, their trust was not already changing at different rates between Wave 1 and Wave 2 (t(55) = −.48, ns; p. 131).
  - Trustworthiness factors, Wave 2 → Wave 3 (Table 4): ability 3.57 → 3.67 (experienced) against 3.45 → 3.37 (control); integrity 3.33 → 3.60 against 3.07 → 3.12; benevolence 3.15 → 3.39 against 2.95 → 3.02. The OCR places the 3.39 at the end of the table, so this value is my best reading. None of these group differences was significant (Table 6).
  - The paper reports no separate test of whether the experienced group's own rise was significant. The test is the between-group regression.
  - Bearing on H2: the paper shows that trust in a more powerful party rose when that party acted on the source of the trustors' dissatisfaction. The rise was modest, and it held only for people who saw the fix firsthand. The experienced group ended above both its own earlier level and the untreated control group, which loosely parallels "ending above a never-flagged person". That is the analogy, and it is weak for four reasons:
    1. There was no measured drop before the rise. The Wave 1 mean is not reported, and the "doubt" was a long-standing grievance, not an event.
    2. The trustee fixed things with a new system; it did not answer a question with an explanation.
    3. The grievance was treated as valid and management conceded by changing the system. In our study the flag is wrong and the CISO is vindicated.
    4. The rise took 9 months and was small: about a third of a point on a 5-point scale, ending barely above "neither agree nor disagree".
- **How their situation differs from ours:**
  - Direction of the check is reversed. In the paper, the appraisal is management's check on employees, and the new system let the *checked* employees reply (self-appraisal plus reconciliation). The trust measured was the checked party's trust in the checker's owner. We measure the checker-owner's (board member's) trust in the checked party (CISO).
  - The trustee is a management team, not one person.
  - The trustors have less power than the trustee.
  - Trust is measured as willingness to be vulnerable (4 items), not a single 0–10 rating.
- **Quote:** "Trust for top management rose significantly in response to the new performance appraisal system." (Discussion, p. 133)

#### H3: trust falls further after a denial or non-answer

- **Verdict:** Silent
- **Evidence:** There is no denial, non-answer or failed-response condition. The closest content is an untested argument (p. 126): when management says it rewards performance but the appraisal system does not, its "statements and actions are discrepant" and it will be seen as having lower integrity. The matching hypothesis (the paper's H4, on integrity) was not supported: the group effect on integrity was t = 1.78, p < .08 (Table 6, pp. 130–131). The control group's perceived appraisal accuracy fell from 3.56 to 3.30 (Table 4), but its trust did not fall (2.94 → 2.97).
- **How their situation differs from ours:** No one is confronted and no one answers. The only "non-response" is that the control group simply had not been appraised yet. It had nothing to do with the trustee refusing to reply.
- **Quote:** None that reports a relevant finding.

#### H4: the trust change depends on the participant's action and the answers heard

- **Verdict:** Indirect support (by analogy), weak, and only for the "heard an answer vs. did not" part. The paper is silent on the trustor's own choice of action.
- **Evidence:** The trust change depended on whether the person had direct exposure to the trustee's corrective action: +.36 against +.03 (Table 4, p. 130), group β = .20, p < .05 (Table 6). The authors argue that hearing about the new system from colleagues was not enough. If it had been, the control group's views would have improved, yet its accuracy and instrumentality ratings fell (pp. 133–134, "diffusion of the treatment"). By analogy, a participant who says nothing after a flag gets no answer and so has no firsthand evidence to revise trust upward.
- **How their situation differs from ours:**
  - Exposure was decided by hire month, not by the trustor's choice. In our study the action is self-selected, so people who choose "challenge" may already differ in trust before the flag.
  - The paper checked that its groups were equal at the start (Table 5) and were not already drifting apart (p. 131). We would need the same check on pre-flag trust across the chosen actions.
  - No trustor action (asking or challenging) exists in the paper.
- **Quote:** "The quasi-experiment described in this study shows that the overall rise in trust for top management was driven by the reactions of the employees who had actual experience with the improved performance appraisal system." (p. 133)

### 3. Other relevant content

**A. The questionnaire (Appendix, p. 136; Measures, p. 127; Table 1, p. 128)**

- **Instructions, verbatim:** "Indicate the degree to which you agree with each statement by using the following scale: 1 Disagree strongly, 2 Disagree, 3 Neither agree nor disagree, 4 Agree, 5 Agree strongly." The OCR lost the numbers 3 and 5; the five labels are intact. Then: "Think about [company name]'s top management team [names listed in parentheses for clarity]. For each statement, write the number that best describes how much you agree or disagree with each statement."
- "The anchors shown below were consistent throughout. Headings of construct names are for clarity of exposition, and were not included in the surveys." (p. 136). Respondents did not see which scale an item belonged to.
- **Origin:** shortened from Schoorman, Mayer and Davis (1996a), an unpublished conference paper with 10 ability, 12 benevolence, 13 integrity and 4 trust items. Those full scales had alphas of .93, .95, .96 and .82 (p. 127). The shortening was done "Based on concerns expressed by the company's management about the length of the survey … the items that most clearly reflected each theoretical dimension were selected" (p. 127). The authors also "altered slightly" the items "to reflect a focus on top management" (p. 127). This 1999 appendix is where the short items are published ("All of the previously unpublished scales used in this study can be found in the Appendix", p. 127), so citing Mayer & Davis (1999) for them is correct.
- **Reliability:** the table below gives Cronbach's alpha, which shows how consistently the items of one scale agree with each other. As background knowledge (not from the paper), .70 or higher is the usual minimum.

- **OCR note:** in the scan the Ability and Trust headings sit side by side in two columns. I assigned items by heading, item count (6 and 4, matching Table 1) and content. The asterisks for reversed items survive in the OCR. The instruction "Think about the performance review system at [company name]…" appears among the benevolence items but belongs to the accuracy and instrumentality items.
- **Internal inconsistency:** p. 127 says the Schoorman et al. propensity scale had 7 items, then that "the entire eight-item scale" was used. Table 1 and the Appendix show 8. This does not matter for us.
- **Low reliability of the trust scale** (pp. 128, 133):
  - Its alpha was only .59–.60, against .82 in the earlier study.
  - The authors attribute this mainly to its shortness: "the major reason alpha is low in this sample appears to be the length of the scale" (p. 128).
  - Its stability over time in the control group was .75 over 5 months and .66 over 9 months (p. 128).
  - They argue the low alpha, if it is random noise, makes their trust result conservative.
  - They say "further developmental work with this measure of trust is warranted" (p. 133).

**B. Trust is not the same as the three factors, and this matters for our "check"**

- The trust score correlated about .6 with each factor: at Wave 2, .61 with ability, .60 with benevolence and .58 with integrity (Table 1). The text says "approximately .6" (p. 133).
- A four-factor model fit better than a single-factor model: CFI .96/.94; chi-square difference 133.85 and 100.61, p < .01 (Table 2, p. 129).
- **Trust rose significantly while none of the three factors did:** "Although no single factor went up significantly because of the new appraisal system, trust for top management did rise significantly." (p. 134)
- Practical consequence for us (my inference): a start-vs-end change in the ability/benevolence/integrity questionnaire may not come out significant even when the 0–10 rating moves. The check that the single rating tracks trust is better done as a correlation between the rating and the questionnaire at each time point. A test of whether both changed is a weaker check.
- An expected correlation of about .6 with each factor is the paper's benchmark for trust against trustworthiness.

**C. Fit of the items to a board member rating a CISO (my assessment, based on the item wording)**

- Ability fits best. The flags ("Architecture likely wrong", "Numbers do not add up") and the CISO's answers target perceived competence. The definition makes ability domain-specific: "that group of skills, competencies, and characteristics that allow a party to have influence within some domain" (p. 124). "Numbers do not add up" may also touch integrity.
- The authors say the weight of each factor depends on the situation: "In some situations, the trustee's ability may be much more important than the other two factors." (p. 124). Their Wave 3 path estimates suggest integrity and benevolence weighed more than ability on trust for top management. Table 7 is OCR-scrambled; my best reading is unstandardized paths to trust of about .14–.15 for ability, .23–.24 for benevolence and .29–.30 for integrity.
- Benevolence items assume the trustee can help or hurt the respondent personally ("my welfare", "go out of its way to help me"). For a board member overseeing a CISO the power runs the other way, so these read oddly.
- Two trust items assume the trustee has power over the respondent ("complete control over my future in this company", "wouldn't let top management have any influence…"). They need rewriting, not just a name swap.
- "I really wish I had a good way to keep an eye on top management" (reverse-scored) measures the wish to monitor the trustee. **In the checker group the checker *is* a way to keep an eye on the CISO**, so this item may react to the manipulation itself rather than to trust. Either drop it or report the results with and without it.
- "I would be comfortable giving [the CISO] a task or problem which was critical to me, even if I could not monitor their actions" fits the board–CISO relation very well.
- The theory requires the trustee to be "specific, identifiable, and perceived to act with volition" (p. 124). A single named CISO satisfies this better than a management team does. Following the paper's instruction ("names listed in parentheses for clarity"), our instructions should name the CISO from the profile.

**D. Design elements worth borrowing**

- **Untreated control group with pretests at more than one time point** (Cook & Campbell), which "becomes much stronger when additional pretests are added" (p. 127). Our neutral group is the untreated control. My suggestion: do not put a flag on slide 1. The profile rating plus at least one post-slide rating before the first flag then give two pretests.
- **Equality check at the start and pre-trend check:** groups were compared at baseline on trust, the three factors, propensity, age, tenure and gender (Table 5). They also checked that the groups were not already changing at different rates (p. 131). We should do both for checker against neutral, and for the self-chosen action groups within the checker group.
- **Analysis:** end trust was predicted from start trust plus group, with covariates (Table 6, p. 130). This is the same main comparison we plan (checker against neutral, holding the first rating constant).
- **Administration:** anonymous surveys in on-site meetings run by a researcher, for confidentiality (p. 126).
- **Covariates:** propensity to trust was measured and did not predict Wave 3 trust (t = 0.26, Table 6). Its scale had low reliability (.55/.66). It is optional for us.

**E. Effect sizes for a power calculation (use with care; months-long field setting, not minutes)**

- Whole workforce, Wave 2 vs Wave 3: trust rose 0.19 points on the 1–5 scale; the paper labels this "significant, albeit modest" (d = .28; p. 129).
- Quasi-experiment: the experienced group rose about 0.33 points more than the control group (+.36 against +.03). With 22 against 57 people this reached p < .05 (group β = .20; B = .32 points; Table 6).
- My own rough derivation, not in the paper: trust scores spread by about 0.67 (SD), and the control group's Wave 2–Wave 3 stability was .66. The spread of individual change scores would then be about 0.55, and the 0.33-point group gap is roughly 0.6 of that spread.

**F. Stated limitations to acknowledge or avoid**

- Low reliability of the 4-item trust scale (pp. 128, 133).
- Heavy attrition: only 79 people had both waves, and people whose trust fell may have left or skipped the survey (p. 134).
- Diffusion of the treatment to the control group (pp. 133–134).
- One small company where employees had some contact with top management (p. 134).
- No access to raise data, so they could not rule out that the experienced group got better outcomes (p. 133).
- **They argue against lab studies:** studying this "in a laboratory setting would yield dubious results at best, given the noted concerns with artificiality" (p. 133). They also quote Cardy and Dobbins: "most laboratory studies have a definite artificial character" (p. 133). Our online role-play with students should address this in its limitations.
- **Caution relevant to H2's time scale:** the authors "strongly agree" with Tyler and Degoey that "attempting to build trust is neither quick nor easy" (p. 133). Our H2 expects a rise within seconds of an answer, which is a very different time scale.

**G. Recommended future work that our study partly carries out**

- "How the factors are combined to influence trust warrants further research." (p. 134). Our flags target ability, and possibly integrity, within one short interaction, so we could report which factor items move.
- "whether trust conceptualized as a willingness to be vulnerable consists of multiple dimensions" (p. 133).
- A larger organization (p. 134).
- Procedural against distributive justice (p. 133). This is not relevant to us.

**H. Theory worth citing as framing**

- Trust is "a willingness to be vulnerable to the actions of another party" (Mayer, Davis, & Schoorman, 1995, as summarised on p. 124).
- Trust develops in two ways (p. 124):
  1. through feedback from the outcomes of earlier vulnerability;
  2. through outside "incidents or developments" that prompt a reappraisal of ability, benevolence or integrity.
- A checker flag and a CISO answer are both such incidents. This gives a citable reason why H1 and H2 should move trust through perceived ability. It is the authors' theory and was not tested in this paper.

### 4. Check of our current sentence

**Sentence A:** "Trust questionnaire at the start and the end, adapted from Mayer and Davis (1999), to check that the single rating measures trust."

- "adapted from Mayer and Davis (1999)": **accurate.** The items are published in its Appendix (p. 136). The paper itself adapted earlier items to a new referent (p. 127), which is a precedent for rewording.
- "Trust questionnaire": **imprecise.** The paper separates 4 *trust* items (willingness to be vulnerable) from 17 items on perceived *ability, benevolence and integrity*. It argues these are distinct constructs (p. 134). If we take only ability, benevolence and integrity items, we measure perceived trustworthiness, not trust in the paper's sense.
- "to check that the single rating measures trust": **not something the paper tests.** It does not examine single-item ratings. It does give a benchmark: trust correlates about .6 with each factor. It also gives a warning: its own trust scale was unreliable (.59–.60), and trust changed while the factors did not significantly change. The check should therefore be a correlation at each time point, not a comparison of changes.
- **Suggested wording:** "A short questionnaire at the start and the end, with items from Mayer and Davis (1999) reworded for the CISO, covering perceived ability and integrity of the CISO and willingness to rely on the CISO, to check that the single 0–10 rating goes together with these."

**Sentence B:** "Mayer and Davis (1999) has 6 ability, 5 benevolence and 6 integrity items on a 5-point agree/disagree scale."

- **Accurate** (p. 127; Table 1, p. 128; Appendix, p. 136).
- **Incomplete:** it leaves out the 4 trust items, which are the paper's measure of trust itself, and the 8 propensity items. It also omits that one integrity item and two trust items are reverse-scored, and that the scale runs 1 = disagree strongly to 5 = agree strongly with 3 = neither.
- **Suggested wording:** "Mayer and Davis (1999) measured perceived ability (6 items), benevolence (5 items) and integrity (6 items) of top management, and trust itself (4 items), each rated from 1 (disagree strongly) to 5 (agree strongly). Ability, benevolence and integrity were reliable (alpha .82–.89), the four trust items less so (.59–.60)."

**Sentence C:** "The items refer to 'top management' and have to be reworded for 'the CISO'."

- **Accurate:** every ability, benevolence, integrity and trust item names "top management", and respondents were told to think about the named top management team (p. 136).
- **Incomplete:** swapping the name is not enough.
  - "Top management … its job" is a group and needs singular wording.
  - Several items assume the trustee has power over the respondent, which is reversed for a board member: "my welfare", "complete control over my future in this company", "wouldn't let top management have any influence over issues that are important to me".
  - "I really wish I had a good way to keep an eye on [the CISO]" will likely react to the checker itself.
- **Suggested wording:** "The items refer to 'top management' as seen by its employees; we reword them for 'the CISO' as seen by a board member, and drop or rewrite items that assume the trustee has power over the respondent."

**Sentence D:** "A selection of six items is enough for the check."

- **Not supported by the paper, and its evidence points the other way for very short scales.**
- The paper did shorten its scales, but only to 5–6 items per factor. It then confirmed by factor analysis that the short forms stayed distinct (pp. 127–129).
- Its only very short scale, the 4 trust items, had low reliability (.59–.60), which the authors blame mainly on its length (p. 128).
- Six items spread over three or four factors (one or two each) will not give reliable per-factor scores. At most they can form one combined score whose alpha we would have to report.
- **Suggested wording:** "We use a shortened selection of the items, as Mayer and Davis (1999) did because of survey length. Because their four-item trust scale had low reliability (alpha .59–.60), we report the selected items as one combined score and give its alpha."
- My own suggestion, not from the paper: if time allows, use the 6 ability items (alpha .85–.88), which match what the flags attack, plus the reworded trust items.

### 5. Bottom line

Cite this paper as the **source of the measure** (the Appendix items, the 1–5 agree/disagree format, and the alphas), and for the ability/benevolence/integrity framing of why a flag and an answer should move trust. For **H2** it is only a weak analogy. Trust in a more powerful party rose modestly, from 2.87 to 3.24 on a 1–5 scale against 2.94 to 2.97 in the control group, over 9 months after the trustee fixed a real grievance. It is not a drop-and-recovery after a wrong claim, and the Wave 1 trust mean is not reported. The one fact that matters most for us: **trust rose significantly while none of ability, benevolence or integrity did, and the four-item trust scale had low reliability (.59–.60).** So the questionnaire should be used as a correlation check on the 0–10 rating, with more than a token number of items, and not as a second test of change.
