## 1. The paper in one paragraph

This is one laboratory role-play experiment with a within-person comparison of two workers. The participants were 40 male undergraduates at the University of North Carolina (psychology, sociology and business administration classes), split into Group I and Group II with 20 each (Table 2, p. 206). Each participant played a supervisor behind a one-way screen. He managed two workers, A and B, who did "monotonous arithmetic problems" in 30-second work periods. The workers were fictitious, but the participant believed they were real. He could fine a worker 10¢ of a possible $1.00 wage for falling below his output standard of 5 problems per period. He could win $10.00 or $5.00 (OCR "|1000 or $5 00") if output stayed as high in Part II as in Part I (pp. 202–203). **Manipulated:** in Part I (10 periods) the experimenter's schedule had the supervisor check A after 9 periods (1–4, 6–10) and B after only 2 (periods 5 and 10) (p. 203). Both workers produced the same total output, and every check he made showed the standard met. In Group I, neither worker ever fell below the standard. In Group II, each fell below once, and only in a period that was not checked: A in period 5 and B in period 3 (Table 1 and p. 204). **Measured:** Questionnaire I after Part I contained (a) one relative trust item: "trust B more than A / A more than B / equally" (p. 206), (b) a forced choice of which worker is "more dependable" (p. 209), and (c) an open-ended question on "the reasons for A's (B's) successfully meeting the output minimum". Seven judges coded the answers as internal or external cause (p. 207). In Part II (10 more periods), the supervisor had a grading task and could check either worker whenever he chose, one per period. Every check again showed 5 problems (p. 205). Questionnaire II after Part II asked about his confidence in each worker (confident / not confident / not sure) and whom he would have checked more if forced to choose (pp. 205, 211). **Main results:** (1) In Part II, supervisors checked A on average 0.55 more times than B in both groups (Group I t = 2.39, p = .015; Group II t = 2.20, p = .021; Table 2, p. 206). (2) More supervisors said they trusted the rarely checked B more than the often-checked A than said the reverse, with ties split (exact binomial p = .039 in Group I and < .001 in Group II; Table 3, pp. 206–207; the counts are unreadable). (3) When forced to choose, most rated B as "more dependable": 18 of 20 in Group I and 19 of 20 in Group II, both p < .001 (Table 6, p. 210). (4) Most said they would have picked A for more checking (p = .013 and .032; Table 7, p. 210). (5) The proposed explanation was that supervisors attributed A's compliance to their own checking and B's compliance to B himself. The link was only "indicated", not confirmed (Tables 4 and 5, pp. 208–209). (6) Checking in Part II went with relative confidence in Group I (p = .038) but not in Group II (p = .208) (Table 8, p. 211). Groups I and II did not differ significantly on any main proportion (p. 205).

## 2. Verdict per hypothesis

### H1: trust in the CISO drops when a (simulated VLM) claim is displayed

- **Verdict:** `Indirect support (by analogy)`
- **Evidence:**
  - *Relative trust (Table 3, pp. 206–207; N = 20 per group):* at the end of Part I, more supervisors said they trusted the rarely checked worker B more than the often-checked worker A than said the reverse. Answers of "equal trust" were split half to each side. The exact binomial p is **.039 in Group I** and **< .001 in Group II**. The raw counts in Table 3 are not readable in the OCR. Reconstructed: Group I's p = .039 is exactly the average of the binomial tails for 14 and for 15 out of 20 (.058 and .021). That fits 14.5 of 20 (about 72%) favoring B after the split. The same averaging reproduces the printed Group I p = .013 in Table 7 from the counts given in the text, which supports this reading. Group II's p < .001 requires at least 17.5 of 20 (about 88%) favoring B.
  - *Dependability, forced choice (Table 6, p. 210):* B was seen as more dependable by **18 of 20 in Group I** and **19 of 20 in Group II** after splitting non-answers, both p < .001. Reconstructed for Group I: 16 chose B, 0 chose A and 4 did not answer (split 2 and 2).
  - *Continued checking (Table 2, p. 206):* when free to choose in Part II, supervisors checked A more often than B. The mean excess was 0.55 checks out of 10 periods in each group. The standard error was .23 in Group I (t = 2.39, p = .015) and .25 in Group II (t = 2.20, p = .021). The p values appear to be one-tailed: the column header reads "{\-tatLtest)", probably "(1-tail test)", and the values match one-tailed t(19). By my calculation, this is a within-person effect of about half a standard deviation (0.53 and 0.49). Individual counts (p. 211): in Group I, 9 checked A more, 1 checked B more and 10 checked both equally; in Group II, the counts were 11, 3 and 6.
  - All of this held although both workers produced identical totals (52 and 52 in Group I; 50 and 50 in Group II; Table 1) and every check came back clean.
- **How their situation differs from ours:**
  - *Who checks whom:* the participant (a supervisor) checks the workers himself; no machine or third party is involved. In our study, a machine held by the board member checks the CISO.
  - *Power:* the checker holds formal power (he can fine) over workers doing a simple task, and there is no gap in expertise. In ours, the board member has power but the CISO has the expertise.
  - *Correctness and content of the check:* Strickland's checks were **correct and always positive**: the worker had met the standard. Nothing negative was ever claimed. Trust fell because of the checking itself, not because of any claim. In ours, the checker makes **wrong, negative** claims. Strickland therefore does not test what a wrong accusation does.
  - *Reply:* the workers never replied or explained anything.
  - *What was measured:* trust in a worker's motivation and reliability ("loyalty, reliability, or trustworthiness", p. 202), measured once after 10 periods of checking. It was a **relative** judgment (A vs B) with three options (A more, B more, equal). There was no rating before checking and no condition without checking. Ours is an absolute 0–10 rating of an expert, taken right after each flag and compared with a neutral group.
  - *Timing:* Strickland's effect built up over 9 checks against 2. H1 is about a drop right after a single flag.
- **Quote:** "Ss showed less trust of the more highly surveyed subordinate than of the infrequently surveyed subordinate." (Summary, finding 2, p. 215; the OCR reads "subordmate")

### H2: trust recovers or rises after the CISO answers a question with a knowledgeable explanation

- **Verdict:** `Silent`
- **Evidence:** none. The fictitious workers never spoke, explained or answered. No trust was measured after a check was answered. The paper's framework (trust follows when the observer attributes good behavior to something inside the other person) and its discussion are *consistent with* H2, but they are theory, not data. The discussion argues that a superior who never got to test "a needed characteristic or skill" in another person may assume it is missing (p. 214). A knowledgeable answer from the CISO would be exactly such a test. This paper does not test that.
- **How their situation differs from ours:** there was no channel for the checked person to respond, and no recovery phase.
- **Quote (closest statement, theory only):** "If the previous interaction context has been such that the individual has not been able (or has neglected) to test the existence in another of a needed characteristic or skill, the individual may be forced to infer that it is not present and so modify his own behavior." (Discussion, p. 214)

### H3: trust falls further if the CISO answers with a denial or non-answer

- **Verdict:** `Silent`
- **Evidence:** none. There were no denials, no non-answers and no replies of any kind.
- **How their situation differs from ours:** the checked party was mute and fictitious.
- **Quote:** none relevant.

### H4: the change in trust depends on the participant's action and the CISO's answers

- **Verdict:** `Silent`
- **Evidence:** no data bear on this. The paper's central thesis is that the supervisor's own checking behavior shapes his later trust: he "may become victimized by his own previous supervisory behavior", leading to "a self-perpetuating information loss" (p. 201). But the checking that changed trust (Part I) was **assigned by the experimenter**, not chosen by the participant, and no answers were heard. The one chosen behavior (Part II checking) ran from trust to checking, not from checking to a change in trust. It went with relative confidence in Group I (N = 20, p = .038), not in Group II (N = 19, p = .208), and in the combined data (N = 39, p = .011). The author gave this only "tentative acceptance" (Table 8, pp. 211–212). Confidence was measured after Part II, so the order of cause and effect cannot be read from these data.
- **How their situation differs from ours:** there was no menu of actions such as asking or challenging, and the checked person gave no reply.
- **Quote (theory only):** "The supervisor, who presumably is concerned with the degree to which his subordinates will work without constant supervision, may become victimized by his own previous supervisory behavior." (Introduction, p. 201)

## 3. Other relevant content

**Design elements worth borrowing or citing**
- **Equal performance, only the checking differs.** Both workers had identical output totals, and every check showed the standard met. The only difference was how often each was checked (Table 1, pp. 203–204). This is the same logic as our "the slide contains no error; only the flag differs". Strickland is an early precedent for holding the checked person's actual performance constant.
- **Believability of simulated people.** The participant read the instructions aloud to the fictitious workers over a sound system, "to minimize the probability that he might suspect that there were no subordinates" (p. 203). Everyone got a full debriefing at the end (p. 205). The paper reports **no suspicion check**, so we do not know how many participants saw through the setup (reviewer's observation). Our simulated CISO and checker should include one.
- **A second task to limit checking.** In Part II, the grading task made constant checking costly, so the participant had to rely on his judgment of each worker (pp. 204–205). This is comparable to our board member having to decide whether to act on a flag at all.

**Measures and wording**
- Trust item (p. 206): the participant "could state that he held greater trust in B than A, greater trust in A than B, or equal trust in both". The full item wording is not reproduced in the paper.
- Dependability (p. 209): a forced choice with no "equal" option. It found a difference in almost everyone (18/20 and 19/20). With "equal" allowed, many participants did not pick a side. Every participant who trusted B more also saw B as more dependable (p. 210). **Lesson for us:** relative and forced formats inflate the apparent size of the effect. Our 0–10 absolute rating avoids that. It also means Strickland's percentages cannot be carried over to our scale.
- Confidence item (p. 211): asked separately for each worker: "he thought he could have confidence in the subordinate at the beginning of Part II / he did not think he could / he wasn't sure".
- Cause-of-compliance item (p. 207): open-ended: "the reasons for A's (B's) successfully meeting the output minimum". Seven judges, blind to how the participant had checked, sorted answers into internal, external, both, or not classifiable, and at least 5 of 7 had to agree. A multiple-choice version was dropped after the first 6 participants per group because it "yielded practically no range of scores". **Possible borrowing:** an open-ended question after the CISO's answer ("Why do you think the CISO's slide was built this way?"), coded for whether the participant credits the CISO's own knowledge. This would test the mechanism behind H2.

**Uncertainty rather than distrust**
- "There was some evidence that S's more frequent monitoring of A was a function of his not being sure about having confidence in the subordinate instead of his actually lacking confidence in him, insofar as only one S made the latter declaration" (p. 213). Only 1 of 40 said he did *not* have confidence in a worker. The others were confident or unsure. **For us:** a drop on a 0–10 trust rating after a flag may reflect uncertainty rather than distrust. The Mayer & Davis questionnaire, or an added "how sure are you" item, could separate the two.

**A caution for the H1 comparison (reviewer's inference)**
- In Strickland, trust in the checked person fell although every check came back clean. So the mere presence of checking may lower trust. Our checker group differs from the neutral group in two ways: the checker is present, and it raises flags. The comparison with the neutral group at the same slide therefore mixes both. The before/after-flag comparison within the checker group isolates the flag. We should decide what the checker shows on the two unflagged slides (for example "no issues found") and compare flagged with unflagged slides within the checker group.

**Checking leads to more checking**
- When free to choose, supervisors kept checking the worker they had checked most before (Table 2), and most said they would do so if forced (Table 7). **For us:** look at whether participants who challenge at the first flag keep challenging at later flags, and how their trust in the checker (Jian et al.) moves alongside.

**Moderator that did not matter**
- Group II, where each worker had one visible lapse in a period that was not checked, did not differ significantly from Group I. There was "only a slight tendency" toward larger proportions (p. 205). A's one lapse "had little effect as a determinant of S's behavior toward the subordinate" (p. 213). The *pattern* of checking mattered more than one piece of negative evidence.

**Effect sizes for a power calculation**
- The paper has no between-group comparison on a continuous trust scale, so nothing transfers directly to our main outcome. The only continuous effect is the within-person excess in checking: 0.55 checks, about half a standard deviation (my calculation from Table 2). Trust results are proportions of one relative choice. Reconstructed, about 72% favored the rarely checked worker in Group I and at least 88% in Group II, with ties split. Do not use these for our power calculation.

**Stated limitations and suggested future work**
- Relative judgment: "It is questionable whether or not S actually had something like an absolute distrust of A or felt that he was really undependable, since he evaluated his subordinates relative to one another. The answer to this question must await an experimental variation in which S has only high or low surveillance over his subordinates." (p. 213) Our between-group design with absolute ratings (checker vs neutral) partly carries out this suggested variation, though with a machine checker and negative flags rather than plain checking.
- Other limitations (the first is the author's; the rest are reviewer's notes): the cause data cover only 19 of 40 participants (8 of 14 and 11 of 14 who answered the open-ended item; p. 208). One borderline answer was counted as supporting the hypothesis on the experimenter's own judgment (footnote, p. 208). The sample was all male undergraduates, with 20 per group. The combined analysis of both groups is "intended only as a supplement" and never replaces the per-group tests (p. 205).

**Background the paper cites (not checked by me)**
- Solomon (1957), testing Deutsch's theory: a person trusts another more when he sees the other as well-meaning toward him (p. 212). Strickland adds that trust also followed when participants credited the worker with a self-oriented motive, a personal wish to do well (pp. 212–213).

## 4. Check of our current sentence

**Draft:** "In Strickland (1958), participants who monitored one of two subordinates more often came to trust that one less. The monitor there was a supervisor. Ours is a machine held by the person in authority. This informs H1."

| Claim | Accurate? | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| "participants who monitored one of two subordinates more often" | **Misleading.** The participants did not choose to check one worker more. The experimenter's schedule required every participant to check A after 9 of 10 periods and B after 2 (p. 203). The workers were fictitious and produced equal output. As worded, the sentence suggests that participants who chose to check more then trusted less, which would reverse cause and effect. | "participants who, as supervisors, were told to check one of two (fictitious) workers after 9 of 10 work periods and the other after only 2, although both produced the same output" |
| "came to trust that one less" | **Partly accurate.** The direction is right and significant in both groups (p = .039 and < .001, Table 3). But trust was measured **once**, after the checking, with no earlier rating, so "came to" (a change) is not shown. It was a **relative** judgment (which one do you trust more, or equal). The author himself doubts that there was absolute distrust (p. 213). | "trusted that worker less than the rarely checked one" |
| "The monitor there was a supervisor." | **Accurate, with a qualifier.** The participant, a male undergraduate, played a supervisor in a lab simulation with the power to fine. He was both the one who checked and the one whose trust was measured. | "The checker there was the participant himself, playing a supervisor" |
| "Ours is a machine held by the person in authority." | A statement about our study, not the paper. It leaves out the larger difference: **Strickland's checks were correct and always found the work in order. Nothing negative was ever claimed.** Ours are wrong negative flags. | Add: "and his checks never found fault, whereas our checker raises wrong flags" |
| "This informs H1." | **Acceptable only as analogy.** The paper shows that checking alone, with clean results, lowered *relative* trust. It does not test whether a wrong negative claim lowers trust right after the claim, compared with before and with an unchecked group. | "This suggests, by analogy, that checking itself can lower trust in the person checked (H1)." |

**Suggested replacement:**
"In Strickland (1958), 40 male students played a supervisor who was told to check one of two fictitious workers after 9 of 10 work periods and the other after only 2. Both workers produced the same output, and every check showed the work in order. Afterwards, more supervisors said they trusted the rarely checked worker more than the often-checked one than said the reverse (p = .039 and p < .001 in the two groups). When free to choose, they kept checking the often-checked worker more. There the checking was done by the supervisor himself and never found fault. In our study, a machine held by the person in authority raises wrong flags. The study suggests, by analogy, that checking alone can lower trust in the person being checked (H1)."

**Working-notes claim: "the more closely monitored subordinate was trusted less."**
Accurate in direction, but it needs two qualifiers: *less than the rarely checked one* (a relative, one-item judgment), and the closeness of checking was *assigned*, not chosen. Suggested: "the worker the supervisor had been told to check more often was trusted less than the rarely checked one." The forced "more dependable" judgment (18/20 and 19/20, Table 6) is the strongest single number and could be cited with it.

**Was Strickland's explanation (attributing the cause of compliance) confirmed?**
**No. It was only indicated, and the author says so.**
- Checking and cause (Hypothesis 1-a, Table 4, p. 208): Group I N = 8, p = .107; Group II N = 11, p = .197; combined N = 19, p = .015. The author: "One may not accept the hypothesis in its original statement as conclusively supported. However, there is certainly an indication…" (pp. 208–209).
- Trust and cause (Hypothesis 2-a, Table 5, p. 209): Group I p = .214; Group II p = .182; combined p = .017. The author: "As with Hypothesis 1-a, this prediction cannot be viewed as confirmed. The data are, however, consistent with those of Hypothesis 1-a, and are certainly indicative of a relationship…" (p. 209).
- Summary, finding 3: "A strong tendency, albeit not a conclusive one…" (p. 215).
- Why the combined p < .05 does not count: the author states that the combined analysis "is never included as an alternative for the two for which the original design called" (p. 205). Other weaknesses: only 19 of 40 participants gave classifiable answers, and one of them was classified by the experimenter's own judgment (footnote, p. 208). Reviewer's note: cause and trust were asked on the same questionnaire at the same time, so the data cannot show that the attribution *produced* the lower trust.
- Correct wording: "Strickland proposed that supervisors credited the often-checked worker's compliance to their own checking rather than to the worker. The data indicated this but did not confirm it."

## 5. Bottom line

Cite Strickland (1958) for **H1** as classic **background** evidence (by analogy, not the closest design) that checking a person can lower the checker's trust in that person even when the checks find nothing wrong. Also cite it for its suggested variation with absolute rather than relative trust, which our checker-vs-neutral design partly carries out. It is silent on H2–H4, because the checked person never replied. The fact that matters most: with identical output and every check showing the work in order, supervisors trusted the often-checked worker less than the rarely checked one (p = .039 and < .001) and rated him less dependable (18/20 and 19/20). So in our design, the checker's mere presence, not only its wrong flags, may lower trust in the CISO, and our analysis should separate the two.
