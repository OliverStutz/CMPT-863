# 06 Annotation: artefacts, theory and portfolios

**Purpose:** Present Move 3, theory as annotation and the annotated portfolio, as the essay's constructive proposal, and test whether it delivers what Gaver claims for it. **Slides:** 2. **Time:** 3:00. **Speaker:** Oliver. **Previous section:** 05 Convergence. **Next section:** 07 How the essay argues.

## Slide 06.1: Artefacts as theory nexus, theory as annotation

**Time:** 1:30 · **Visual:** the chain of claims as five short cards, left to right, each with its page (p. 943 to 944): 1 Examples over theory, 2 Ultimate particular, 3 Theory nexus, 4 Embody, not encode, 5 Theory as annotation. A thin two-way arrow under the cards labelled with the symmetry line. No figure from the paper.

**On the slide**
- Designers "turn to existing examples of design" (p. 943)
- The artefact is an "ultimate particular" (Stolterman), with "the same dignity and importance as truth in science" (p. 944)
- "Theories may be provisional, but designed artefacts ... are not" (p. 944)
- A "theory nexus" (Carroll and Kellogg): values, function, users, form (p. 944)
- "If artefacts embody theory, however, they do not encode it" (p. 944)
- So theory is annotation, "to explain and point to features of 'ultimate particulars'" (p. 944)
- Theory also "is underspecified by design" (p. 944)

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** Move three is the constructive one. Gaver starts from an observation: most practising designers do not engage with major HCI theory. They use a mix of techniques and orienting concepts, and they look at existing designs. Why? Gaver's answer: theory is provisional, artefacts are definite. He takes Stolterman's phrase, the ultimate particular, which Stolterman gives the same dignity as truth in science. He takes Carroll and Kellogg's phrase, theory nexus: every artefact carries choices about values, interaction, users and form. Then the key sentence: artefacts embody theory, but they do not encode it. You cannot read the important dimensions off the object. And the gap runs both ways. Theory underspecifies design, and many aspects of a good design are not captured by any given theory. His example is a rotary controller for a microwave oven: to understand controlling several functions at once, you look at that design. So theory's job is to point, not to replace. We think this flips the usual HCI order. Normally the system illustrates the theory. Here the theory annotates the system.
- **Transition:** If one artefact needs annotation to show its dimensions, Gaver's next step is to put many artefacts side by side.
- **Watch out:**
  - "Ultimate particular" is Stolterman's [31], quoted by Gaver; "theory nexus" is Carroll and Kellogg's, quoted by Gaver (pitfall 7). Say "Gaver quotes."
  - The ellipsis in "designed artefacts ... are not" replaces the parenthetical "(as opposed to demonstrators, for example)." If asked, give it in full: Gaver is distinguishing finished designs from demonstrators, which matters because many HCI prototypes are demonstrators.
  - A sharp classmate may look up Carroll and Kellogg in the reference list and find reference 3 is Collins 1994. That is a copy-editing slip (pitfall 10). The paper is real: Carroll and Kellogg, "Artifact as theory-nexus: Hermeneutics meets theory-based design," CHI 1989, pp. 7 to 14. Mention it lightly if at all, never as a gotcha.
  - "Most practicing designers" is Gaver's claim with a secondhand source ("[27] cited in [31]," p. 943). It is an assertion, not a finding of the essay.
  - The microwave example comes from Djajadiningrat, Matthews and Stienstra 2007 [6], not from Gaver's own work.
  - Do not say Gaver rejects theory. He gives theory a role: making "accessible the kinds of decisions and rationales that comprise an artefact's embodied theory" (p. 944).
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: What exactly is a "theory nexus"?* A: The phrase is Carroll and Kellogg's, from "Artifact as theory-nexus: Hermeneutics meets theory-based design," CHI 1989. Their claim is that an HCI design embodies several distinct psychological claims at once, so the artefact itself may be the most effective medium for theory development in HCI. Gaver widens it: a design's choices reveal "both the issues they think are important, and their beliefs about the right way to address those issues," from the philosophical to the aesthetic (p. 944). So Gaver's version is broader than the original, which was about psychological claims.
  - *Q: Where does "ultimate particular" come from, and what does Stolterman mean by it?* A: Gaver quotes Stolterman 2008, p. 59 (p. 944). Stolterman contrasts science, which looks for the universal and the existing, with design, which deals with "the specific, intentional and non-existing." The ultimate particular is the final, manifested outcome of a design process, and even a mass-produced object is one "in its specific use context," so the term is not about uniqueness. Stolterman himself credits the "dignity" line to Nelson and Stolterman, The Design Way (2003).
  - *Q: What does "embody but not encode" mean in plain words?* A: The object contains the decisions but does not label them. An artefact in design space does "not highlight the salient or fruitful dimensions of variation that space offers" (p. 944). Theory's job is "making accessible the kinds of decisions and rationales that comprise an artefact's embodied theory, or give dimensionality to its design space" (p. 944). Annotation points; it does not replace.
  - *Q: What is the microwave example, concretely?* A: Gaver gives one sentence (p. 944). In Djajadiningrat, Matthews and Stienstra 2007 it is a concept design by a TU Eindhoven industrial design student, Rombout Frieling, 2004: a control on the oven door that you rotate to set power and slide to set cooking time, so you can set both while closing the door. A novice does the three actions one by one; for a skilled user, they write, "the separate actions may melt into one." That one design shows what the phrase "simultaneous analogue control of multiple parameters" (p. 940) means better than the phrase does.
  - *Q: Isn't "most practicing designers do not engage with major theoretical approaches" just an assertion?* A: In this essay it is secondhand: "[27] cited in [31]" (p. 943), that is Rogers 2004 as reported by Stolterman. Stolterman gives the numbers from Rogers's practitioner survey: almost no one used predictive modelling such as GOMS, 10 percent used experiments, 5 percent guidelines, and 85 percent relied mainly on their own intuition and experience, though many "used" single concepts such as affordance (Stolterman 2008, p. 56). So there is evidence behind it, but Gaver does not show it, and the survey was published in 2004, eight years before the essay.
  - *Q: Why does Gaver say artefacts are definite but exclude demonstrators?* A: The full sentence is "Theories may be provisional, but designed artefacts (as opposed to demonstrators, for example) are not" (p. 944). The paper does not define "demonstrator." We read it as a prototype built to show that a technique works, which leaves many design choices open, whereas a finished design commits to all of them. That matters here because many HCI papers present demonstrators.
  - *Q: Doesn't this make theory unfalsifiable all over again?* A: Gaver accepts that. In Move 1 he says the goal is to create "theories that are sometimes right" (p. 940 to 941), and here theory's role "is limited to inspiration and annotation" (p. 945). His test is Lakatos's: whether a theory leads to "productive research programmes" (p. 941). So Move 3 is consistent with Move 1, not a contradiction. The harder tension is p. 945, where portfolios are said to allow "extensibility and verifiability"; section 03 takes that up.
  - *Q: Isn't calling artefacts "the truths of design" an overclaim?* A: The wording is Gaver's: theory points to features of "'ultimate particulars', the truths of design" (p. 944), echoing Stolterman's "same dignity and importance as truth in science." We read "truth" as the final reference point, not a proposition that is true or false. The fair challenge is that an artefact can fail; Stolterman himself writes on the same page that "The final measure of success for a design is something revealed in location, in real use, and over time" (Stolterman 2008, p. 59). Gaver does not take up that point.
- **Sources:** p. 943 (designers and examples, "[27] cited in [31]," provisional versus definite); p. 944 (underspecified by design, microwave controller [6], ultimate particular, theory nexus, embody and encode, annotation). Carroll and Kellogg 1989: https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/artifact-as-theory-nexus-hermeneutics-meets-theory-based-design/ (DOI 10.1145/67449.67452).

## Slide 06.2: Annotated portfolios: Rams, and the converse of patterns

**Time:** 1:30 · **Visual:** Figure 1 (p. 945) at full width, cropped from `assets/p9-09.png` (crop to the figure and its caption, excluding the page header and body text). Bullets in a narrow strip beneath or beside it.

**On the slide**
- One design: a point in design space; a portfolio: "an area in that space" (p. 944)
- Shows a designer's dimensions of choice and "the invariances among them" (p. 944)
- "As artefacts are to theory ... design portfolios are to research programmes" (p. 944)
- Figure 1: seven Rams products, four of his ten principles (p. 944 to 945)
- "neither dominates, and neither is subservient" (p. 945)
- "the converse of Alexander's [1] design patterns" (p. 945)
- Claims "'extensibility and verifiability'" (p. 945)
- Not a replacement for all theory (p. 945)

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** One design is a point in design space. A portfolio, several designs by the same or associated designers, marks out an area. Comparing them shows the dimensions a designer's choices range over, and what stays constant. Gaver's analogy: portfolios are to research programmes what artefacts are to theory. His example is Dieter Rams. The figure is labelled a sketch. Seven products, short annotations, and four of Rams's ten principles in the centre. What defines the form is balance: neither the examples nor the ideas dominate. Gaver calls it the converse of Alexander's design patterns, which abstract regularities; portfolios keep the particulars. He says it still allows the extensibility and verifiability the critics asked for. And he limits it: one serious form, not a replacement for all theory. We argue the gap is verification. The essay never says who annotates, or how two annotations would be compared. That is what verifiability would need. **[classmate]**
- **Transition:** That is the whole argument; now we look at how the essay makes it, with what method and what evidence.
- **Watch out:**
  - **Test, labelled "we argue," four points.** (a) Who annotates. In the text, Rams's own ten principles annotate his portfolio (p. 944). In the figure, the short notes refer to Rams in the third person ("Rams redesigned the unit, dissatisfied with the need for two heating elements"), so someone other than Rams wrote them; the paper does not say who. The essay does not say who else may annotate a portfolio or how two annotators would be compared, and that is what "verifiability" would need. (b) The distance from patterns is smaller than "converse" suggests. Alexander's patterns are also anchored in examples: each pattern in A Pattern Language is presented with a photograph of an archetypal example and a diagram (Fincher 2012, URL below). The direction of abstraction differs: patterns generalise across many designers' solutions to a recurring problem; portfolios stay with one designer's set. Note Gaver hedges, "in several respects" (p. 945). (c) The caption says annotated portfolios "are not defined by their graphic presentation" (p. 945), and the text says "The notion of annotated portfolios is not a formal one" (p. 944). So the proposal is a stance toward theory, not a format. (d) The extensibility claim is asserted in one sentence (p. 945), not shown.
  - Figure 1 shows four of ten principles; the text names two, "'good design is unobtrusive', 'good design is thorough down to the last detail'" (p. 944). Never say the figure shows all ten (pitfall 8).
  - What the figure contains, so nobody overclaims: seven photographs (a coffee maker in three colours, a wall shelving system, a radio, a lounge chair, a prototype portable TV, a shaver the note calls "the S3," and a small portable device in three colours), each with a short note; the four principles in the centre, each with a quoted gloss such as "'at best, it is self explanatory'"; the sentences of each note coloured to match the principles; images credited to Flickr Creative Commons. The figure does not name the product models beyond "S3."
  - "Extensibility and verifiability" are Zimmerman and Forlizzi's words, which Gaver quotes back (p. 945; pitfall 3). Say "the words the critics used."
  - Gaver proposes portfolios as "a serious form of theoretical contribution," not the only one (p. 945; pitfall 9).
  - Footnote 1: "Many of the ideas here about annotated portfolios have been developed in collaboration with John Bowers" (p. 944). The idea was developed further in Gaver and Bowers, "Annotated Portfolios," Interactions 19(4), 2012, which section 10 covers. Mention only.
  - If a classmate asks why Rams and not a research example: the essay offers no research-through-design portfolio of its own. Section 07 picks this up as a reflexivity point.
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: What are Rams's ten principles, exactly?* A: As Vitsoe gives them: Good design is innovative; makes a product useful; is aesthetic; makes a product understandable; is unobtrusive; is honest; is long-lasting; is thorough down to the last detail; is environmentally-friendly; is as little design as possible. Vitsoe says Rams formulated them in the late 1970s, asking himself "is my design good design?" Gaver's text names two (p. 944); Figure 1 shows four, numbers 2, 4, 5 and 8 in Vitsoe's order (p. 945).
  - *Q: Are the four in the figure in Rams's own words?* A: Nearly. The headings match Vitsoe's except that the figure adds a comma, "thorough, down to the last detail" (p. 945). The glosses are fragments of Rams's explanations, for example "'both neutral and restrained'" and "'nothing must be arbitrary or left to chance'"; the figure prints "'at best, it is self explanatory'" where Vitsoe has "self-explanatory." The notes beside the photographs are not Rams's; they speak of Rams in the third person.
  - *Q: What is an Alexander design pattern?* A: A Pattern Language (Alexander, Ishikawa and Silverstein, 1977) has 253 patterns. Each has a fixed format: a photograph of an archetypal example, an introductory paragraph linking it to larger patterns, a one or two sentence problem headline, a body, a solution stated as an instruction after "Therefore," a diagram, and a closing paragraph linking to smaller patterns. Example, pattern 159, Light on Two Sides of Every Room: people "will always gravitate to those rooms which have light on two sides"; therefore, locate each room so it has outdoor space on at least two sides and put windows in those walls.
  - *Q: So why does Gaver call portfolios the "converse" of patterns?* A: Patterns "abstract regularities from repeated attempts to design for the same domains"; portfolios "maintain the particularity of individual examples" (p. 945). Patterns abstract "across instances," portfolios respect the "'ultimate particular'" (p. 945). He hedges, "in several respects." Jonas Löwgren, writing in Interactions in 2013, saw a strong relation between annotated portfolios and the way patterns are treated, so our point (b) has company.
  - *Q: Who is allowed to annotate, and can two people annotate the same portfolio?* A: The essay allows it in principle: "Portfolios can support multiple conceptual perspectives, and similar perspectives can be applied to different portfolios" (p. 945), and Gaver calls this "reflecting the lack of convergence in the field as a virtue." What it does not say is how two annotations would be compared or which one is better. Löwgren 2013 reports Gaver saying, in personal communication, that a portfolio may not do abstraction itself but "affords abstraction (in the sense of extraction) by others."
  - *Q: How exactly do portfolios deliver "extensibility and verifiability"?* A: The paper asserts it in one clause (p. 945) and does not define either word. The nearest support: juxtaposition "grounds and specifies theoretical concepts" by "yoking them to particular design manifestations" (p. 945), which we read as making concepts checkable against examples; and designers can move around a design's point "or even by jumping away from it along understood dimensions" (p. 944), which we read as extensibility. Neither is a test in the critics' sense.
  - *Q: What does "design space" mean here, and what is reference [12]?* A: [12] is Gaver 2011, "Making spaces: how design workbooks work," CHI 2011, pp. 1551 to 1560. Workbooks are collections of design proposals made early in a project. There Gaver calls design space "a valuable metaphor" but warns it can mislead: variations do not reduce to a few dimensions, and it is more like a landscape with uninhabitable areas. "Design creates the spaces in which it operates." That is why p. 944 says designs may be "creating a design space around themselves."
  - *Q: What did Gaver and Bowers 2012 add?* A: "Annotated Portfolios," Interactions 19(4), pp. 40 to 49. According to Löwgren's 2013 summary, which quotes them, they propose annotated portfolios "as a way to communicate design research," as an alternative to the view that design must engage in theory formation. The method: select a collection of designs, re-present them in a suitable medium, add brief textual annotations. Their example is their own studio's work: two artefacts for older people, then three more, where one annotation ("influencing autonomous drift") holds across the larger set and another drops out. So the sequel supplies the research portfolio the CHI essay lacks. We could not open the article itself.
  - *Q: And Bowers 2012?* A: John Bowers, "The Logic of Annotated Portfolios: Communicating the Value of 'Research Through Design'," DIS 2012, pp. 68 to 77. The abstract draws on Feyerabend to argue that design research should build its own "limited rationality," and presents annotated portfolios as a way of "capturing the family resemblances that exist in a collection of artefacts," demonstrated on nine well-known pieces from the Goldsmiths studio. Löwgren reports that Bowers treats annotations as indexical and inseparable from their artefacts, not as abstractions.
  - *Q: Pullin 2010 is in the reference list. What is it and why is it there?* A: It is reference [25] and is never cited in the text (pitfall 10). Graham Pullin, "Statement of Practice: Curating and creating design collections," Design and Culture 2(3), 2010, pp. 309 to 328. He defines a design collection as objects made independently by several designers but "conceived as an entity at the time of creation," and argues collections can illuminate themes and imply a landscape of alternatives beyond any single piece. We read that as close to Gaver's "area in that space" (p. 944), which may be why it is listed; the paper does not say.
- **Sources:** p. 944 (point and area, dimensions and invariances, artefacts and programmes, Rams, ten principles, "not a formal one," footnote 1); p. 945 (Figure 1 and caption, "neither dominates," Alexander, extensibility claim, limit). Alexander, Ishikawa and Silverstein 1977, Oxford University Press, 253 patterns: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Pattern_Language. Pattern format (photograph "showing an archetypal example of the pattern in use," diagram): Sally Fincher, "Some Roles of Patterns and Pattern Languages in the Capture and Transfer of Design Knowledge," 2012, https://www.cs.kent.ac.uk/people/staff/saf/patterns/WhyDoPatternsWork.pdf. Gaver and Bowers 2012: https://research.gold.ac.uk/id/eprint/14820 (Interactions 19(4), pp. 40 to 49, DOI 10.1145/2212877.2212889; volume and pages also in https://dblp.dagstuhl.de/pid/g/WilliamWGaver.html).

## Background for the presenter

1. **Stolterman 2008, "The nature of design practice and implications for interaction design research," International Journal of Design 2(1), pp. 55 to 65.** Argument: HCI research meant to support design practice has not always succeeded because it lacks an understanding of what design practice is. Science deals with "the universal and the existing," design with "the specific, intentional and nonexisting" (Stolterman p. 59), so science is not the best place to borrow methods for design complexity. The "ultimate particular" (Stolterman p. 59) is the final designed outcome in its use context; Stolterman takes the "dignity" line from Nelson and Stolterman, The Design Way (2003). His section "Acting Designerly with Discipline and Rigor" argues design has its own rigour (rationality, judgment, argumentation, sketching). "Prepared-for-action, not guided-in-action" (Stolterman p. 61): education and tools can prepare a designer but cannot prescribe action in a complex situation; Schön's reflection-in-action is his example of an approach that works. Gaver draws on him on pp. 937, 938, 939, 941, 943 and 944: he is one of the four sources of the "undercurrent" of questioning [9, 31, 33, 35] (p. 937) and also a main source for Gaver's own answer.
2. **Carroll and Kellogg 1989 and the task-artifact cycle.** "Artifact as theory-nexus: Hermeneutics meets theory-based design," CHI 1989, pp. 7 to 14, DOI 10.1145/67449.67452. Claim: HCI designs embody multiple, distinct psychological claims, and artefacts are perhaps the most effective medium for theory development in HCI. The related "task-artifact cycle" (Carroll, Kellogg and Rosson, 1991, in Designing Interaction, Cambridge University Press) says tasks set requirements for artefacts, and new artefacts change the tasks, so the two co-evolve; Carroll and Rosson 1992 (ACM TOIS 10(2), pp. 181 to 212) turned this into "claims" analysis and scenario-based design. A fact that may land: CHI 1989, where "theory nexus" was introduced, was held in Austin, Texas, the same city as CHI 2012.
3. **Djajadiningrat, Matthews and Stienstra 2007, "Easy doesn't do it: skill and expression in tangible aesthetics," Personal and Ubiquitous Computing 11(8), pp. 657 to 676.** Argument: product interaction has neglected movement; button-based products burden cognition and "deny us the opportunity of building bodily skill," and the movement of products themselves could be expressive. "Skill" means bodily, perceptual-motor skill built through use; "expression" means the expressiveness of product movement and form. Buttons give a narrow input "bandwidth"; their remedy is analogue controls and control of several parameters at once by one skilled action. Four examples: an interactive toy, a mobile phone, the microwave oven, a programmable heating controller. Gaver uses this paper three times: as an articulation of practice about embodied movement (p. 938), as the "simultaneous analogue control" example (p. 940), and for the microwave (p. 944).
4. **Rams and Figure 1.** Vitsoe dates them to the late 1970s and says Rams began the storage system behind the company in 1957; Vitsoe was founded in 1959. A detail that supports the "who annotates" point: the current Vitsoe page itself is a small annotated portfolio, pairing each principle with one product, and principle 8, "thorough down to the last detail," is illustrated with the ET 66 calculator (1987) by Dietrich Lubs, not by Rams (Vitsoe page as archived in January 2026, not as Gaver saw it in 2012). Gaver says vitsoe.com "emphasises his design principles over the portfolio of products themselves" (p. 945).
5. **Alexander, Ishikawa and Silverstein 1977, A Pattern Language (Oxford University Press).** 253 patterns from towns down to construction details, each in a fixed format (photograph, context, problem headline, body, "Therefore" solution, diagram, links to smaller patterns). A pattern names a recurring problem and the core of a solution that can be used "a million times over" without doing it the same way twice (the book's introduction, quoted by Fincher 2012). We read the real contrast as this: a pattern's photograph is one archetypal example standing for a solution many builders have found, while Gaver's portfolio is one designer's set, kept as a set. Note Gaver's reference [1] lists only Alexander and Ishikawa (p. 946).
6. **Where annotated portfolios went next.** Gaver 2011 (workbooks, [12]) is the methodological root: collections of many concrete proposals let issues emerge "rather than being theory-driven" and create a design space instead of describing one. Gaver and Bowers 2012 (Interactions) and Bowers 2012 (DIS, nine studio pieces, "family resemblances," Feyerabend) develop the idea; footnote 1 already credits Bowers (p. 944). Löwgren 2013 ("Annotated portfolios and other forms of intermediate-level knowledge," Interactions 20(1)) places annotated portfolios between particular artefacts and general theory, next to patterns, strong concepts and experiential qualities. We read the 2012 essay as the argument and the Interactions and DIS papers as the worked examples; section 10 covers them.
