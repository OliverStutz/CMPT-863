# 04 Theory: the Popper test and the Lakatos rescue

**Purpose:** Present Move 1 as a test the essay runs on design theory, show what Gaver concludes from it, and then test the test. **Slides:** 2. **Time:** 3:00. **Speaker:** Oliver. **Previous section:** 03 The argument on one slide. **Next section:** 05 Convergence.

## Slide 04.1: Five genres of design theory, and the Popper test

**Time:** 1:30 · **Visual:** two-column layout. Left: the five genres as a stacked list, one example each (p. 938 to 939). Right: Popper's criterion as a quote card (p. 939), and below it two boxes, "Theory underspecifies design" and "Design is generative" (p. 940), with the "implicit sometimes" sentence highlighted. No figure from the paper.

**On the slide**
- Five genres of research through design (RtD) theory (p. 938 to 939):
  - Articulations of practice: Djajadiningrat et al., embodied movement
  - Borrowed theory: affordances become "'irresistables'" (p. 938)
  - Manifestos: Reflective Design; ludic design
  - Frameworks: Forlizzi's Product Ecology
  - Meta-theory: Zimmerman and Forlizzi; Stolterman; this essay
- Popper: "the criterion of the scientific status of a theory is its falsifiability" (p. 939)
- Why RtD theory fails it, says Gaver (p. 940):
  - Underspecification: "there is always an implicit sometimes in statements about how to design successfully"
  - Generativity: designing for refutation means "no longer ... doing research through design"

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** Gaver's first move starts with an inventory. He sorts the theory that research through design produces into five genres. Articulations of practice, like Djajadiningrat and colleagues on embodied movement, built from reflecting on their own designs. Borrowed theory, like affordances, which Overbeeke and Wensveen turned into "irresistables." Manifestos, like Sengers's Reflective Design or Gaver's own ludic design. Frameworks, like Forlizzi's Product Ecology. And meta-theory, theory about research through design itself, where Gaver puts Zimmerman and Forlizzi, Stolterman, and this essay. Then he asks how good this work is, and runs Popper's test: a theory is scientific only if it can be refuted. Gaver says design theory fails, for two reasons. First, theory underspecifies design. Claims take the form "designing for X can lead to successful outcomes," with an implicit sometimes, and no number of failures disproves "sometimes." Second, design is generative. If designers built things to refute theories, he says, they would no longer be doing research through design. Note the conditional: unscientific if Popper's criterion is accepted. He does not treat Popper as the last word.
- **Transition:** So Gaver turns to a second account of science, one in which confirming examples count for something: Lakatos.
- **Watch out:**
  - Gaver's best evidence is his own earlier paper on ambiguity [14] (Gaver, Beaver and Benford, CHI 2003). The claim that ambiguity "encourages them to start grappling conceptually with systems and their contexts" (p. 940) is, he grants, "in principle a falsifiable statement." But a fair test would need "comparable ambiguous and unambiguous systems," and "Simply tweaking an unambiguous system to be ambiguous" would be unrepresentative of real design. Hence "the synthetic nature of design is incompatible with the controlled experiments useful for theory testing" (p. 940). If asked for an example of the argument, use this one.
  - Gaver is candid about the cost: Popper "holds a potentially unflattering mirror up to the theories produced as a part of research through design" (p. 940). He also concedes Popper "is widely criticised both in Philosophy of Science and by practicing scientists" (p. 940). Do not present him as defensive.
  - Do not say "Gaver says design is unscientific." The sentence is conditional: "Research through design is clearly unscientific if Popper's [24] criterion of falsifiablity is accepted" (p. 940, the paper's spelling). The other half is on slide 04.2: it "depends on the account of science one adopts" (p. 941).
  - Attribution: the in-text "Zimmerman & Forlizzi [34]" (p. 938 to 939) points to reference 34, Zimmerman, Forlizzi and Evenson, CHI 2007 (p. 946), but the two phrases Gaver quotes there, "agreed upon form of practice, evaluation, and outcome" and "extensible, systemic approaches," are in Zimmerman and Forlizzi 2008, reference 33 (confirmed by the reference check, `../reports/opus-REF-paper-references.md`). Say "Zimmerman and Forlizzi" without a year, or say 2008. "Ludic design" and "homo ludens" are Gaver's own earlier work [13]. "Ultimate particulars" is Stolterman's [31] (p. 939).
  - "Irresistables" is the paper's in-text spelling (p. 938); the reference title spells it "irresistibles" (p. 946, ref. 23). If someone notices, it is a typo in the original, not ours.
  - A sharp classmate may ask why the "sometimes" form is unique to design. It is not; slide 04.2 takes that up as our test.
  - **[classmate]** slot here if a commentary summarised the five genres or picked one as the weakest.
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: Manifestos and frameworks do not look like theory. What does Gaver count as "theory" here?* A: He uses a broad sense. When conceptual statements are put in general terms and applied to several examples, "they become recognisable as theories in their own right" (p. 938). Even Product Ecology, which avoids prescribing methods, "implies a conceptual orientation" (p. 938). He also says the essay targets "the more specific, content-oriented theories" in the first four genres, not meta-theory (p. 939).
  - *Q: What did Popper actually claim, and when?* A: Karl Popper, born in Vienna in 1902, died 1994, worked on the demarcation problem: what separates science from non-science. His answer is falsifiability. He set it out in Logik der Forschung (1935; English 1959). The chapter Gaver quotes, "Science: Conjectures and Refutations," was first given as a lecture in 1953 and opens Conjectures and Refutations (1963). His classic contrast: Einstein's light-bending prediction, which Eddington's 1919 observations could have refuted, versus psychoanalysis, which no observation could refute.
  - *Q: Gaver admits Popper is widely criticised. Why use him at all?* A: Because Popper is the version of science most people carry around. Gaver calls falsifiability "influential in popular accounts of scientific reasoning" (p. 939), and says he wants to "problematise the (potentially tacit) accounts of science that seem to underlie some views of research" (p. 939). Science is "a useful foil" (p. 939). He also notes the demarcation debate was driven by "evolution v. 'creation science'" (p. 939). We read Popper as a deliberate foil: the popular picture of science, chosen on purpose, and Gaver says so himself.
  - *Q: Where does Kuhn fit, and what is "puzzle-solving"?* A: Thomas Kuhn's The Structure of Scientific Revolutions came out in 1962; Gaver cites the 1970 second edition (ref. 17, p. 946). Normal science, for Kuhn, is puzzle-solving inside an accepted paradigm; Chapter IV is titled "Normal Science as Puzzle-solving." Gaver's point: when a puzzle fails, it "is considered the fault of the scientist using the theory, not the fault of the theory itself" ([17] p 67, quoted on p. 939). So real science does not drop theories at the first refutation.
  - *Q: Isn't the Feynman quote just Popper in a physicist's voice?* A: Yes, and Gaver uses it that way (p. 939). Then he quotes Feynman on weak decay, "striking for its inconsistency with his previous account" (p. 939). One thing beyond the paper: right after "If it disagrees with experiment it is wrong," Feynman adds that you must first check the experiment was done and reported correctly, "some dirt or something." Both passages are in lecture 7, "Seeking New Laws," of his 1964 Messenger Lectures at Cornell.
  - *Q: What is "simultaneous analogue control of multiple parameters"?* A: It is one of Gaver's examples of X in "designing for X" (p. 940), from Djajadiningrat, Matthews and Stienstra 2007 [6]. Gaver glosses it on p. 944 as "simultaneous control over multiple functions," with "a rotary controller for a microwave oven [6]" as the example. The phrase is a section heading in the source paper: "From narrow bandwidth to simultaneous analogue control of multiple parameters." A button carries only on or off, so many presses have to be strung together; a continuous control can set several parameters in one skilled movement. Gaver's p. 944 gloss, "simultaneous control over multiple functions," is the source's own phrase from its microwave analysis.
  - *Q: Is a "sometimes" claim really unfalsifiable? Couldn't you test whether X raises the success rate?* A: Fair. Gaver's logical point is about the bare form "X will sometimes lead to successful outcomes" (p. 940). A claim that X works more often than not-X is a different and stronger claim, and could in principle be tested. Gaver's answer to that is his second argument: "Underspecification makes falsification difficult in practice as well as in principle" (p. 940), shown by the ambiguity case. We think the practical argument carries more weight than the logical one.
  - *Q: What is in the ambiguity paper he uses as his test case?* A: Gaver, Beaver and Benford, "Ambiguity as a resource for design," CHI 2003, pp. 233 to 240. It names three kinds: ambiguity of information, which sits in the artefact; of context, in the cultural discourses used to read it; and of relationship, in the person's own interpretive and evaluative stance. Gaver picks his own earlier claim to test (p. 940), which we read as a fair move: he does not pick on a rival's theory.
  - *Q: What are "irresistables" and Product Ecology, concretely?* A: Overbeeke and Wensveen, DPPI 2003, Pittsburgh, pp. 92 to 97: they started from Gibson's affordances and shifted the focus to emotional appeal (p. 938). The paper's own title spells it "irresistibles"; Gaver's reference list also misspells Wensveen as "Wesveen" (p. 946). Forlizzi's Product Ecology (International Journal of Design 2(1), 2008) frames a product inside five factors: the product, other products, people, place, and social and cultural context, illustrated with a study of households given a Roomba.
- **Sources:** p. 938 (genres: articulations of practice, borrowed theory, "'irresistables'", manifestos, frameworks, meta-theory begins); p. 939 (meta-theory continued, "a contribution to meta-theory", "But how good is this conceptual work?", Popper criterion); p. 940 (unscientific if Popper, unflattering mirror, underspecification, "implicit sometimes", ambiguity example, design is generative); p. 946 (references 6, 10, 13, 14, 23, 24, 30, 31, 34).

## Slide 04.2: Lakatos, and what "provisional, contingent, and aspirational" buys

**Time:** 1:30 · **Visual:** top half, Lakatos's three terms as a small diagram (core inside belt, with the problem-solving mechanism as an arrow) and his test in a quote card (p. 939 to 940); a hedge strip with Gaver's two hedges (p. 941); bottom half, a box labelled "We argue" with three short lines.

**On the slide**
- Lakatos: "'hard core'", "'protective belt'", "'powerful problem-solving mechanism'" (p. 939)
- Test: programmes "predict novel facts" (p. 940)
- Examples become "testimony to the fertility of the overarching theory" (p. 941)
- Gaver's hedges: accounts not "particularly representative"; science or not "depends on the account of science one adopts" (p. 941)
- Goal: "theories that are sometimes right" (p. 941)
- **We argue:**
  - "Sometimes" claims also exist in medicine and education, which built evidence standards
  - "Productive" is a standard; who judges it?
  - The ambiguity case defeats controlled comparison, not evaluation

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** Gaver's alternative is Lakatos. Science works in programmes: a hard core of theory, a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses that absorbs awkward results, and a problem-solving mechanism. A programme earns respect by predicting novel facts. Seen this way, a manifesto with its designed examples is not weak confirmation. Gaver calls it testimony to fertility. Unfalsifiable theory is not a flaw, he says, as long as it leads to productive research programmes. Two hedges matter. He does not claim Popper and Lakatos represent the philosophy of science. And whether design is a science depends on the account of science you adopt. So the goal is theories that are sometimes right. Now we test the test. We argue three things. One: medicine and education also make claims that hold only sometimes, and they still built evidence standards. Unfalsifiability alone does not exempt a field. Two: "productive" is itself a standard. Lakatos also described degenerating programmes, and the essay does not say who judges fertility, or how. Three: the ambiguity example shows that controlled comparison fails, not that evaluation fails. Field deployments are evidence too, and the essay does not discuss them here.
- **Transition:** If theory is only sometimes right, why should everyone agree on it? That is the convergence question, and Gaver offers three answers.
- **Watch out:**
  - Pitfall 4: Lakatos's test is novel facts and productive programmes, not confirmation. Gaver's claim is that confirming examples show fertility, not truth: "What Popper would reject as 'merely' confirmatory evidence is, from this point of view, testimony to the fertility of the overarching theory" (p. 941). Do not say "Lakatos accepts confirmation."
  - Pitfall 3: "provisional, contingent, and aspirational" is Gaver's own summary (p. 941; also p. 937, 938). "Extensibility and verifiability" is what Zimmerman and Forlizzi asked for, which Gaver quotes and says these features "limit" (p. 941). Do not swap them.
  - Fair reply to our point (b): Gaver does name the criterion. On p. 941 he reports that Lakatos offers "the relative productivity of competing programmes as the grounds for scientists' choice amongst them." So he has a standard; what is missing is who applies it in design and how. Keep our point that narrow.
  - "Degenerating" is Lakatos's term, not Gaver's. The essay never uses it. In Lakatos's methodology a programme is degenerating when its successive theories do not deliver novel predictions, or deliver predictions that turn out false (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, section 2.2). Present it as our addition.
  - Our point (a) is an argument, not a citation. Do not claim that medicine is unfalsifiable or that its standards solved the problem; claim only that "sometimes" claims and evidence standards coexist elsewhere.
  - Our point (c) does not say Gaver rejects evaluation. He says controlled experiments fit poorly (p. 940); field studies are simply not discussed in this section.
  - A classmate who knows the philosophy may say Popper and Lakatos are a narrow sample: no Feyerabend, no Hacking (neither is in the reference list, p. 946). Answer with Gaver's own hedge: "I am not presenting Popper's and Lakatos' accounts as particularly representative of Philosophy of Science," and he chose them to show "how unsettled and controversial accounts of science are" (p. 941).
  - The setup between the two accounts, if asked: Gaver uses Kuhn's "'puzzle-solving'" (p. 939), Newton's admitted failure on the moon's orbit (p. 939) and Feynman's "'You need courage to say the experiments must be wrong'" (p. 939) to show theories survive contrary evidence in real science. The Newton point cites [3], which in the reference list is Collins 1994; it belongs to Curd and Cover [5] (pitfall 10). Mention only if asked, as a copy-editing slip.
  - The Lakatos reference is "Science and pseudoscience," 1977, ref. 19 (p. 946), "Reprinted in [3]," which again should be [5].
  - **[classmate]** slot here for a commentary on falsifiability, Popper or whether design is a science.
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: What exactly are the hard core and the protective belt?* A: The hard core is the set of central claims a programme treats as untouchable by decision; Lakatos's "negative heuristic" forbids aiming refutations at it. The protective belt is the ring of auxiliary hypotheses that takes the hits and gets modified instead, guided by a "positive heuristic" (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, section 2.2). Gaver's third term, the "powerful problem-solving mechanism," is what generates evidence and makes it "compatible with theory" (p. 939).
  - *Q: Can you tell the Neptune story properly?* A: Uranus, found in 1781, drifted from the orbit Newton's laws predicted. Instead of dropping Newton, Urbain Le Verrier in Paris and John Couch Adams in Cambridge each calculated where an unseen planet would have to be. Johann Galle at the Berlin Observatory found Neptune on 23 September 1846, about one degree from Le Verrier's position. In Lakatos's terms, the belt changed (a new planet), the core stayed, and the change produced a novel fact. Gaver tells it in one sentence, citing [5] (p. 940).
  - *Q: But can't the protective belt save any theory?* A: That is the risk, and the same move can fail. The Stanford entry's example is Mercury's perihelion: the extra-body move did not work, "there seems to be no such body," and astronomers did not give up Newton until Einstein's theory came along. That is why Lakatos needs the novel-facts test. A belt that only explains away, without predicting, marks a degenerating programme.
  - *Q: Lakatos asks for novel facts. What novel facts has design predicted?* A: This is the strongest challenge, and the honest answer is that Gaver changes the test. On p. 940 the test is to "predict novel facts"; on p. 941 what is at stake is a theory's "ability to inspire new designs." New designs are new artefacts, not predicted facts, and the essay does not argue that the two are equivalent. We read this as a loosening of Lakatos that Gaver's hedge on p. 941 lets him make.
  - *Q: What does "degenerating" mean, and did Lakatos use it on real cases?* A: A programme degenerates when its successive versions stop delivering novel predictions, or when the predictions turn out false; it is progressive when its new content is corroborated (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, section 2.2). Lakatos's own target was Marxism: "Has...Marxism ever predicted a stunning novel fact successfully? Never!" The word is not in Gaver's essay; it is our addition.
  - *Q: Is ref. 19, "Science and pseudoscience," Lakatos's main statement?* A: No. It is a short radio talk, first broadcast on 30 June 1973 in the Open University course A303, Problems of Philosophy, later printed as the introduction (pp. 1 to 7) to The Methodology of Scientific Research Programmes (1978). The full theory is "Falsification and the Methodology of Scientific Research Programmes," 1970, in Criticism and the Growth of Knowledge. Gaver cites the talk as reprinted in an anthology (p. 946). Lakatos lived 1922 to 1974.
  - *Q: Does Gaver say design is not a science?* A: Not flatly. He says, "Personally, I tend to think that the nature of design practice and research is distinct from the ideals, and often practice, of science" and then that whether it "can be considered a science depends on the account of science one adopts" (p. 941). Give both halves. His target is "easy scientism," using "'science' as shorthand" for criteria assumed to be good (p. 941).
  - *Q: Isn't point (a) unfair? Medicine can run trials; design cannot build comparable systems.* A: That is Gaver's reply on p. 940, and we accept that controlled comparison is harder in design. Our claim is narrower: the "sometimes" form alone does not exempt a field from evidence standards. We do not say design should copy medicine, and we do not say medicine's standards solved the problem.
  - *Q: What did Stolterman mean by "prepared-for-action but not guided-in-action"?* A: It heads a section of Stolterman 2008, International Journal of Design 2(1), p. 61, quoted on p. 941. His point: a designer has to be immersed in the particular case, so "Design education can prepare for such situations, but it cannot prescribe how to act in them." His model is Schön's reflection-in-action. Worth knowing: a page earlier he writes that "the argument here is not that design research cannot be done in a scientific way" (p. 60).
  - *Q: Where do "Reflective Design," "Supple Interfaces" and "Designing for the Self" come from?* A: Reflective Design is Sengers, Boehner, David and Kaye, Critical Computing, Aarhus 2005, with six principles and six strategies. Supple Interfaces is Isbister and Höök, a CHI 2007 workshop on interfaces that "build richer connections between people as well as deeper emotional experiences." Designing for the Self is Zimmerman, CHI 2009, on products that help people "become the person they desire to be." Details are in the Background section below.
- **Sources:** p. 939 (Lakatos's three terms; Kuhn, Newton, Feynman); p. 940 ("predict novel facts", "dynamic machine", ambiguity test); p. 941 (fertility, "not a flaw", hedges, "sometimes right", "provisional, contingent, and aspirational", "relative productivity"); p. 946 (references 19, 24, 5, 3). Lakatos on degenerating programmes: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/lakatos/ (section 2.2).

## Background for the presenter

1. **Popper and Kuhn, the two accounts Gaver plays off.** Karl Popper (born Vienna 1902, died 1994) made demarcation, telling science from non-science, the central problem of philosophy of science. His criterion is falsifiability: a theory is scientific if some possible observation would conflict with it. He set this out in Logik der Forschung (1935; English as The Logic of Scientific Discovery, 1959). The text Gaver quotes, "Science: Conjectures and Refutations," began as a 1953 lecture and is chapter 1 of Conjectures and Refutations (1963); Gaver cites it as ref. 24 (p. 946). Thomas Kuhn (1922 to 1996) published The Structure of Scientific Revolutions in 1962, with a second edition in 1970, with its Postscript dated 1969, the one Gaver cites (ref. 17, p. 946). Normal science is puzzle-solving inside a paradigm. Kuhn's two senses of paradigm, the broad "disciplinary matrix" and the narrow "exemplar," are what Gaver describes on p. 941 as "sometimes an exemplar of successful research, and sometimes a set of common understandings."

2. **Lakatos, and why Neptune is the textbook case.** Imre Lakatos (1922 to 1974) worked at the London School of Economics. His full account is "Falsification and the Methodology of Scientific Research Programmes" (1970). Terms: the hard core, protected "by methodological fiat"; the negative heuristic, which forbids attacking the core; the protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses; and the positive heuristic, which guides how the belt changes. A programme is progressive if each new version has excess empirical content and some of it is corroborated, and degenerating if not (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, section 2.2). Neptune is the clean success: Uranus (found 1781) misbehaved, Le Verrier and Adams each predicted an unseen planet, and Galle found it in Berlin on 23 September 1846 within about a degree of Le Verrier's position. Mercury's perihelion is the matching failure: no extra body existed, and the anomaly waited for Einstein. Ref. 19 is not the 1970 paper but the 1973 Open University radio talk "Science and pseudoscience." We read Gaver's use as a fair summary of the picture but a loose use of the test (see the novel-facts question above).

3. **Feynman, in context.** Both passages come from lecture 7, "Seeking New Laws," of The Character of Physical Law: the Messenger Lectures Feynman gave at Cornell in November 1964, recorded by the BBC, published in 1965 by the BBC and from 1967 in paperback by MIT Press. In that edition the "First we guess it" passage is on p. 156 and "You need courage to say the experiments must be wrong" on p. 163, which matches Gaver's page numbers. Two things that would impress if raised: Feynman qualifies the first passage at once, "I mean after the experiment has been checked"; and he ends the weak-decay passage with "I will explain where that courage comes from later." We read Feynman as not seeing the two passages as inconsistent; Gaver reads the tension into them (p. 939). We think that is a fair rhetorical use, not a misquotation.

4. **The three manifestos Gaver names on p. 941.** *Reflective Design* (Sengers, Boehner, David and Kaye, Critical Computing, Aarhus 2005, pp. 49 to 58): reflection on unconscious values in computing should be a core principle of design. Its six principles: designers should use reflection to uncover and alter the limitations of design practice; designers should use reflection to re-understand their own role; designers should support users in reflecting on their lives; technology should support skepticism about and reinterpretation of its own working; reflection is folded into action, not separate from it; dialogic engagement between designers and users through technology can enhance reflection. Two case studies: technology in an art museum, and Intimate Objects for couples in long-distance relationships. *Supple Interfaces* (Isbister and Höök, CHI 2007 extended abstracts, pp. 2853 to 2856, ref. 16) was a CHI 2007 workshop; its call described systems that "build richer connections between people as well as deeper emotional experiences," with "an emphasis on the quality of experience rather than outcome," for example affective systems and games. *Designing for the Self* (Zimmerman, CHI 2009, pp. 395 to 404, ref. 36) applies product attachment theory: products should help people "become the person they desire to be" in a specific role, for example an alarm clock that keeps young children in bed and helps parents feel like better parents.

5. **Two details on the slide 04.1 sources worth having ready.** First, Stolterman (International Journal of Design 2(1), 2008, pp. 55 to 65) is less anti-science than his use in this essay might suggest: on p. 60 he writes that "the argument here is not that design research cannot be done in a scientific way," and his "prepared-for-action" section on p. 61 rests on Schön's reflection-in-action. We read him as a middle position, though Gaver quotes him accurately. Second, Product Ecology (Forlizzi, International Journal of Design 2(1), 2008, pp. 11 to 20) was illustrated with a year-long study in which households received either a Roomba or an upright vacuum; the Roomba changed roles and routines in the home, the upright did not. That is a concrete answer if someone asks what a "framework" produced.
