**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** Move two starts from an observation. Gaver says there must be dozens of design manifestos, and asks why the field does not converge. He offers three readings. First, Kuhn. Without a shared paradigm, everyone argues from first principles, and in Gaver's words such research "must founder." He says this reading is "seemingly behind recent calls" for standards. Second, Latour. Science looks unified from a distance but is full of controversy up close, and design researchers already share a lot: Gaver says "most of us" value contact with the people we design for, a wide design space, craft, and making as a route to discovery. He hedges: "Perhaps we do not all agree." Third, many worlds, a phrase from Law and Urry. Design makes new worlds. After the iPad, every tablet designer works in a world with an iPad in it. Then shared standards are "potentially repressive acts of ontological politics." We argue three things. The readings are not exclusive, and the "most of us" list is already a draft of the shared description the critics asked for. Gaver's own summary grants pre-paradigmatic work "valuable contributions," yet says it "must founder." And if every criterion is ontological politics, so is valuing proliferation. **[classmate]** Which reading would you pick?
- **Transition:** If the field will not converge on one theory, what should theory do instead? Gaver's answer is annotation.
- **Watch out:**
  - Pitfall 6. "Many worlds" and "ontological politics" are Law and Urry's phrases, which Gaver applies to design (p. 943). The iPad example is Gaver's own (p. 943). The claim is "potentially repressive," never "repressive."
  - Pitfall 5. The shared-values list (p. 942) is hedged twice: "most of us ... seem to share" and "Perhaps we do not all agree with all of these assumptions all of the time." Say both hedges if anyone pushes.
  - "Must founder" is Gaver's sentence summarising a Kuhnian view (p. 942), not a quotation from Kuhn. Our point (b) is an internal tension in Gaver's own summary, which on the same page says "Valuable contributions may be made by disparate approaches." Kuhn's own text grants the early schools "significant contributions" (Structure, chapter II; see the If asked block and the log), so "must founder" is Gaver's stronger wording, and you may say so.
  - Latour is cited, not quoted. "characterises scientific activity in terms of controversy" is Gaver's summary of Latour [20] (p. 942).
  - A fourth test point, kept out of the Say for time: the critics' words on p. 937 ask for standards, protocols and guidelines, not for a single paradigm, so this section answers "must there be one paradigm" more than "can there be standards." Best reply from Gaver's side: on p. 941 he frames the calls for reform as appeals for agreement about "'methods, approaches, and outcomes'," so on his framing the convergence question does address them. He also answers standards directly, once in each of the other two readings: "calls for standardisation, formalisation, overarching theory and the like are misplaced" (p. 942) and shared standards may be "potentially repressive" (p. 943); section 08 softens its version of this point the same way. Our narrow answer: his strongest reading, Kuhn's, answers the paradigm question; the standards question gets a sentence each.
  - A sharp classmate may quote p. 941 against us: Gaver himself sees "the desirability of clearly articulating the methodological and conceptual features of current research through design practice, as well as the standards one might want to advocate for them." Concede it. It supports point (a): Gaver is not against articulating standards; his targets are convergence, formalisation and standardisation.
  - Gaver softens the many-worlds point in his conclusion: norms of process or outcome are "political acts to be approached with care," "greater consensus may be emerging," and "convergence may not be the only or best model for progress" (p. 945 to 946). Use this if someone says point (c) is unfair; it shows Gaver's final position is milder than "potentially repressive."
  - The verdict strip comes from the essay's plan on p. 938, not from the convergence section itself. Gaver's wording there for the first reading is that it "implies that the community should work towards greater shared understandings." "Converge" in the table is our shorthand for that.

- **If asked:**
  - *Q: What does Kuhn mean by "paradigm," and which sense is Gaver using?* A: Gaver gives both senses on p. 941: "an exemplar of successful research" and "a set of common understandings." Kuhn split them himself in the 1969 Postscript to the 1970 second edition, which is the edition Gaver cites [17]. One sense is the whole "constellation of beliefs, values, techniques, and so on shared by the members of a given community," which Kuhn renamed the "disciplinary matrix"; the other is the concrete puzzle-solutions used as models (Structure, pp. 175 and 182). Kuhn called the exemplar sense "the deeper of the two" (p. 175). We read Gaver's portfolios in section 06 as building on that exemplar sense.
  - *Q: Does Kuhn really say pre-paradigmatic research "must founder"?* A: No. Those are Gaver's words, in his summary of a Kuhnian view (p. 942). Kuhn, writing about optics before Newton, says "all these schools made significant contributions" and "Those men were scientists," yet "the net result of their activity was something less than science" (Structure, ch. II, p. 13). So Gaver's "does not really add up to a science" (p. 942) is close to Kuhn, and "must founder" goes a step further. That is our point (b), now checked against Kuhn.
  - *Q: Where does the claim come from that Kuhn was inspired by quarrelling social scientists?* A: From Kuhn's own preface. He spent 1958 to 1959 at what he calls the Center for Advanced Studies in the Behavioral Sciences, "in a community composed predominantly of social scientists," and was "struck by the number and extent of the overt disagreements between social scientists about the nature of legitimate scientific problems and methods" (Structure, pp. vii to viii). Trying to explain why natural scientists did not fight like that led him to "paradigms." Gaver cites this on p. 943. The irony is worth saying: design's lack of convergence puts it next to the very fields that gave Kuhn the idea.
  - *Q: What does Latour actually argue in "Science in Action"?* A: Latour (1987) studies science "in the making" rather than "ready made science." His image is a two-faced Janus: one face shows settled facts, the other shows open controversy. Settled facts and machines become "black boxes," a cyberneticians' term for something you only need to know by its input and output. Gaver uses the controversy half: up close, science "may well exhibit just as much uncertainty, interpretation and debate as does research through design" (p. 942).
  - *Q: Isn't "invisible consensus" just "we already agree, so leave us alone"?* A: Gaver's reason is more specific. Shared assumptions "are precisely those unlikely to be addressed in the literature," so we may "underestimate the discord of science" and "overestimate the divergence of research through design" (p. 942). And he hedges: "Perhaps we do not all agree" (p. 942). Our reply is point (a): if that consensus is real, writing it down is cheap, and on p. 941 Gaver himself endorses "clearly articulating" the field's features and standards.
  - *Q: What are Law and Urry actually saying, and are they talking about design?* A: No, about social science methods. Their abstract says "social inquiry and its methods are productive: they (help to) make social realities and social worlds." Gaver repeats two of their examples: labelling theory leading to de-institutionalisation, and British sociology bringing inequality into view (p. 943). The "multiple worlds" sentence closes their critique of three usual answers to inconsistent findings: some methods are better, methods are tools for different jobs, or methods give different perspectives. They also say "the relations of the world will put up greater or lesser resistances," so it is not "anything goes." Applying this to design is Gaver's move (p. 943).
  - *Q: Where does the phrase "ontological politics" come from?* A: Law and Urry credit it in a footnote: "Perhaps the term belongs to Foucault, but it has been brought to prominence, discussed, and developed recently by Annemarie Mol (1999, 2002)." Mol's 1999 chapter is "Ontological politics: a word and some questions," in Law and Hassard's "Actor Network Theory and After." The idea: if practices enact reality, then choosing among practices is a choice about what becomes real, and that is political. Gaver does not cite Mol; the phrase reaches him through [21].
  - *Q: What did Osborne and Rose show about public opinion?* A: Their 1999 paper in the British Journal of Sociology argues, against philosophers who say only natural sciences create phenomena, that the social sciences can and do create them. Their case is public opinion research in the USA and Britain, with the representative sample as the key technique; by the 1950s public opinion was treated as a real thing in the world. They add that such creation is difficult, contested and rarely successful. Gaver uses only the first half (p. 943).
  - *Q: Was the iPad really new when Gaver wrote?* A: Apple announced it on 27 January 2010 and sold the Wi-Fi model in the US from 3 April 2010. CHI 2012 ran 5 to 10 May 2012, so the example was about two years old. Gaver's wording is careful: tablets "were not widely known or available" (p. 943). He does not say tablets did not exist.
  - *Q: Collins [3] is in the reference list. Doesn't he help the critics?* A: Partly the reverse. Collins (1994) argues the natural sciences have had high consensus and rapid discovery since about the 17th century because of inherited lineages of research technology that generate new phenomena. The social sciences lack such lineages, and he says the cause is not too little empirical work, weak method or more ideological conflict. We read this as cutting against standards as the fix: on Collins's account, protocols and guidelines would not produce convergence. Gaver cites him only in passing, after the Kuhn preface point ("c.f. [3]," p. 943).

## Background for the presenter

- **Kuhn, "The Structure of Scientific Revolutions" (1962; second edition 1970, the one Gaver cites as [17]).** Normal science is "puzzle-solving" inside a shared paradigm; anomalies pile up, a crisis follows, and a revolution installs a new paradigm. Chapter II, "The Route to Normal Science," describes the pre-paradigm period: rival schools, fact-gathering that is "a far more nearly random activity," and writers who each "felt forced to build his field anew from its foundations" (pp. 13 and 15). Gaver's "from the very basics every time" (p. 942) is a close paraphrase of that line. Kuhn's other example is electricity before Franklin, where theories had "no more than a family resemblance" (p. 14). The schools usually end, Kuhn says, through "the triumph of one of the pre-paradigm schools" (p. 17), not through agreed rules.
- **Two Kuhn passages that would impress if raised.** First, on painting: "For many centuries ... painting was regarded as the cumulative discipline," while its goal was representation (p. 161). Second, in the Postscript: difficulties around "style in the arts may vanish if paintings can be seen to be modeled on one another rather than produced in conformity to some abstracted canons of style" (pp. 208 to 209). We read this as Kuhn himself pointing at Gaver's later move: learning from examples rather than from abstract rules. And on p. 160 Kuhn asks of the debates over whether some field is a science: "Can very much depend upon a definition of 'science'?" That is close to Gaver's "the futility of debating whether design is or should be a science" (p. 945).
- **Latour, "Science in Action" (Harvard University Press, 1987).** Its first rule of method is "We study science in action and not ready made science or technology." The two cases in the introduction are the DNA double helix and a Data General minicomputer, each followed back from a closed black box to the controversy that made it. Gaver cites Latour twice: for controversy on p. 942, and on p. 943 ("c.f. [20]") for the point that debates in design cannot use "evidence from a single, independent world as an ally." Latour's Rule 3 is the source of that idea: "Since the settlement of a controversy is the cause of Nature's representation, not its consequence, we can never use this consequence, Nature, to explain how and why a controversy has been settled." We read Gaver as going one step further for design: there is no single world for a settled controversy to point back to.
- **Law and Urry, "Enacting the social" (Economy and Society 33(3), 2004, pp. 390 to 410), and Mol.** Three claims: methods are performative, they "help to bring into being what they also discover"; if so, research is in "ontological politics"; and current social science methods still tend to make "a single world" fitted to nineteenth-century, nation-state politics. Their examples include labelling theory, British studies of inequality, the 1854 London cholera cases mapped around the Broad Street pump, and Durkheim on suicide rates. The phrase "ontological politics" they credit to Annemarie Mol (1999, in Law and Hassard, "Actor Network Theory and After," pp. 74 to 89). Worth knowing: Gaver uses [21] once before this section, on p. 940, where design "changes the context of its own activities [21]." We read that citation as the hinge between Move 1 and Move 2: the same generativity that makes design theory hard to falsify is what makes the field diverge.
- **Osborne and Rose (1999) and Collins (1994).** Osborne and Rose, "Do the social sciences create phenomena? The example of public opinion research," British Journal of Sociology 50(3), pp. 367 to 396: public opinion became a real, measurable object through survey methods and the representative sample, though such creation is hard and rarely succeeds. Collins, "Why the Social Sciences Won't Become High-Consensus, Rapid-Discovery Science," Sociological Forum 9(2), pp. 155 to 177: consensus in the natural sciences comes from lineages of research technology, which the social sciences lack. In two sentences: rapid discovery and consensus are driven by equipment that keeps producing new phenomena, not by better method or less ideology. Without such equipment, the social sciences will stay low-consensus.
- **Examples from the arts and architecture.** Gaver's own are new artistic movements, hemlines going up and down, and the redesign of household fittings (p. 943). A ready one from architecture: Mies van der Rohe's "Less is more" was answered by Robert Venturi's "Less is a bore," from "Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture," published by the Museum of Modern Art in 1966 and widely read as a founding text of the turn away from International Style modernism. Neither side refuted the other; each made buildings, which fits Gaver's "reactions, reconsiderations and fresh beginnings" (p. 943). For the iPad: announced 27 January 2010, on sale in the US 3 April 2010, about two years before CHI 2012 (5 to 10 May 2012).
