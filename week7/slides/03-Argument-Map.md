## Slide 03.1: The argument on one slide

**Time:** 1:30 · **Visual:** three rows, one per move, each with a premise cell on the left, a conclusion cell on the right, and the pages in small type; a fourth row across the bottom holds the essay's conclusion. The row for each move is revealed as it is spoken. No figure.

**On the slide**
- **Move 1, pp. 939 to 941.** By Popper's test, unfalsifiable: theory underspecifies design; design is generative. By Lakatos's test, no flaw if fertile. So: "provisional, contingent, and aspirational" (p. 937).
- **Move 2, pp. 941 to 943.** Why no convergence? Pre-paradigmatic, invisible consensus, or many worlds. Only the first says standardise.
- **Move 3, pp. 943 to 945.** Artefacts are "the definite facts" (p. 945). Theory annotates them. Annotated portfolios: "a serious form of theoretical contribution" (p. 945).
- **Conclusion, pp. 945 to 946.** Expect less of theory. Norms: "political acts to be approached with care" (p. 945). "consensus can come to look like constraint" (p. 946).

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** Here is the whole essay on one slide, so that from now on we can argue with it instead of retelling it. Three moves and a conclusion. Move one asks what kind of theory design produces. Gaver runs it past Popper: design theory cannot be falsified, because theory underspecifies design and because design is generative. Then he runs it past Lakatos, for whom a theory is judged by the programme it feeds, and unfalsifiability stops mattering. His label for design theory is provisional, contingent and aspirational. Move two asks why the field has dozens of manifestos and no convergence. He offers three readings. Only the first, Kuhn's pre-paradigmatic field, says the cure is standards. Move three says that if theory is provisional, the artefacts are the definite facts, so theory should annotate them, and a portfolio of related designs with annotations should count as a theoretical contribution. The conclusion follows: expect less of theory, treat norms as political acts, and remember that consensus can look like constraint. Each move rests on a premise we can test. The next three sections do that, one move at a time. (about 185 words)
- **Transition:** Move one first: what kind of theory does design produce, and by whose test?
- **Watch out:**
  - The three-part structure is Gaver's own: "The essay has three parts" (abstract, p. 937). We are not imposing it.
  - Do not say Move 1 concludes that design is not a science. Gaver's conclusion is that it "depends on the account of science one adopts" (p. 941). The slide says "by Popper's test" and "by Lakatos's test" for that reason.
  - "Only the first says standardise" is Gaver's own reading, p. 938: "one of these perspectives implies that the community should work towards greater shared understandings, the other two imply either that there is no problem to solve or that progress in this area will be marked by proliferation rather than agreement."
  - A sharp classmate may say Move 3 does not follow from Moves 1 and 2. Gaver connects them twice: "research through design should be appreciated for its proliferation of new realities, and its theory considered as annotation of the artefacts that are its fundamental achievement" (p. 941), and designers turn to examples "both because of the provisional nature of theory, and the definite nature of designed artefacts" (p. 943). Whether the inference holds is one of the tensions in section 11; say so and move on.
  - "Expect less of theory" is our compression of "we should moderate expectations of creating extensible and verifiable theory" (p. 945). If quoted back, give the full sentence.
  - [classmate] slot: if a commentary summarised the argument differently, for example as two parts, credit it here and ask the author later whether the map is fair.
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: Are the three moves logically independent, or does each one need the one before?* A: Partly independent. Move 1 and Move 2 can be taken apart: you could accept the Lakatos reading of theory and still think the field is pre-paradigmatic. But they share one premise, that design is generative: it is a subsection of Move 1 (p. 940), Gaver flags that it "has deeper implications ... as I will argue later" (p. 941), and it is the engine of the many-worlds reading (p. 943). Move 3 leans on both: theory is provisional (Move 1, p. 943) and portfolios reflect "the lack of convergence in the field as a virtue" (Move 2, p. 945). So we read the map as two roughly parallel moves feeding a third.
  - *Q: Which critic does each move answer?* A: Gaver says so himself on p. 938, where each part answers an assumption. Move 1 answers the demand for theory with "a level of extensibility and verifiability" (Zimmerman and Forlizzi 2008 [33], quoted p. 937). Move 2 answers calls for "standards, formalisation, protocols and guidelines" that assume convergence (p. 938), and he names them: "recent calls for formalism and standardisation [9, 35]" (p. 942), that is the CHI 2011 panel and Zimmerman, Stolterman and Forlizzi 2010. Move 3 answers the assumption that "an endless stream of design examples is an inadequate basis" for the field (p. 938), and he cites [33, 35] again where his annotation section opens (p. 943).
  - *Q: What does the abstract promise, and does the body deliver it?* A: The abstract promises three parts: two theories from the Philosophy of Science, three interpretations of diversity, and theory as annotation of portfolios (p. 937). The body delivers them in that order, pp. 939 to 945. Two small gaps: the abstract says "two influential theories," but the body also leans on Kuhn and Feynman (p. 939); and we read the abstract's "natural for a generative endeavour" as leaning towards the many-worlds reading, while the body presents three readings without formally choosing one (pp. 941 to 943).
  - *Q: Does "theory" mean the same thing in all three moves?* A: No, and that is worth saying. In Move 1 it means content-oriented design theories, the genres of p. 938, statements of the form "designing for X can lead to successful outcomes" (p. 940); he puts meta-theory, his own essay included, out of scope (p. 939). In Move 2 the unit is bigger: research programmes and paradigms, with manifestos "each seeking to establish a research programme" (p. 941). In Move 3 theory is annotation, which makes explicit what an artefact embodies, from "the philosophical" to "the aesthetic" (p. 944).
  - *Q: What exactly do "provisional," "contingent" and "aspirational" each mean?* A: The paper does not define them one by one. The triad appears four times, always together (pp. 937, 938, 941, 945). The nearest text is p. 941: theory "tends to underspecify practice and to be generative in nature, and thus that it is provisional, contingent, and aspirational." We read provisional as the "implicit sometimes" (p. 940), contingent as tied to "the specific circumstances of production and use" (p. 940), and aspirational as "creating what might be" (p. 940). That mapping is ours.
  - *Q: Doesn't Move 3 contradict Move 1? He says portfolios allow "extensibility and verifiability."* A: Yes, p. 945 says portfolios respect the ultimate particular "while allowing for the 'extensibility and verifiability' for which some of the HCI design community have called." Yet the conclusion says "we should moderate expectations of creating extensible and verifiable theory" (p. 945). We read this as Gaver reclaiming the opponents' words for a softer sense, and he does the same with "relevant and rigorous theoretical work" (p. 946), echoing [35] p. 316. He never says what verifying a portfolio would involve. Fair to raise; it belongs to the tensions in section 11.
  - *Q: Which of the three readings does Gaver actually hold?* A: He never says outright. The conclusion leans on the last two: "greater consensus may be emerging ... than is sometimes acknowledged" is the invisible-consensus reading, and "convergence may not be the only or best model for progress" is the many-worlds reading (pp. 945 to 946). The pre-paradigmatic reading he calls "a tempting conclusion" (p. 942). So we say he rejects only the first, and keeps two compatible alternatives open.
  - *Q: Isn't the conclusion inconsistent: consensus is emerging, but consensus is constraint?* A: Both lines are there, on pp. 945 and 946. They come from different readings, and Gaver hedges both with "may" and "can come to look like." We read the point as: there is more agreement than the critics think, and pushing for still more has a cost. That is a coherent position, though a classmate can fairly ask where the line falls.
  - *Q: Why Popper and Lakatos, of all philosophers?* A: Gaver says so himself: "Contrasting accounts are chosen to complicate notions of what makes theories 'scientific'" (p. 938), and that he is "not presenting Popper's and Lakatos' accounts as particularly representative of Philosophy of Science" (p. 941). He adds that the Philosophy of Science asks how to tell science from non-science, which is the question behind the standards debate (p. 939). The choice is a foil, not a survey.
  - *Q: Give me the whole essay in one sentence.* A: Our version: design theory is provisional rather than extensible and verifiable, the field's diversity need not be a failure, so theory should annotate artefacts, especially portfolios, and standards should be pushed with care. Gaver's own nearest sentence: "the role of theory should be to annotate those examples rather than replace them" (p. 938).
- **Sources:** p. 937 (abstract: three parts; "provisional, contingent, and aspirational"); p. 938 (which readings imply what); p. 939 to 941 (Popper, Lakatos, underspecification, generative); p. 941 (depends on the account of science; proliferation and annotation); p. 941 to 943 (three readings); p. 943 (provisional theory, definite artefacts); p. 943 to 945 (annotation, portfolios); p. 945 ("the definite facts," "a serious form of theoretical contribution," "political acts to be approached with care," "moderate expectations"); p. 946 ("consensus can come to look like constraint").

## Background for the presenter

1. **Gaver's own map of the essay is on p. 938, not only in the abstract.** The paragraph starting "In this essay, my intention is to temper calls for disciplinary consolidation" gives one assumption per part. First, that theory should be "extensible and verifiable"; his answer is "provisional, contingent and aspirational." Second, that "disciplinary convergence is a prerequisite for cumulative growth of understanding"; his answer is three perspectives, only one of which calls for shared understandings. Third, that "an endless stream of design examples is an inadequate basis"; his answer is that "the role of theory should be to annotate those examples rather than replace them." If anyone asks whether the slide imposes a structure, read this paragraph.

2. **What "theory" covers.** Gaver lists genres on p. 938: articulations of practice (Djajadiningrat et al. [6]), borrowed theory (affordances, context, situatedness), manifestos (Reflective Design [30], ludic design [13]), frameworks (Forlizzi's Product Ecology [10]), and a "final genre" of meta-theory ("Zimmerman & Forlizzi [34]," which the reference check shows should be [33], their 2008 paper; and Stolterman [31]) that runs onto p. 939. On p. 939 he says the essay is itself "intended as a contribution to meta-theory" but addresses "the more specific, content-oriented theories described earlier." We read this as a quiet scope limit: the Popper test in Move 1 is aimed at manifestos and frameworks, not at the critics' meta-theory.

3. **The threads that tie the moves together.** Three words recur across the moves. "Generative": Move 1 (p. 940, "Design Is Generative"), forward-flagged on p. 941, and the core of the many-worlds reading (p. 943, "a generative discipline, able to create multiple new worlds"). "Provisional": Move 1 (p. 941) and the reason designers turn to examples in Move 3 (p. 943, p. 944 "Theories may be provisional, but designed artefacts ... are not"). "Proliferation": the p. 938 plan, p. 941 ("proliferation of new realities"), and p. 945 (lack of convergence "as a virtue"). If the moves feel loosely connected, these words are the joints.

4. **Gaver turns the critics' words around.** The opponents' phrases come back in his favour. "Extensibility and verifiability" ([33], p. 937) returns as something annotated portfolios allow (p. 945). "Relevant and rigorous theory" ([35] p316, p. 937) returns as annotated portfolios furthering "our ambitions to produce relevant and rigorous theoretical work" (p. 946). And Zimmerman et al.'s "right thing" ([34]) is used to support the claim that design is generative (p. 940). We read this as deliberate. It makes the essay less of a rebuttal and more of a redefinition of the critics' own goals.

5. **Two facts beyond the paper.** First, Gaver's Lakatos source [19] is "Science and pseudoscience," dated 1977 in his list and "Reprinted in [3]" (p. 946; the [3] is the reference-list slip, the reprint is in Curd and Cover, reference 5). It began as a short radio talk: Open University course A303, programme 11, first broadcast 30 June 1973, 18 minutes 27 seconds. So the Lakatos of Move 1 is a popular summary, not the long 1970 methodology paper. Second, the annotated portfolio idea was developed further the same year in Gaver and Bowers, "Annotated Portfolios," Interactions 19(4), 2012, pp. 40 to 49. Footnote 1 of this paper already credits John Bowers (p. 944). Section 10 covers that follow-up.
