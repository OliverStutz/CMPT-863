# 10 What followed, 2012 to 2026

**Purpose:** Show where the field went after the essay, in three lanes (Gaver's way, the critics' way, both), so the class can judge the essay's influence before we give our own reading. **Slides:** 1. **Time:** 1:30. **Speaker:** Oliver. **Previous section:** 09 Connections. **Next section:** 11 Our reading.

## Slide 10.1: What followed: Gaver's way, the critics' way, or both?

**Time:** 1:30 · **Visual:** three-column table, one column per lane, each item as author, venue, year; one "we argue" line under each column; the three open questions as a single line across the bottom. Spell out "Research Through Design (RTD) conference" in the column if space allows; the notes say it in full. No figure from the paper.

**On the slide**
- **Gaver's way:** Gaver and Bowers 2012; Bowers, DIS 2012; RTD conference from 2013, artefacts exhibited; DIS Pictorials from 2014; Pierce, DIS 2014
  - We argue: the form found venues, not a rule.
- **The critics' way:** strong concepts, Höök and Löwgren 2012; bridging concepts, Dalsgaard and Dindler 2014; Zimmerman and Forlizzi 2014; Bardzell et al. 2016, documenting RtD
  - We argue: generalising never stopped.
- **Both:** Löwgren 2013; Frauenberger 2019; Beaudouin-Lafon, Bødker and Mackay 2021
  - We argue: portfolios became one form among several.
- **Open:** CHI reviewing? Generative AI's cheap "many worlds"? Lab budgets?

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** So did the field go Gaver's way? Partly. Right after CHI, Gaver and Bowers published "Annotated Portfolios" in Interactions, and Bowers argued at DIS that design research should not be brought in line with outside criteria for rigour. A Research Through Design conference started in 2013 and put artefacts on exhibition. DIS added Pictorials in 2014. Pierce asked for "thingly publications." We argue the form found venues, not a rule. The other lane kept going too. Höök and Löwgren proposed strong concepts, Dalsgaard and Dindler bridging concepts, both knowledge that sits above single designs. Zimmerman and Forlizzi wrote that they worked to formalise research through design. In fact, one 2014 book, Ways of Knowing in HCI, prints both: their chapter and a Gaver chapter that says annotated portfolios "may capture best what we can know through design." And Löwgren filed portfolios under intermediate-level knowledge. We argue that is Gaver's "serious form" surviving, while his warning against convergence faded. Three questions for later: has CHI reviewing moved toward him; does generative AI, which makes many worlds cheap, help or hurt portfolios; and do portfolios favour well-funded labs?
- **Transition:** So here is our reading.
- **Watch out:**
  - Same studio. Gaver and Bowers 2012 and Bowers 2012 come from Gaver's own studio at Goldsmiths. A sharp classmate will say that is continuation, not uptake. Agree. The independent evidence in Gaver's lane is the RTD conference, DIS Pictorials and Pierce.
  - "The critics' way" is our lane label, not a claim that these authors opposed Gaver. Höök and Löwgren, and Dalsgaard and Dindler, do not frame themselves as answering him in the abstracts we checked. Say "toward generalisable or formalised knowledge," and do not call anyone a positivist (pitfall 13).
  - Bridging concepts could sit in "both": the abstract says they need "a theoretical foundation, a set of design articulations and a range of exemplars" and are "accountable to both theory and practice." If challenged, concede the move.
  - Bardzell et al. 2016 is a framework "for planning and evaluating RtD documentation." That speaks to the 2010 call for protocols for "processes" (p. 937), but it does not ask for design to become a science (pitfall 2).
  - Beaudouin-Lafon, Bødker and Mackay use the word "generative," as Gaver does (p. 940), but their theories draw on empirical theories to give "actionable principles." Do not say they cite or answer Gaver; we did not check their reference list.
  - Frauenberger's entanglement paper speaks of "ontological uncertainties" and moving "beyond user-centred design." It echoes Law and Urry's ontological politics (p. 943) but calls itself a possible "next wave," which is paradigm talk. That is why it sits in "both."
  - Gaver never said portfolios should be the only form (pitfall 9, p. 945). So "portfolios became one kind among others" is not a defeat for him on his own terms. The defeat, if any, is for "wary of impulses towards convergence and standardisation" (p. 937).
  - CHI reviewing: Zimmerman and Forlizzi report that, by 2014, CHI had "two technical papers committees devoted to design research" (p. 178). We have no checked fact about CHI reviewing after that. Keep the question open.
  - Influence is not proven by chronology. Items after 2012 may cite the essay or not; we checked existence, venue and abstract, not citation.
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: Did these later works actually cite Gaver's essay, or is this just chronology?* A: Some did. Höök and Löwgren 2012, Bowers 2012, Löwgren 2013, Pierce 2014 and Bardzell et al. 2016 all list it in their references. For Dalsgaard and Dindler 2014, Frauenberger 2019 and Beaudouin-Lafon, Bødker and Mackay 2021, the reference lists deposited at Crossref do not include it, so we claim no link there. OpenAlex counts about 1,100 citations to the essay as of October 2026.
  - *Q: What exactly is an annotated portfolio, in Gaver and Bowers's own terms?* A: Löwgren sums it up as selecting a collection of designs, re-presenting them in a suitable medium, and adding brief textual annotations. Gaver and Bowers, as Löwgren quotes them, offer it "as a way to communicate design research" and as "an alternative to accounts that suggest for design to become productive as research, it should engage in some sort of theory formation." In the essay, what defines it is a balance between detailed examples and "articulations of the issues, values and themes" that link them (p. 944).
  - *Q: Didn't Löwgren and Bowers disagree about what the annotations are?* A: Yes. Löwgren reports that Bowers sees annotations as "purely indexical and inseparable from their particular artifacts," not abstractions. Löwgren reads them as abstractions with a wider scope, and quotes Gaver, in personal communication, that a portfolio "affords abstraction (in the sense of extraction) by others." So even friendly readers argued about the status of the annotation within a year.
  - *Q: What is a strong concept, briefly?* A: Höök and Löwgren call it intermediate-level design knowledge: generative, carrying a core design idea across use situations, about interactive behaviour rather than static appearance, part of an artefact while speaking of use over time, and above particular instances. Their examples are social navigation and seamfulness. They judge such knowledge by whether it is "contestable, defensible, and substantive."
  - *Q: How is a bridging concept different from a strong concept?* A: Dalsgaard and Dindler say a bridging concept has three parts: "a theoretical foundation, a set of design articulations and a range of exemplars." The explicit theoretical foundation is the difference; that is why they call it accountable to both theory and practice. Their worked example is "peepholes," a concept for spurring curiosity and engagement.
  - *Q: Did Zimmerman and Forlizzi answer Gaver in 2014?* A: Not directly. Their chapter does not cite the 2012 essay (reference list, pp. 187 to 189). They say they "worked to formalize" the approach (p. 178) and give five steps: select, design, evaluate, reflect and disseminate, repeat (p. 184). They also call Gaver's Drift Table "one of the earliest and best examples of RtD published within HCI" (p. 175). We read that as neither rebuttal nor concession.
  - *Q: Isn't Zimmerman and Forlizzi's "repeat" step close to Gaver anyway?* A: We think so. They write that "It is through the development of research programs much more than through individual projects that the best results emerge" (p. 186). Gaver says "As artefacts are to theory ... design portfolios are to research programmes" (p. 944). Both value programmes of related work; they differ on whether a protocol should steer them.
  - *Q: What does Gaver's 2014 chapter add?* A: A new name for the difference. Science has "epistemological accountability," the duty to defend the basis of its knowledge; design has "aesthetic accountability," the duty to show that a design works (p. 147 of the chapter). He warns that imposing methodological frameworks on design trades away a "productive indiscipline" (p. 162). We read it as moving past the 2012 "depends on the account of science one adopts" (p. 941) to a positive standard of his own.
  - *Q: The RTD conference stopped. Doesn't that weaken "the form found venues"?* A: Partly, and we should concede it. It ran in 2013 at the BALTIC in Gateshead, then Cambridge 2015, Edinburgh 2017 and Delft and Rotterdam 2019, then paused after 2019 because of the pandemic. Its steering committee announced in July 2026 that it returns in 2027 as "RTD Roams," at three sites: York, Melbourne and Tokyo.
  - *Q: Are DIS Pictorials really Gaver's way?* A: The format talks his language. The DIS 2026 call says: "In papers images illustrate the text, while in pictorials text is used to annotate the visuals." Pictorials have a 12-page limit excluding references, dual anonymous review, and count as the same quality as papers. But a pictorial need not be a portfolio, so we claim a venue for the form, not his influence.
  - *Q: Has CHI reviewing moved toward Gaver?* A: There is one sign. The CHI 2026 Design subcommittee invites work that explores "alternatives to scientistic ways of knowing." Design drew 485 papers in 2026, and 37.9% went on to revise and resubmit, against 38.7% overall. A scope statement is not reviewing practice, so the question stays open.
  - *Q: What makes you think generative AI matters for portfolios?* A: Two CHI 2024 papers. Suh et al.'s Luminate notes that language models can produce "hundreds and thousands" of outputs, yet current interfaces push users "towards rapid convergence on a limited set of ideas." Davis et al. found that graphical design-space interfaces supported ideation better than text prompts. Neither is about research through design, so the link to Gaver's "definite nature of designed artefacts" (p. 943) is our question, not their finding.

## Background for the presenter

**Gaver and Bowers, "Annotated portfolios," Interactions 2012.** The magazine version of the idea, written in the same studio. They propose annotated portfolios "as a way to communicate design research" and as an alternative to the view that design must "engage in some sort of theory formation" to count as research. They add that one should "grow design's identity as research from its existing practices and reasoning" rather than mutate design "to become more like 'real' research" (both as quoted by Löwgren 2013; the Goldsmiths full text is restricted). Their worked example starts with two pieces for older people, with annotations such as "influencing autonomous drift," then adds three earlier studio pieces (Löwgren's summary). What it adds to Gaver 2012: a worked portfolio, which the essay only sketched with Rams (p. 945).

**Bowers, "The logic of annotated portfolios," DIS 2012.** Bowers reads research through design as an orientation to "Third Wave" HCI and discusses the "disciplinary anxieties" it raised. Drawing on Feyerabend and the sociology of science, he argues design research might build its own "limited rationality" rather than follow outside norms of rigour. The portfolio captures "family resemblances" across a collection, shown on "nine well-known pieces created by the Goldsmiths Interaction Research Studio." What it adds: the philosophical backing that the essay's footnote 1 credits to Bowers (p. 944).

**Löwgren, "Annotated portfolios and other forms of intermediate-level knowledge," Interactions 2013.** Löwgren places portfolios on a scale from single artefacts to general theory and lists other intermediate forms, roughly in date order: design methods and tools; design guidelines and usability heuristics; patterns; concepts (the Dynabook) and strong concepts; experiential qualities; and criticism, with an "interaction criticism" discipline. He agrees general theory is of limited value but says theory "can be generative for design at times." He suggests rigour for such knowledge could come from "horizontal and vertical grounding." What it takes from Gaver: portfolios become one form among several, which is our "both" lane.

**Höök and Löwgren, "Strong concepts," TOCHI 2012.** Strong concepts are intermediate-level knowledge "more abstracted than particular instances, without aspiring to be at the scope of generalized theories." They are generative, carry a core design idea across use situations and domains, concern interactive behaviour rather than static appearance, and sit above particular instances. The examples are social navigation and seamfulness; the criteria are "contestable, defensible, and substantive." The paper cites Gaver 2012. We read its stated aim, "an academic culture of discursive knowledge construction," as close to Gaver's "discursiveness and elaboration" (p. 946), though it seeks knowledge above single designs.

**Dalsgaard and Dindler, "Between theory and practice: bridging concepts in HCI research," CHI 2014.** A bridging concept is intermediary knowledge between theory and practice with three parts: "a theoretical foundation, a set of design articulations and a range of exemplars." The paper compares it with strong concepts and conceptual constructs, and works through "peepholes." What it adds: an explicit tie to theory, which Gaver treats as secondary to artefacts (p. 945).

**Zimmerman and Forlizzi, "Research Through Design in HCI," Ways of Knowing in HCI, 2014.** The chapter tells the history of their 2007 model, says CHI now has "two technical papers committees devoted to design research" (p. 178), and that they "worked to formalize this research approach" (p. 178). It recommends five steps: select, design, evaluate, reflect and disseminate, repeat (p. 184). Teams choose a practice from Koskinen et al.'s Lab, Field and Showroom (p. 184) and find exemplars. The chapter does not cite Gaver 2012, but it names the Drift Table and Prayer Companion as exemplars (pp. 175, 186 to 187; from the studio's other publications, pitfall 12).

**Gaver, "Science and Design: The Implications of Different Forms of Accountability," same volume, 2014.** Science is defined by "epistemological accountability," being able to "explain and defend the basis of one's claimed knowledge"; design by "aesthetic accountability," showing "that one's design works," where "aesthetic" means how well many features are composed (p. 147). He argues methodological frameworks can cost design its "productive indiscipline" (p. 162) and states that "Annotated portfolios may capture best what we can know through design" (p. 161). We read it as the essay's sequel.

**Research Through Design (RTD) conference.** Biennial: 2013 at the BALTIC in Gateshead, 2015 Cambridge, 2017 Edinburgh, 2019 Delft and Rotterdam. Its format is "a curated exhibition of design research artefacts accompanied by round-table discussions in 'Rooms of Interest'," which the organisers say is inspired by the design school "crit." It paused after 2019 and returns in 2027 as RTD Roams in York, Melbourne and Tokyo.

**DIS Pictorials.** Introduced at DIS 2014 in Vancouver. Rules in the DIS 2026 call: at most 12 pages excluding references, an abstract of at most 150 words, the DIS pictorial template, dual anonymous review, ACM archival status, the same quality as papers. Visuals lead: "text is used to annotate the visuals." TEI and Creativity and Cognition also run pictorial tracks.

**Pierce, "On the presentation and production of design research artifacts in HCI," DIS 2014.** Pierce reviews examples of research through design and sorts design research artefacts into three types: operational prototypes and products, conceptual and material design studies and experiments, and design proposals. He separates verbal articulation, design articulation and "concept-things," and ends with three provocations: "thingly publications, pure design research, and making design research artifacts more public." It cites Gaver 2012. What it adds: a push to publish the artefact itself, not just a paper about it.

**Bardzell, Bardzell, Dalsgaard, Gross and Halskov, "Documenting the Research Through Design Process," DIS 2016.** From two contrasting cases they build "a framework for planning and evaluating RtD documentation" with three core concerns: "the medium of documentation, the performativity of documentation, and providing equal support for both research and design." It cites Gaver 2012. We read it as procedure for the process, which is what the 2010 call asked for (p. 937), without a scientific model.

**Frauenberger, "Entanglement HCI The Next Wave?," TOCHI 2019.** Our entanglement with technology raises "ontological uncertainties, epistemological diffusion and ethical conundrums." Drawing on Actor-Network Theory, post-phenomenology, object-oriented ontology and agential realism, he gives four perspectives: (a) the performative relationship between humans and technology; (b) knowledge generation reframed around phenomena; (c) tracing accountabilities, responsibilities and ethical encounters; (d) design and mattering practices beyond user-centred design.

**Beaudouin-Lafon, Bødker and Mackay, "Generative Theories of Interaction," TOCHI 2021.** A generative theory draws on empirical theories of human behaviour to define concepts and "actionable principles" for analysing, critiquing and constructing new artefacts. Their three examples are Instrumental Interaction, Human-Computer Partnerships, and Communities and Common Objects, with tables of questions for analysis, critique and construction. Gaver's "generative" (p. 940) means making new worlds; theirs means theory that guides design.

**Facts for the three open questions.**
- CHI reviewing. (1) The CHI 2026 Design subcommittee invites "suggestions and provocations exploring new design tools, processes, methods, or principles, including those that explore alternatives to scientistic ways of knowing." (2) CHI 2026 had eighteen subcommittees and 6,730 paper submissions; Design had 485, with 37.9% moving to revise and resubmit.
- Generative AI. (1) Suh et al., Luminate, CHI 2024: language models can produce "hundreds and thousands" of outputs, but current interfaces push "towards rapid convergence on a limited set of ideas." (2) Davis et al., CHI 2024: graphical interfaces for design space exploration "Better Support Ideation Than Text Prompts" (title).
- Lab budgets. (1) The ThirdWaveHCI grant acknowledged in the essay (p. 946), ERC grant 226528, had an EU contribution of 2,439,757 euros, 1 April 2009 to 31 December 2014, held at Goldsmiths. (2) Its objective was a methodology built on deploying and studying 50 to 100 batch-produced prototypes in real settings. That shows the studio's scale; that portfolios favour such labs is our argument.
