# 01 Background

**Purpose:** Put the room in 2012: what research through design is, who William Gaver is, and who asked for standards between 2007 and 2011. **Slides:** 2. **Time:** 3:00. **Speaker:** Oliver. **Previous section:** title slide. **Next section:** 02 The problem.

## Slide 01.1: Research through design, and whose studio this is

**Time:** 1:30 · **Visual:** three stacked bands. Top band "The term": Frayling 1993, then Zimmerman et al. 2007, then Gaver's one-line definition (p. 937). Middle band "The author": name and affiliation (p. 937) with three small photos of studio artefacts, captioned with year and "from the studio's other publications." Bottom band: the grant line (p. 946) and the "we note" line.

**On the slide**
- Frayling 1993: research into, through and for art and design [11]
- Zimmerman, Forlizzi and Evenson, CHI 2007: research through design (RtD) as a named HCI method [34]
- Gaver: design practice "brought to bear on situations chosen for their topical and theoretical potential"; output "primarily, of artefacts and systems" (p. 937)
- William Gaver, Interaction Research Studio, Goldsmiths, University of London (p. 937)
- Studio's other work: Drift Table (2004), History Tablecloth (2006), Prayer Companion (2010)
- Funding: ERC grant 'ThirdWaveHCI' (p. 946)
- **We note:** a practitioner whose output is a portfolio proposes portfolios as theory

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** Three things before the argument. First, the term. Christopher Frayling, at the Royal College of Art in 1993, separated research into design, research for design and research through design. The third one means the making is the method. In 2007, Zimmerman, Forlizzi and Evenson brought research through design into CHI as a named method for interaction design research. Gaver's own definition, on page 937: design practice "brought to bear on situations chosen for their topical and theoretical potential," with output "primarily, of artefacts and systems." Second, the author. William Gaver works at the Interaction Research Studio at Goldsmiths. You may know the studio's work from its other publications: the Drift Table, a coffee table showing slowly drifting aerial photos; the History Tablecloth, which glows under objects left on it; the Prayer Companion, made for a convent of nuns. Third, the funding line: a European Research Council grant called ThirdWaveHCI. We note one thing and only note it: the essay proposes portfolios as theory, and a portfolio is the form the studio's own work already takes.
- **Transition:** So that is the practitioner; now the people he was answering, and what they asked for.
- **Watch out:**
  - The "we note" line is an observation, not a motive. If challenged ("so he is defending his own lab"), answer: we cannot know his motive, and the essay should be judged on its argument. Pitfall 14.
  - None of the three artefacts is mentioned in this paper. Say "from the studio's other publications" every time (pitfall 12). The Drift Table paper is Goldsmiths-listed under the studio, but do not claim where it was built in 2004; we did not verify the studio's location that year.
  - Frayling's labels vary in secondary sources: "into, through, for" in the RCA's own 2021 conference paper, "in, through, for" in Biggs 2002. Gaver cites Frayling only as reference [11] (p. 937, 946) and does not use the three labels himself. The RCA catalogue dates the paper 1993/4.
  - Gaver's definition cites three sources, "[4, 11, 34]" (p. 937): Crampton-Smith and Tabor 1996, Frayling 1993, Zimmerman et al. 2007. The definition sentence is Gaver's.
  - "Third wave HCI" (only if asked): Bødker's 2006 NordiCHI paper uses "third wave" for HCI moving beyond the workplace into homes and everyday life, with themes of multiplicity, context, experience and participation. The CORDIS project summary describes the grant's aim in similar terms, interaction as meaning-making in everyday life. The paper itself names only the grant (p. 946).
  - CORDIS lists Goldsmiths as host and the Interaction Research Studio as doing the work; it does not name Gaver as principal investigator on the pages we checked. Say "the grant that funded this essay," not "Gaver's grant."
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: What exactly were Frayling's three categories?* A: In the best secondary account we found, research into art and design is the traditional kind: historical, aesthetic or theoretical research, written up as a thesis. Research through art and design is materials research, development work and action research, where studio work is documented and reported. Research for art and design is work where the thinking is embodied in the artefact itself, so it is not verbally communicable; Frayling called that one thorny. Gaver cites Frayling as [11] (p. 937) but never uses the three labels.
  - *Q: Is Gaver's research through design the same thing as Frayling's?* A: The paper does not say. Gaver's definition puts the weight on the artefact: output "primarily, of artefacts and systems" (p. 937). We read that as sitting between Frayling's "through", which stresses documented process, and his "for", where the artefact carries the knowledge. That is our reading, not Gaver's.
  - *Q: What did Zimmerman, Forlizzi and Evenson actually propose in 2007?* A: Two things: a model and four criteria. In the model, designers integrate "true" knowledge from behavioural scientists, "how" knowledge from engineers and "real" knowledge from anthropologists, and try to make "the right thing," a product that moves the world from its current state to a preferred state (their pp. 493, 497). The four lenses for judging a contribution are process, invention, relevance and extensibility (p. 499). They illustrate them with three cases: Xerox copier interfaces, Philips' Vision of the Future, and Apple Guides (pp. 500 to 501).
  - *Q: Where does Gaver use "the right thing"?* A: On p. 940, in the section "Design Is Generative": design is concerned "with creating what might be, and moreover, in Zimmerman et al.'s [34] formulation, on making the 'right thing'." He uses their phrase to support his own point that design makes, rather than describes. So he borrows from the people he argues against.
  - *Q: Did Zimmerman and colleagues invent research through design in HCI?* A: No, and they say so: "the idea is not new within the HCI and interaction design research community" (Zimmerman et al. 2007, p. 499). They base their model on Frayling (p. 493). What they did was name it and propose criteria. They even cite the Drift Table as "a well known example of critical design in HCI" (p. 496).
  - *Q: Who is Gaver, beyond this paper?* A: He trained as a psychologist: a PhD at UC San Diego in 1988 on everyday listening and auditory icons. His 1986 paper defined auditory icons as caricatures of everyday sounds, and he built the SonicFinder, an auditory interface, at Apple. In 1999, at the Royal College of Art, he published "Cultural Probes" with Tony Dunne and Elena Pacenti. So he came to design from psychology and cognitive science. We think that background matters when he writes about science; that is our reading.
  - *Q: What were those three artefacts for?* A: All three come from the studio's other publications, not this paper. The Drift Table (CHI 2004) is a coffee table showing slowly moving aerial photography, steered by weight placed on its surface, built to study ludic, curiosity-driven use at home. The History Tablecloth (DIS 2006) lights up under objects left on it, as a halo that grows over hours. The Prayer Companion (CHI 2010) showed a stream of news and social media items to cloistered nuns as possible topics for prayer.
  - *Q: What is "third wave HCI," and what did the grant aim to do?* A: Bødker (NordiCHI 2006) says the third wave broadens use contexts beyond the workplace, into homes, everyday life and culture. Harrison, Tatar and Sengers (alt.chi 2007) tell a similar story as three paradigms. The CORDIS summary says the grant aimed to "help bring to maturity the emerging paradigm of third-wave HCI," with two case studies and 50 to 100 batch-produced prototypes in large field trials. The paper itself only names the grant (p. 946).
  - *Q: Isn't the "we note" line just an ad hominem?* A: It would be if we used it to dismiss the argument; we do not. It tells the room what kind of evidence Gaver knows best. He says himself that he writes "as a long-term practitioner of research through design" (p. 937), so the position is on the record, not our guess. The argument still has to stand on pp. 939 to 945.
  - *Q: Is Gaver still at Goldsmiths?* A: In 2012, yes (p. 937). The studio's own page says it was set up in its present form at Goldsmiths in 2006, with roots in Computer Related Design Research at the Royal College of Art in the late nineties, and moved to Northumbria University in late 2021. Gaver is now Professor of Design there.

## Slide 01.2: 2007 to 2012: the call for standards

**Time:** 1:30 · **Visual:** a horizontal timeline with five dots (2007, 2008, 2010, 2011, 2012); under it three quote cards from p. 937, each tagged with its source; R4's line as a footer strip from p. 946.

**On the slide**
- 2007 Zimmerman, Forlizzi, Evenson, CHI: RtD as a method [34]
- 2008 Zimmerman, Forlizzi: artifacts and theory [33]
- 2010 Zimmerman, Stolterman, Forlizzi, DIS: towards a formalization [35]
- 2011 CHI panel "Quality control", Forlizzi et al. [9]
- 2012 Gaver, CHI, Critical Perspectives on Design
- RtD "lacks clear expectations and standards"; needs "actionable metrics" (panel [9], quoted p. 937)
- "develop protocols, descriptions, and guidelines" ([35], quoted p. 937)
- "a level of extensibility and verifiability" ([33], quoted p. 937)
- Reviewer R4: "an elephant in the room...: the move towards 'scientism' in HCI" (p. 946)

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** Now the other side, in their own words. Between 2007 and 2011 the people who brought research through design into CHI started asking for standards. Gaver quotes the 2011 CHI panel, "Quality control": research through design "lacks clear expectations and standards for what constitutes good design research," and needs "some actionable metrics for bringing rigor in critique." He quotes Zimmerman, Stolterman and Forlizzi at DIS 2010: the community should "develop protocols, descriptions, and guidelines," "like any other research approach," and build "a proper research methodology that can produce relevant and rigorous theory." And Zimmerman and Forlizzi in 2008 want theory with "a level of extensibility and verifiability." Keep those words in mind: standards, protocols, guidelines, extensible theory. They did not ask design to become a science. One more detail. An anonymous reviewer, R4, read this essay as addressing "an elephant in the room": "the move towards scientism in HCI." Gaver thanks that reviewer on page 946. So at review, this was already read as an essay about scientism.
- **Transition:** So the critics asked for standards and theory; what exactly does Gaver think is wrong with that? That is the problem in section 02.
- **Watch out:**
  - Pitfall 2: never paraphrase the opponents into "design should be a science." Quote them. Attribution is fixed: [9] the CHI 2011 panel; [35] Zimmerman, Stolterman and Forlizzi 2010; [33] Zimmerman and Forlizzi 2008; [34] Zimmerman, Forlizzi and Evenson 2007.
  - Pitfall 3: "extensibility and verifiability" are Zimmerman and Forlizzi's words. "Provisional, contingent, and aspirational" are Gaver's, and belong to section 04.
  - Stolterman is on both sides. He co-wrote [35], which calls for formalization, and he is also Gaver's source for the "ultimate particular" (p. 939, 944), the idea Gaver leans on most in section 06. If a classmate says the field split into two camps, this is the answer: the same people held parts of both views.
  - Reference [33] is listed in the paper as "Human-Computer Interaction Institute. Paper 37" (p. 946). It is the Carnegie Mellon repository copy of an article in the journal Artifact, vol. 2, no. 1, 2008, pp. 41 to 45. The journal description is verified; the repository series label "Paper 37" could not be confirmed because the repository blocked our fetches.
  - The paper's reference list gives [35]'s subtitle as "toward a formalization"; Crossref gives "towards a formalization." Do not put the subtitle in quotation marks on the slide.
  - Quotation marks: the panel's line has "good" in inner quotes in the paper, and R4's line has "scientism" in inner double quotes inside Gaver's single quotes. On the slide the R4 line uses single inner marks; in speech, just say the words.
  - Do not say why the critics asked (legitimacy, funding, careers). Gaver himself only says standards "may be a route towards disciplinary legitimacy" (p. 937), and that belongs to section 02.
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: What did the 2011 panel actually say?* A: Its abstract says design research has "fundamental differences in the development of design knowledge as compared to scientific knowledge," and that the panel wants to develop critical discussion of design research in HCI. So even the panel Gaver quotes did not ask design to become a science. The quotations on the slide come from p. 824 of that panel, as Gaver gives them (p. 937).
  - *Q: What is in the 2010 DIS paper by Zimmerman, Stolterman and Forlizzi?* A: Its abstract says they interviewed 12 leading HCI design researchers, examined three historical research through design projects that kept coming up, and critiqued current practice. Their stated aim is "expanding and formalizing" the approach and understanding how theory comes out of it. Gaver quotes it for "protocols, descriptions, and guidelines" and "a proper research methodology" (p. 937).
  - *Q: What is the 2008 Zimmerman and Forlizzi paper?* A: "The Role of Design Artifacts in Design Theory Construction," a short article in the journal Artifact, vol. 2, no. 1, pp. 41 to 45; Gaver lists the Carnegie Mellon repository copy (p. 946). Gaver quotes it for "a level of extensibility and verifiability" and "theory designers can apply in research in practice" (p. 937); the source reads "research and practice," a small misquotation confirmed by the reference check (`../reports/opus-REF-paper-references.md`). Gaver's p. 939 quotations "agreed upon form of practice, evaluation, and outcome" and "extensible, systemic approaches" also come from this 2008 paper, though he cites [34] there.
  - *Q: Wasn't the 2007 paper already a call for standards?* A: Yes. Zimmerman, Forlizzi and Evenson write that "there is no agreed upon standard of what research through design means nor what a high quality contribution should be," and propose their four lenses to fix that (2007, p. 499). So the call starts in 2007, which is why the timeline starts there. Gaver's quotations on p. 937, though, come from 2008, 2010 and 2011.
  - *Q: Is Gaver attacking a straw man, then?* A: He hedges carefully. He has "some sympathy with these concerns" and is uneasy about "tendencies" (p. 937). His worry is that standards "may adopt, or be interpreted in terms of, inappropriate 'scientific' models" (p. 938). So the target is a risk and an interpretation, not a claim the critics made. The 2007 paper even replaces validity with relevance and says there is "no expectation that reproducing the process will produce the same results" (Zimmerman et al. 2007, p. 499).
  - *Q: Does Gaver reject extensibility and verifiability completely?* A: No. Near the end he claims annotated portfolios allow "for the 'extensibility and verifiability' for which some of the HCI design community have called" (p. 945). What he resists is expecting extensible and verifiable theory as the main output; his conclusion says "we should moderate expectations" (p. 945).
  - *Q: Who is reviewer R4, and is it normal to quote a reviewer?* A: The paper does not say who R4 is; the reviewer is anonymous. Gaver thanks R4 in the acknowledgements (p. 946). We use the line only to show how one reviewer read the essay in 2012, not as evidence for the argument.
  - *Q: Were these really two separate camps?* A: Less than it looks. Stolterman co-wrote [35] and supplies Gaver's "ultimate particular" (p. 944). Bardzell and DiSalvo sat on the 2011 panel [9], and Gaver himself cites a CHI 2012 panel, "The humanities and/in HCI," with Bardzell, Bardzell, DiSalvo, Gaver and Sengers (reference [2], p. 946, cited on p. 938 and p. 939). It is one small community arguing with itself.
  - *Q: What does Stolterman 2008 argue, briefly?* A: Gaver uses it for two ideas: design aims at the "ultimate particular," which has "the same dignity and importance as truth in science" (p. 944), and designers "can be prepared-for-action but not guided-in-action by detailed prescriptive procedures" (p. 941). The paper is in the International Journal of Design 2(1), pp. 55 to 65 (p. 946). Section 06 goes deeper.

## Background for the presenter

1. **Frayling 1993.** "Research in Art and Design," Royal College of Art Research Papers vol. 1 no. 1 (p. 946, ref. 11; the RCA catalogue dates it 1993/4). A 2021 RCA conference paper summarises the three modes this way: research into art and design is historical, aesthetic or theoretical research leading to a thesis; research through art and design covers materials research, development work and action research, with studio work plus a report; research for art and design is work where thinking is embodied in the artefact, which Frayling called thorny because it is not verbally communicable. We read Gaver's definition (p. 937) as closest to "through," with the artefact weighted as heavily as in "for."
2. **Zimmerman, Forlizzi and Evenson 2007** (CHI, pp. 493 to 502). Based on interviews with interaction designers and HCI researchers. The model: designers integrate "true" knowledge (behavioural science), "how" knowledge (engineering) and "real" knowledge (anthropology) to make "the right thing," moving the world to a "preferred state" (pp. 493, 497). The four lenses (p. 499 to 500): process (rigour and a rationale for methods, documented so it can be reproduced, with no expectation of the same result), invention (a novel integration, backed by a literature review), relevance (replaces validity; a shift "from what is true" to "what is real"), extensibility (the community can build on the process or the artefacts). OpenAlex counted about 2,300 citations on 2026-10-19 (approximate). It cites Gaver's Drift Table as "a well known example of critical design in HCI" (p. 496), so Gaver's own work was already part of the case for the method.
3. **The three studio artefacts** (from the studio's other publications; none is in this paper). Drift Table (CHI 2004, Gaver, Bowers, Boucher and others): an electronic coffee table showing slowly moving aerial photography, steered by weight placed on its surface. It was a study of ludic activities "motivated by curiosity, exploration, and reflection rather than externally-defined tasks," lived with in volunteers' homes for several weeks. History Tablecloth (DIS 2006): a screen-printed electroluminescent cloth, a grid of lace-like cells that light up under objects as a halo growing over hours. A long-term household study found it prompted reflection on how objects move through a home. Prayer Companion (CHI 2010, Gaver, Blythe, Boucher, Jarvis, Bowers, Wright): a device for a group of cloistered nuns that shows a stream of RSS news and social networking items as possible topics for prayer. After ten months of deployment, the nuns said it had a meaningful, lasting role in their prayer.
4. **Who Gaver is.** PhD in psychology, UC San Diego, 1988, thesis on everyday listening and auditory icons. "Auditory Icons" (Human-Computer Interaction 2(2), 1986, pp. 167 to 177) proposed caricatures of everyday sounds as interface feedback. The SonicFinder (Human-Computer Interaction 4(1), 1989, pp. 67 to 94), "an auditory interface I developed at Apple Computer." "Technology Affordances" (CHI 1991) brought Gibson's ecological psychology to interface design. "Design: Cultural Probes" (Gaver, Dunne and Pacenti, interactions 6(1), 1999, pp. 21 to 29), written at the Royal College of Art: packets of postcards, maps and disposable cameras given to older people in Oslo, the Bijlmer near Amsterdam and Peccioli near Pisa, for a European project on increasing the presence of the elderly in local communities, used to inspire design rather than to collect data. The studio took its present form at Goldsmiths in 2006 and moved to Northumbria University in late 2021. We read the psychology background as one reason the essay is at home in philosophy of science.
5. **Third wave HCI and the grant.** Bødker 2006 (NordiCHI, "When second wave HCI meets third wave challenges"): the second wave centred on work; in the third, use contexts broaden and mix, and technology spreads from the workplace into homes, everyday life and culture. Harrison, Tatar and Sengers, "The Three Paradigms of HCI" (alt.chi, CHI 2007): human factors, then cognitive science, then a third paradigm they call the "phenomenological matrix." The ERC grant (CORDIS 226528, ERC-2008-AdG, host Goldsmiths, 1 April 2009 to 31 December 2014, EU contribution about 2.44 million euro) aimed to "help bring to maturity the emerging paradigm of third-wave HCI" through two case studies, one on environmental awareness in the home and one giving "a behind the scenes view of local neighbourhoods," with 50 to 100 batch-produced prototypes deployed in field trials "several orders of magnitude larger than normal."
6. **A fact that would impress.** The panel Gaver quotes as wanting "actionable metrics" says in its own abstract that design knowledge develops with "fundamental differences" from scientific knowledge. And the 2007 paper that started the call for standards already replaced validity with relevance. We read this as support for Gaver's own hedge: the risk he names lies in how standards "may adopt, or be interpreted in terms of," scientific models (p. 938), not in what the critics said.
