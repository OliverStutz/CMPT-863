# 08 Strengths and limitations

**Purpose:** Give a fair verdict on the essay: five strengths anchored to the page, five limitations argued as ours with Gaver's best reply to each, and one point that cuts against our own reading. **Slides:** 2. **Time:** 2:30. **Speaker:** Oliver. **Previous section:** 07 How the essay argues. **Next section:** 09 Connections.

## Slide 08.1: Strengths

**Time:** 1:00 · **Visual:** five numbered rows, each a short claim on the left and the quotation with its printed page on the right; no figure.

**On the slide**
- Names it: HCI "prone to 'scientism'" (p. 938); R4: "an elephant in the room" (p. 946)
- Hedges: "I have some sympathy with these concerns" (p. 937); whether RtD is a science "depends on the account of science one adopts" (p. 941)
- Constructive: annotated portfolios "as a serious form of theoretical contribution" (p. 945)
- Disagreement as evidence: "It is difficult to call for research to be more scientific if what it means to be scientific is under question" (p. 941)
- Balance: "neither dominates, and neither is subservient" (p. 945)
- **[classmate]**

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** Five strengths, and we think they are real. First, Gaver names scientism on the CHI stage, and his reviewer R4 called it "an elephant in the room." Second, he keeps his hedges. He has "some sympathy" with the critics, and whether design is a science "depends on the account of science one adopts." Third, he does not stop at critique. He proposes annotated portfolios as "a serious form of theoretical contribution." Fourth, he turns disagreement about science into evidence. You cannot ask research to be more scientific while the meaning of scientific is in dispute. Fifth, the balance rule. Theory and artefact sit together, "neither dominates, and neither is subservient." That protects both sides, the maker and the theorist. **[classmate]**
- **Transition:** Strengths like these make the gaps easier to see, so here is what the essay does not do.
- **Watch out:**
  - Do not say Gaver was the first to raise scientism. He cites Sengers, "Must design become 'scientific'?", a DIS 2006 workshop paper, as [29] on p. 941. The strength is that he names it in a CHI paper and builds the essay around it. Gaver himself says the essay addresses R4's elephant only "in part" (p. 946).
  - Do not quote him as saying design is unscientific. The full hedge on p. 941: he "tend[s] to think" design is "distinct from the ideals, and often practice, of science," and the verdict depends on the account of science (pitfall 1).
  - The concession on p. 937 goes further than sympathy: standards "may be a route towards disciplinary legitimacy within HCI." If a classmate says Gaver is simply against standards, this line is the answer.
  - The limit he sets on his own proposal (p. 945): "I am not proposing here that annotated portfolios subsume all other forms of design theory." Use it if someone says he wants to replace theory (pitfall 9).
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: Where exactly does Gaver use the word "scientism," and does he define it?* A: Four times, and he never defines it. On p. 938 he writes "insofar as HCI is prone to 'scientism' in its cultural assumptions," keeping the word in quotation marks. On p. 946 it is R4's word, quoted in the acknowledgements. The nearest thing to a definition is p. 938: standards "may adopt, or be interpreted in terms of, inappropriate 'scientific' models of research and theory for the field."
  - *Q: Isn't a reviewer's comment in the acknowledgements weak evidence for a strength?* A: Yes, as evidence it is weak, and we do not use it as proof that Gaver is right. We use it only to show that the essay's framing was recognised in review and printed at CHI (p. 946). Gaver himself says the essay addresses the elephant only "in part" (p. 946).
  - *Q: Who raised scientism before Gaver?* A: He cites one source himself: Sengers 2006, "Must design become 'scientific'?", a position paper at the DIS 2006 workshop on Exploring Design as a Research Activity ([29], p. 941, listed p. 946). Sengers is also thanked in the acknowledgements and co-authored references [2] and [30] (p. 946). We could not find the text of Sengers 2006 online, so we do not say what it argued.
  - *Q: Aren't all those hedges just a way to make the essay impossible to refute?* A: A fair challenge, and it mirrors Gaver's own Popper test. Our answer: the hedges scope the claim without emptying it. He says outright he is "not presenting Popper's and Lakatos' accounts as particularly representative of Philosophy of Science" (p. 941), and his concrete claims stay checkable, for example the "most of us" list of shared values (p. 942), which a survey of the field could test.
  - *Q: What is an annotated portfolio, in one sentence from the paper?* A: A balance "between descriptions of specific, detailed examples of design practice, and articulations of the issues, values and themes which characterise the relations among the collection" (p. 944). It is not a layout: the Figure 1 caption says portfolios "are not defined by their graphic presentation" (p. 945). Gaver calls them "the converse of Alexander's [1] design patterns" because they keep the particulars instead of abstracting regularities (p. 945).
  - *Q: How can "constructive" be a strength if your next slide calls the proposal a sketch?* A: Both are true, and we keep them on separate slides on purpose. The strength is that he offers a form at all, instead of critique only; the limitation is that he does not demonstrate it in this paper. He points to existing presentations of Rams's work, the Design Museum page and vitsoe.com (p. 944 to 945), and the form was worked out later with John Bowers (footnote 1, p. 944; section 10).
  - *Q: Isn't "disagreement as evidence" just saying science is messy too?* A: Partly, but the move is narrower. If what counts as scientific is itself contested, a call to be "more scientific" has no fixed target (p. 941). His conclusion draws the same line: "continuing controversies about how to characterise science should help undermine assumptions about research that draw on HCI's tendencies towards scientism" (p. 945). He does not claim design is as rigorous as physics.
  - *Q: Doesn't the balance rule clash with "It is the artefacts we create that are the definite facts" (p. 945)?* A: There is a real tension, and a sharp listener may spot it. On the same page he says generalised theories are "limited to inspiration and annotation" (p. 945). We read "neither dominates" as a rule for how a portfolio is presented, and the "definite facts" line as a claim about what has priority in research through design. The paper does not reconcile the two explicitly.
- **Sources:** p. 937 (sympathy, legitimacy), p. 938 ("prone to 'scientism'"), p. 941 (account of science, "difficult to call," [29]), p. 945 ("serious form," "neither dominates," "not proposing ... subsume"), p. 946 (R4).

## Slide 08.2: Limitations, and what the essay does not do

**On the slide**
- **We argue:**
  1. "productive research programmes" is a standard with no judge named (p. 941)
  2. Silent on reviewing and funding; critics asked for "actionable metrics" (p. 937)
  3. Mostly reads calls for standards as calls for convergence: "seemingly behind recent calls" (p. 942)
  4. Sketched, not shown: one figure, of Rams's products for Braun and Vitsoe, not an RtD portfolio (p. 945)

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** Now the limitations. Each is our argument, each has a reply. One: Gaver's test for theory is whether it leads to "productive research programmes." That is a standard, but he never says who judges productivity, or when. Two: the critics' problem lives in reviewing, funding and examining. They asked for "actionable metrics." The essay does not say how a committee decides without shared criteria. Three: Gaver mostly reads the call for standards as a call for one paradigm, "seemingly behind recent calls for formalism and standardisation." Standards are a smaller request than a paradigm. Four: the proposal is sketched. One figure, and it annotates Rams's products for Braun and Vitsoe, not a research through design project. Five: missing voices. Pullin on curating design collections, the closest earlier work to portfolios, and Schön are listed but never cited; Cross is absent. And one point against our own case. Gaver's list of what "most of us" share, contact with the people we design for, wide exploration, craft, making as discovery, already works as criteria. A fair reader could say he supplies standards, just not metrics. He hedges it himself: "Perhaps we do not all agree." **[classmate]**
- **Transition:** The course readings we have already met sharpen each of these points, so let us put Gaver next to them.
- **Watch out:** Gaver's best reply to each, in order.
  1. *Productive undefined.* Reply: Lakatos, quoted by Gaver, gives the test content, programmes that "predict novel facts" (p. 940); Gaver states the stake as "their ability to inspire new designs" (p. 941), and his conclusion says such theories "may nonetheless inspire thriving research programmes" (p. 945), so the test is judged after the fact, in later work. Our point stands only in the narrow form: the essay does not say who counts the inspiration, or how.
  2. *Silent on reviewing.* Reply, Gaver's strongest first: annotated portfolios respect the ultimate particular "while allowing for the 'extensibility and verifiability' for which some of the HCI design community have called" (p. 945), his direct answer to the critics' demand. Our narrow claim: he says portfolios allow it, not how a reviewer would judge it. Then: the essay is about what theory can be, not about committee procedure; Gaver grants that standards may help "clarify expectations" (p. 937) and asks the field to "reflect on the appropriate ways to pursue our research on its own terms" (p. 945). He also proposes a form reviewers could judge. Keep our claim factual: the words reviewing, funding and examining do not appear in the essay; it does not say a committee needs no criteria.
  3. *Standards read as convergence.* The limitation now rests on Gaver's own sentence: the pre-paradigmatic diagnosis is "a tempting conclusion, one seemingly behind recent calls for formalism and standardisation [9, 35]" (p. 942). Reply: he does address standards directly, twice. The invisible-consensus reading says "calls for standardisation, formalisation, overarching theory and the like are misplaced" (p. 942), and the many-worlds reading says "the assumption that shared standards are necessary, possible or desirable" may be "potentially repressive" (p. 943). So soften ours: the strongest reading, Kuhn's, answers the paradigm question, and the standards question is answered by the other two in a sentence each.
  4. *Sketched.* Reply: the caption itself says portfolios "are not defined by their graphic presentation" (p. 945); Gaver points to the fuller form himself, a more detailed treatment "might well take the form of an illustrated essay" (p. 945); the proposal is a stance, offered in an essay, and footnote 1 says it was being developed with John Bowers (p. 944). Section 10 shows it was developed further. Do not say the claim is unsupported, say it is not demonstrated here.
  5. *Missing voices.* Reply: a ten-page essay cannot cite everything, and listing Schön shows awareness. Cross's "designerly ways of knowing" argues that design has its own culture, which supports Gaver, so its absence costs lineage, not argument. Koskinen et al. appeared in late September 2011, around the CHI 2012 submission deadline, so it may have come too late to engage; that is why it is off the slide, as contemporaneous rather than missing. Do not add Höök and Löwgren's "strong concepts" here as an omission: it appeared in TOCHI in October 2012, after CHI 2012, so it is a parallel answer from the same year, not something Gaver ignored.
  - *Against our own case.* Keep the hedges from p. 942: "most of us," "seem to," "Perhaps we do not all agree with all of these assumptions all of the time" (pitfall 5). Gaver's own framing: there "seems to be the outlines of a broad consensus," and he draws the conclusion himself: "we already share many of the attributes of a research paradigm" (p. 942 to 943). So this reading is Gaver's, not a gotcha.
  - *Two more limitations, for questions only.* (a) Many worlds: if every criterion is ontological politics, so is the call to value proliferation. Gaver's reply is already in the text: the claim is "potentially repressive" (p. 943) and the conclusion says norms are "political acts to be approached with care" (p. 945), which asks for care, not exemption. This is the weakest of our points; do not lead with it. (b) Who benefits: an established studio with a recognisable portfolio loses little without shared criteria, while a student who must be examined and published needs legible ones; "consensus can come to look like constraint" (p. 946) reads differently from a PhD seat. A reply in Gaver's spirit, not in the text: his worry is that standards would be "overly restrictive" (p. 937 to 938), and restriction falls hardest on newcomers doing unconventional work. Both readings are defensible; this is a discussion question, not a verdict. State it as our reading, never as Gaver's motive.
  - *Reference list, one light sentence if asked, never on the slide.* In-text [3] points to Collins 1994 in places where Curd and Cover [5] is meant (Newton and the moon, p. 939; the reprints, p. 946), and Carroll and Kellogg (p. 944) is missing from the list; the "c.f. ## Background for the presenter

1. **Schön 1983, The Reflective Practitioner (Basic Books).** Schön looks at five professions: engineering, architecture, management, psychotherapy and town planning. His target is "technical rationality," the idea that professionals apply theory from science to practical problems. Against it he puts knowing-in-action, tacit skill that practitioners cannot fully put into words, and reflection-in-action, rethinking a situation while acting in it and testing the new framing on the spot. Chapter 3, "Design as a reflective conversation with the situation," follows an architecture studio review between a teacher, Quist, and a student, Petra, where the drawing "talks back." We read this as the missing ancestor of Gaver's view that theory should "emerge from situated design practice" (p. 942); Gaver lists the book as [28] but never cites it (p. 946).
2. **Cross 1982, "Designerly ways of knowing" (Design Studies 3(4), pp. 221 to 227).** Building on the Royal College of Art "Design in general education" report, Cross contrasts three cultures (Cross p. 221 to 222): the sciences study the natural world by experiment and value objectivity and "truth"; the humanities study human experience by analogy and criticism and value "justice"; design studies the man-made world by modelling, pattern-formation and synthesis and values practicality, ingenuity, empathy and "appropriateness." His five aspects of designerly knowing (Cross p. 226): designers tackle "ill-defined" problems; their problem-solving is "solution-focused"; their thinking is "constructive"; they use "codes" that turn abstract requirements into concrete objects; and they "read" and "write" in "object languages." The fact that impresses: Cross ends by calling for a Lakatosian research programme for design (Cross p. 226), the same philosopher Gaver leans on in 2012.
3. **Pullin 2010 and Koskinen et al. 2011, two near neighbours.** Pullin's "Curating and creating design collections" (Design and Culture 2(3), pp. 309 to 328) defines a design collection as objects by several designers, "conceived as an entity at the time of creation," set, in the abstract's words, "in the context of collections by Hussein Chalayan, Dunne & Raby, the Royal National Institute for Deaf People, and the Museum of Jurassic Technology"; collections "imply a landscape of alternatives." Koskinen, Zimmerman, Binder, Redström and Wensveen's Design Research Through Practice (Morgan Kaufmann, 2011) maps "constructive design research" onto Lab (can design be studied in a laboratory), Field (following design through society) and Showroom (research meeting design and art). We read Pullin as an earlier version of the portfolio idea and the book as the critics' own fuller account of the field.
4. **Höök and Löwgren 2012, strong concepts (ACM TOCHI 19(3), article 23).** A strong concept is intermediate-level knowledge: generative, carrying a core design idea across use situations and domains; about interactive behaviour, not static appearance; part of an artefact that also speaks of use over time; and above the level of single instances. Their examples are social navigation and seamfulness, assessed as "contestable, defensible, and substantive." Löwgren's 2013 Interactions piece, "Annotated portfolios and other forms of intermediate-level knowledge," then placed Gaver's form beside strong concepts. Section 10 picks this up.
5. **Sengers 2006 and Gaver 2014, before and after.** Sengers 2006 is a DIS 2006 workshop paper that Gaver cites for the claim that what "scientific" means is under question (p. 941, [29]); we could not find its text, so say only what Gaver uses it for. Gaver's 2014 chapter, "Science and Design: The Implications of Different Forms of Accountability" (in Olson and Kellogg, eds., Ways of Knowing in HCI, Springer, pp. 143 to 165), is the nearest he comes to limitation 2: it asks what science and design must each defend: science, "the basis of one's claimed knowledge"; design, "that one's design works" (p. 147). The distinction is older: Koskinen and Krogh 2015 quote Gaver, Hooker and Dunne 2001, "Science is epistemologically accountable," "Design is aesthetically accountable."
6. **"Who benefits" is our argument, not a finding.** We found no verified source on how design papers or PhDs were actually judged around 2012, so present it as a question for the room. The textual anchors are Gaver's worry about self-policing that is "overly restrictive" (p. 937 to 938) and his line that "consensus can come to look like constraint" (p. 946). The paper never mentions reviewing, funding or examining.
