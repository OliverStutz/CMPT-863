**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** Gaver cites none of our course readings, so every link here is ours. Olsen, writing about systems, says three diverse solutions support a claim about the space between them. That is Gaver's portfolio, which "establishes an area." We argue Olsen states what Gaver only sketches: more diverse examples make a stronger general claim. Tangible Bits joins three platforms with one optical metaphor. We think that is the clearest annotated portfolio we read, fifteen years before Gaver named the form. Greenberg and Buxton make Gaver's Popper diagnosis for HCI evaluation: our tests seek confirmation. But their cure is split, existence proofs for the really new, refutation for the mainstream. Gaver drops refutation for design theory. Wobbrock and Kientz and Gaver both say "wary." Gaver is wary of convergence; they are wary of constraint, yet embrace definition. That is the debate in one word. Wobbrock, Morris and Wilson extended an empirical method begun in 2005; a 2020 review covers 216 such studies. By Gaver's fertility test that programme thrives, so we argue fertility rescues design theory without setting it apart from science. Finally, Shin and colleagues tested a design for four hours in a realistic setting, which makes Gaver's "inconceivable" look too strong. Yet nearly every participant guessed the purpose, as I flagged in Week 6. So Gaver is half right. (about 227 words)
- **Transition:** Two of these readings, Wobbrock and Kientz and Shin, already came after the essay; here is what else followed from 2012 on.
- **Watch out:**
  - **Gaver does not cite any of these nine readings** (reference list, p. 946). Never say "Gaver draws on Olsen" or "Gaver responds to Greenberg and Buxton." Every connection is our reading.
  - **Olsen is about UI systems and toolkits, not design.** His "Generality" criterion is for tools: "If one has used the tool to solve three diverse problems then one can argue that the tool solves most of the problems lying in the space between the demonstrated solutions. The greater the diversity and the larger the number of demonstrated solutions, the stronger the claim." (pre-print p. 4, "Generality"). Applying it to portfolios is our analogy. Gaver asserts on p. 945 that portfolios allow "the 'extensibility and verifiability'" the critics asked for, and on p. 944 says comparison shows "the dimensions along which a designer's choices may range," but never says why more or more varied examples make the claim stronger; Olsen's diversity and number of examples is one way to state it. Cite Olsen by section name: the copy is a seven-page pre-print dated March 2007, not the UIST proceedings layout (pp. 251 to 258).
  - **Tangible Bits as an annotated portfolio is our judgment.** The quote is from the "Optical metaphors" passage: "we would like to discuss one user interface metaphor that can be applied seamlessly across the metaDESK, ambientROOM, and transBOARD platforms" (p. 240). Gaver's definition: a balance between "specific, detailed examples of design practice, and articulations of the issues, values and themes which characterise the relations among the collection" (p. 944). If a classmate says the paper was not written as research through design, agree: the point is that the form already existed in HCI, which supports the proposal and weakens any claim that it is new.
  - **Greenberg and Buxton's cure:** "For really novel innovations, existence proofs are likely appropriate. For mainstream systems or modest variations of established ideas, we should likely favor risky hypothesis testing." (p. 118). Their diagnosis: "the existence proof as used in HCI is confirmative hypothesis testing, for the evaluator is seeking confirmatory evidence" (p. 113). Gaver's: "practice is usually intended to confirm theories, not falsify them" (p. 940), and confirmations are "testimony to the fertility of the overarching theory" (p. 941). They are about evaluating systems, Gaver about design theory; say "same diagnosis," not "same argument."
  - **The two "wary" sentences.** Wobbrock and Kientz, Conclusion: "while we should be wary of constraining our imaginations, we should embrace giving definition to the knowledge we produce." (p. 44). Gaver, abstract: "the design research community should be wary of impulses towards convergence and standardisation" (p. 937). Do not say Wobbrock and Kientz reply to Gaver; they do not cite him in the passage. They also define theory by Popper, "Theories should be testable and falsifiable" (p. 41), which is alternate K1 if a classmate asks whether Gaver's worry was real.
  - **The 216 figure is a review count, not a descendant count.** Villarreal-Narvaez, Vanderdonckt, Vatavu and Wobbrock, "A Systematic Review of Gesture Elicitation Studies: What Can We Learn from 216 Studies?", DIS 2020, p. 855. Say "a review of 216 elicitation studies," never "216 studies built on this paper." Wobbrock is a co-author of both. Gaver's test is Lakatos's: programmes that "predict novel facts" (p. 940) and lead "to productive research programmes" (p. 941). The fair reply from Gaver's side: fertility was never meant to separate design from science, only to rescue design from Popper. Our point is narrower: it shows a programme is alive, not which kind of knowledge produced it. The elicitation method dates from Wobbrock et al. 2005 (DIS 2020 review, p. 855); the 2009 paper adopted its agreement process "from prior work [33]" (p. 1087). Credit the programme, not this paper alone.
  - **Shin et al. half-supports Gaver.** "We conducted the second study in a real-life setting for ecological validity and observed posture correction for four hours." (p. 5); paired t-tests, "t= 3.54, p= 0.002" (p. 7); but "all of the participants guessed that the monitor was trying to correct their posture, except P2" (p. 7). Gaver's sentence is about tests that are "simultaneously unambiguous and ecologically valid" (p. 940); this one was realistic but not fully blind. Gaver's own example is ambiguity, a claim about meaning; Shin et al. test behaviour. Say "half right," not "refuted." Oliver's Week 6 commentary flagged the confound: "Such an extensive setup might have given the people a clear hint that this is about ergonomics."
  - **Section 02 consistency.** Section 02's Watch out quotes Greenberg and Buxton, p. 111, and Olsen's abstract. Those are different sentences from the ones here; both sets are verified and neither contradicts the other.
  - **[classmate]** slot: if a classmate's commentary linked Gaver to Greenberg and Buxton, Olsen or Tangible Bits, credit it on that row.
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: Gaver cites none of these, and two came after him. Isn't the slide anachronistic?* A: The links are ours, and the slide says so; we use the readings as tests, not sources. But the readings do talk to each other. Greenberg and Buxton cite Gaver's studio: "Gaver et. al. are developing methods that probe cultural reactions and technology uptake by niche cultures [11]" (p. 119), the 1999 cultural probes paper. Wobbrock and Kientz cite Greenberg and Buxton and Olsen (endnotes 4 and 5, p. 44), and Olsen thanks Saul Greenberg (pre-print p. 7). Gaver's studio's methods were already cited in this debate by 2008.
  - *Q: Olsen writes about toolkits. Isn't his generality criterion a stretch for portfolios?* A: It is an analogy, and we say so. Olsen's claim needs populations with unsolved problems; a portfolio solves no stated problem. What carries over is the shape: diverse examples support a claim about the space between them. Olsen also says one example is not enough: with a single problem, "What has been created is not a design tool but an application" (pre-print p. 3). We read that as Gaver's move from one artefact, a point, to a portfolio, an area (p. 944).
  - *Q: Is Olsen on Gaver's side about science?* A: No. "User interface technology, like any other science, moves forward based on the ability to evaluate new improvements" (pre-print p. 6). His target is usability tests as the yardstick, not science. His criteria "are not novel but recently have been out of favor" (pre-print p. 7). He offers standards without controlled experiments, a third position between Gaver and the critics.
  - *Q: Tangible Bits is a vision paper, not research through design. And does the optical metaphor really join all three platforms?* A: Agreed on the first; our claim is only that the form existed in HCI by 1997. On the second, the authors concede: "On the transBOARD, the optical metaphor is somewhat weaker due to the limited computer display facilities" (p. 240). We think that helps. Gaver says annotations articulate "the ideas and issues that join and differentiate" the pieces (p. 945). Wobbrock and Kientz list the paper as their "Envisionment" example of an artifact contribution (p. 41).
  - *Q: Greenberg and Buxton want risky hypothesis testing. Aren't they on the other side?* A: Split. Risky tests for "mainstream systems or modest variations"; for the really new, existence proofs and other ways to validate: "a design rationale, a vision of what could be, expected scenarios of use, reflections, case studies, participatory critique" (p. 118). They add: "This is not to say that we should accept hand-waving and slick demos as an alternative" (p. 118). Gaver has no such split for design theory.
  - *Q: Do Wobbrock and Kientz answer Gaver? Isn't the shared word "wary" a coincidence?* A: They do not cite him (endnotes, p. 44), and the word alone would be a coincidence. The real link is their definition of theory: "Theories should be testable and falsifiable," and "a theory that is so broad it can account for just about anything probably does not contain any true descriptive power" (p. 41). That is the Popper view Gaver says makes design theory look unscientific (p. 940): opposite sides of one question.
  - *Q: Where would an annotated portfolio sit among the CHI contribution types?* A: The paper does not say; our reading is "artifact," where "New knowledge is embedded in and manifested by artifacts and the supporting materials that describe them" (p. 40), with annotations as the supporting materials. Gaver's essay would be "Essay/Argument": at CHI 2016, 88 submissions, 19 accepted, 21.6 percent against 23.6 overall; Theory had 142 and 25, 17.6 percent (p. 44, Figure 2).
  - *Q: Gaver never says fertility separates design from science. Isn't the Wobbrock, Morris and Wilson row a straw man?* A: Fair; the Watch out item concedes he uses Lakatos only to rescue design theory from Popper (p. 941). Our point is narrower. The elicitation programme also grows mostly by confirmations: identification studies, which could validate a gesture set, are "rarely applied," and "legacy bias" is a known problem (2020 review, p. 863). An empirical line passes the fertility test just as Gaver describes, so the test does not pick out design.
  - *Q: Does "216 studies" mean 216 papers built on the 2009 paper?* A: No. It is what a 2020 systematic review found: 216 studies, 5,458 participants, 148,340 gestures (p. 855). One detail shows the 2009 paper's pull: the most common sample size, 20, used by 36 percent of studies, is "likely explained by the fact that Wobbrock et al. [123] employed 20 participants" (p. 859). We read that as Kuhn's paradigm in its "exemplar" sense (Gaver p. 941).
  - *Q: Doesn't Shin et al. simply refute Gaver's "inconceivable"?* A: Not quite. Realistic, yes: four hours at a desk. Unambiguous, no. Actuation always came on day 2, by design, to avoid learning effects (p. 5), so day and treatment are confounded, and 11 of 12 participants guessed the purpose (p. 7). Gaver's example is a claim about meaning (p. 940); Shin et al. measure behaviour. Hence "half right."
  - *Q: You blame the setup for the guessing. Isn't that unfair?* A: Partly. The instructions themselves pointed to health: "The monitor will move to support your health while working" (p. 6), although the authors call this a "neutral" explanation. Oliver's Week 6 commentary blamed the fitting procedure. Both point the same way, so the study was not blind; say "the instructions and the setup."
  - *Q: Which of the six links is weakest?* A: Our honest answer: Wobbrock and Kientz, if it rests on "wary" alone; it holds only with their Popper definition of theory (p. 41). Strongest: Greenberg and Buxton, where the diagnosis matches closely (p. 113 against Gaver p. 940), and Tangible Bits, because the room has read it.

## Background for the presenter

**Olsen 2007, "Evaluating User Interface Systems Research."** The argument: UI systems research has stalled, partly for lack of "articulate criteria for evaluating systems architectures" (pre-print p. 1). Usability tests assume walk-up-and-use, a standardised task and a one- or two-hour test, and toolkit research rarely meets any of these ("The usability trap," pp. 2 to 3). His full list of claims, each with its own evidence: importance; problem not previously solved; generality; reduced solution viscosity (flexibility, expressive leverage, expressive match); empowering new design participants; addressing a new problem; power in combination (inductive combination, simplifying interconnection, ease of combination); and "Can it scale up?" (pp. 3 to 6). Just before the quoted sentence: "The best proof of a generality claim is the diversity of the Xi for which a solution is demonstrated" (pp. 3 to 4). Probes: would a portfolio pass "importance," which comes "Before all other claims" (p. 3)? Who is the "population" for a design portfolio? Best objection: Olsen's generality is one tool used many times; Gaver's portfolio is many artefacts. A line that impresses: "if a tool requires careful statistical analysis to prove its value, the innovation is frequently not important enough to make a difference" (p. 3).

**Ishii and Ullmer 1997, "Tangible Bits."** The argument: GUIs show everything as "painted bits" on screens (p. 240); the paper couples bits with graspable objects in the foreground and ambient media in the background, through interactive surfaces, coupling of bits and atoms, and ambient media (p. 235). The platforms: the metaDESK, a back-projected desk with an arm-mounted "activeLENS," a transparent "passiveLENS" and "phicons," shown with the Tangible Geospace map of MIT (pp. 237 to 238); the ambientROOM, a 6 by 8 foot Steelcase Personal Harbor with MIDI-controllable light, sound, airflow and water, where web hits play as rain or ceiling ripples (pp. 238 to 239); the transBOARD, a networked whiteboard on a SoftBoard whose barcode-tagged "hyperCARDs" store strokes (pp. 239 to 240). Just before the quotation: "we realized that metaphors which bridge physical and digital worlds are particularly interesting" (p. 240). Probes: is a portfolio with one weak member still a portfolio? Is "optics" an annotation or a design rule? Best objection: the metaphor came after the artefacts ("We first implicitly invoked this metaphor," p. 240), so it may be a rationalisation. We read that as Gaver's point: annotation follows the artefacts.

**Greenberg and Buxton 2008, "Usability Evaluation Considered Harmful (Some of the Time)."** Main claims (p. 112): scientific methods do not necessarily make science; early evaluation kills promising ideas and narrows the search to one; usability tests cannot predict cultural adoption. Evidence: Barkhuus and Rode's count, about 70 percent of CHI evaluations quantitative and 25 percent qualitative (p. 113), and visions no test could have judged: Marconi, the car, Memex, Sketchpad, NLS (pp. 116 to 117). Remedies (p. 118): usability evaluation as one method among many; ask whether a test would mean anything; drop the blanket demand; risky tests for the mainstream; learn from the studio crit. Alternatives: design rationale, a vision of what could be, scenarios of use, reflections, case studies, participatory critique (p. 118), and in the conclusion design critiques, design alternatives and cultural probes (p. 119). Probes: is the crit a standard in the sense of [9]? Would Gaver accept their split cure? Best objection: they evaluate systems, not theory, and footnote 2 says "It would be just as inappropriate to drop usability testing altogether" (p. 119).

**Wobbrock and Kientz 2016, "Research Contributions in Human-Computer Interaction."** The argument: HCI knowledge comes in a few contribution types, each with its own way of being judged; the types come from Wobbrock's 2012 whitepaper and fed CHI 2016's keywords (pp. 40, 43). The seven: empirical (observation and data); artifact ("generative design-driven activities (invention)"); methodological (how we research or design); theoretical (judged by "novelty, soundness, and power to describe, predict, and explain"); dataset (a shared corpus); survey (synthesis of a mature topic); opinion (essays that "seek to change the minds of readers through persuasion") (pp. 40 to 42). CHI 2016 split empirical in two. Shares of 2,316 submissions: empirical study of system use 44.0 percent, of people 28.4, artifact or system 24.2, method 19.2, theory 6.1, essay or argument 3.8, survey 2.4, dataset 1.3; more than one type could be ticked (p. 44, Figure 2). Probes: is Gaver's essay theory or opinion on their map? Does "Theory validation is almost always achieved through empirical work" (p. 41) leave room for annotation? Best objection: a map is not a demand for convergence, and the level of research activity "should not be confused with the value placed on particular contribution types" (p. 44).

**Wobbrock, Morris and Wilson 2009, and the 2020 review.** Method: 20 non-technical participants (9 female, mean age 43.2) saw the effects of 27 commands on a Microsoft Surface and performed a gesture to cause each, one- and two-handed, thinking aloud (p. 1085). The 1,080 gestures were classified in a four-part taxonomy (p. 1086) and grouped to score agreement. The largest groups formed a conflict-free set covering 57.0 percent of proposals (p. 1087), 48 gestures in all (p. 1088). Each author's own gestures matched only 43.5 percent of the user set (p. 1090). The review: 216 studies, 5,458 participants, 148,340 gestures (p. 855), 10,752 of them agreed upon (p. 864); studies still rising through 2019, "the peak of GES [gesture elicitation studies] has not been reached yet" (p. 858). Probes: is a consensus gesture set a theory, a method or an artefact? Does legacy bias (p. 863) make consensus conservative, Gaver's "consensus can come to look like constraint" (p. 946)? Best objection: Wobbrock co-authored the review, so its upbeat framing is not independent.

**Shin et al. 2019, "Slow Robots for Unobtrusive Posture Correction."** The argument: a robot-arm monitor moved below the perception threshold can correct posture without interrupting work (contributions, p. 2). Study 1: 6 students, 32 conditions (8 motions, 4 speeds) during reading, key press on noticing, about 1.5 hours; at 0.5 mm/s noticing took 64.22 seconds on average (p. 3); the design then used 1.0 mm/s (p. 4). Study 2: 12 students and office workers (mean age 24.55), four hours on two consecutive days, actuation only on day 2, morning and afternoon groups; measures were posture from three cameras, motion logs, a modified NASA-TLX, an annoyance scale and an exit interview (p. 5). Results: unbalanced time fell (t = 3.54, p = 0.002), corrections rose about 66 percent, performance and focus unchanged (p. 7). Probes: would a counterbalanced, blind version still be ecologically valid? Is "slow robots" a "designing for X" claim (Gaver p. 940)? Best objection: Gaver means tests of design theory, and Shin et al. test a device. We read their concept claim (p. 8) as theory of Gaver's kind, which keeps the row fair.
