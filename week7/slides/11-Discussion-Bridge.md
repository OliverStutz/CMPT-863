## Slide 11.1: Our reading of the paper

**Time:** 0:45 · **Visual:** one sentence large, three supports beneath, each with its page

**On the slide**
- **Gaver wins the argument against scientism. He concedes more to his critics than his conclusion admits, and he never says how a field without shared criteria decides what to publish, fund and examine.**
- He accepts "clearly articulating ... the standards one might want to advocate" (p. 941) and a practice "most of us ... seem to share" (p. 942). What he refuses is standardisation and convergence (p. 937, p. 942).
- "Productive research programmes" (p. 941) is a criterion with no judge named.
- The annotated portfolio is a form, not a criterion: one designer, one sketch, "not defined by their graphic presentation" (p. 945).

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** One sentence from us. Gaver wins the argument against scientism, and concedes more to his critics than his conclusion admits. On page 941 he accepts articulating standards; on page 942, that most of us seem to share a practice. What he refuses is standardisation and convergence. "Productive research programmes" is his own criterion, with no judge named. The annotated portfolio is a form, not a criterion: one designer, one sketch, by his own caption not defined by its presentation. And the essay never says how a field without shared criteria reviews, funds or examines. (about 94 words)
- **Transition:** So here is what we want to argue about.
- **Watch out:**
  - Say "concedes more than his conclusion admits," not "contradicts himself." The p. 941 sentence reads: "what this discussion suggests is the desirability of clearly articulating the methodological and conceptual features of current research through design practice, as well as the standards one might want to advocate for them." Gaver's conclusion keeps the distinction: he opposes "convergence and standardisation" (p. 937) and says his essay explores issues that "complicate calls for disciplinary consolidation" (p. 945), not articulation. Our claim is that the distance between "articulating standards" and the critics' "protocols, descriptions, and guidelines" (p. 937) is smaller than the essay's tone suggests. If challenged, concede that the tone and the position are different things, and keep the position.
  - "Wins against scientism" means: the argument that "it is difficult to call for research to be more scientific if what it means to be scientific is under question" (p. 941) is sound, and reviewer R4 read the essay that way (p. 946). It does not mean HCI is scientistic; Gaver's own wording is "insofar as HCI is prone to 'scientism'" (p. 938).
  - Standardisation and convergence are what he refuses, in his words: "wary of impulses towards convergence and standardisation" (p. 937) and "calls for standardisation, formalisation, overarching theory and the like are misplaced" (p. 942). "Actionable metrics" is the critics' phrase (p. 937); the essay never answers it, which is our third point.
  - A classmate may quote p. 943 against us: "even the assumption that shared standards are necessary, possible or desirable" is among the "potentially repressive acts of ontological politics." Concede it. Page 941 accepts articulated standards and page 943 doubts shared ones; that gap is what "concedes more than his conclusion admits" names, and the conclusion settles on "political acts to be approached with care" (p. 945).
  - "No judge named": Gaver's test is that theory "can lead to productive research programmes" (p. 941), and he reports Lakatos's criterion as "the relative productivity of competing programmes" (p. 941). So the criterion is named; what is missing is who applies it in design, over what span, and against what rival. Keep the point that narrow (section 04 says the same). If someone answers "the community decides, over time," agree that this is the Lakatosian answer and ask how a reviewer applies it to one paper this year.
  - "One designer, one sketch": the only portfolio in the essay is Rams's, a product designer, in a figure the paper labels a "Sketch design" (p. 945). The principles in the centre are Rams's (p. 944); the seven product notes speak of Rams in the third person, so they are the sketch's words, not his (section 06). Gaver does say "a collection of designs by the same or associated designers" (p. 944), so a portfolio can be a studio's. Our point: the essay shows an annotator it does not name annotating a designer with the designer's own principles, and never says who else may annotate or how two annotations would be compared.
  - "Form, not a criterion": the caption's "not defined by their graphic presentation" (p. 945) and "The notion of annotated portfolios is not a formal one" (p. 944). Gaver also claims portfolios allow "the 'extensibility and verifiability'" (p. 945); our reply is that he asserts this and does not show how. Support from section 10: Löwgren 2013 filed annotated portfolios as one form of intermediate-level knowledge among others, and Gaver's own 2014 chapter says portfolios "may capture best what we can know through design" (Ways of Knowing in HCI, p. 161), a claim about capture, not about judging.
  - "Reviews, funds or examines" is our framing. The essay mentions none of the three. The nearest text is the critics' "actionable metrics for bringing rigor in critique of design research" (p. 937), which is about reviewing.
  - The limitation that cuts against us (section 08): the "most of us" list on p. 942 is a de facto standard, so a reader may say Gaver does supply criteria, just not metrics. Our thesis already grants that; the gap we name is the step from shared values to a decision about one paper.
- **If asked:**
  - *Q (support 1): Read p. 941 in context. The "articulating standards" sentence opens with "Instead" and follows the point that "it is difficult to call for research to be more scientific." It is Gaver's alternative to the science label, not a concession to the critics.* A: Agreed on the context; in the left column it follows "(see [29])" directly. But look at what the alternative is: articulating features and "the standards one might want to advocate for them" (p. 941). The critics asked for "protocols, descriptions, and guidelines" (p. 937). Description is common ground. We do not say he changed sides; we say the gap is narrower than his tone.
  - *Q (support 1): "Some sympathy" (p. 937) is politeness. He says right after that he is "uneasy." Isn't concession too strong?* A: We do not lean on the sympathy line. We lean on three positive statements: standards "may be a route towards disciplinary legitimacy within HCI, helping clarify expectations" (p. 937), the p. 941 sentence, and his close, where portfolios "may provide a way to further our ambitions to produce relevant and rigorous theoretical work" (p. 946). The critics' phrase was "relevant and rigorous theory" (p. 937, quoting [35]). We read the echo as deliberate common ground, and say "we read," since he does not flag it.
  - *Q (support 1): The "most of us" list is a description of what people do. Describing is not standard-setting.* A: Fair as far as it goes. But Gaver draws the normative step himself: "we already share many of the attributes of a research paradigm" (p. 942 to 943). And by his own taxonomy, meta-theory, which he says this essay is (p. 939), "often" suggests "normative standards for how research through design should be conducted" (p. 938). Keep his hedges: "seem to share," "Perhaps we do not all agree" (p. 942).
  - *Q (support 2): Lakatos does not name a judge either. Programmes are judged by the community, over time. Why demand more of Gaver than of philosophy of science?* A: That is the honest Lakatosian answer, and we accept it for science. Lakatos's test does have content: a programme is progressive if it predicts novel facts and some are corroborated, and degenerating if not (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy). Our point is about HCI's decisions, which happen one paper at a time, this year. Gaver's own phrasing is retrospective: theory that "can lead to productive research programmes" (p. 941).
  - *Q (support 2): Gaver is writing about what theory can be. He was not asked for a reviewing manual.* A: He raises assessment himself. Summarising the reform calls, he writes: "Greater integration would make assessment of new contributions fairer and more rigorous" (p. 941). That claim is stated and never answered. The words reviewing, funding and examining do not appear in the essay; that much is fact. That the gap matters is our argument. His 2014 chapter later names design's test, "does it work?" (Ways of Knowing in HCI, p. 147), which is still not a procedure.
  - *Q (support 2): The critics had no judge either. What did they actually propose?* A: They did propose criteria. Zimmerman, Forlizzi and Evenson 2007, Gaver's [34], offered "four lenses for evaluating an interaction design research contribution: process, invention, relevance, and extensibility" (CHI 2007, p. 499). Gaver cites [34] for making "the 'right thing'" (p. 940), but the essay does not discuss the four lenses. So one side of the debate had a reviewer's checklist and the essay does not engage it.
  - *Q (support 3): Ten pages, and he points to the fuller form: an "illustrated essay" (p. 945). Isn't "one sketch" unfair?* A: Partly. The length limit is real, and Gaver and Bowers published "Annotated portfolios" in Interactions in July 2012, two months after CHI (section 10). Our claim is about this paper only: the form is proposed and illustrated, not demonstrated on research through design work. The sketch's seven items are Rams products, one a prototype television "never produced," with flickr Creative Commons photographs (p. 945).
  - *Q (support 3): A form can carry a criterion. "Neither dominates, and neither is subservient" (p. 945) is a test a reviewer could apply.* A: Good point, and we grant it. It is a test of the portfolio's balance: "a balance is achieved between descriptions of specific, detailed examples ... and articulations of the issues, values and themes" (p. 944). It does not tell a reviewer whether the designs, or the ideas, are any good. Gaver adds that presentation "may vary depending on material, purpose and audience" (p. 944), so even the form is open.
  - *Q (support 3): He says portfolios allow "the 'extensibility and verifiability'" the critics asked for (p. 945). So he does answer them.* A: He asserts it in one clause. The essay does not show what would be extended, or how a claim in a portfolio would be verified. His 2014 chapter says portfolios "may capture best what we can know through design" (Ways of Knowing in HCI, p. 161; section 10), which is about capturing knowledge, not judging it.

## Background for the presenter

- **The course brief's five discussion principles.** The brief (`Instructions Week 7.md`) says the second half is "a conversation, not an additional lecture," and that strong discussions often: (1) move between "high-level questions" about the paper's significance and "specific questions" about its methods, design or evidence; (2) ask questions "that allow for multiple defensible perspectives rather than simple factual answers"; (3) use "brief pair or small-group discussions"; (4) "follow interesting ideas that emerge rather than rigidly working through a list of prepared questions"; (5) "encourage participation across the class rather than allowing a few students to dominate." The presenter's role is to "facilitate": keep it moving, probe, follow up, redirect.
- **How the three steps map onto them.** We read the plan this way. Step 1, hands in three, is a specific question with three defensible answers (principles 2 and 5: everyone votes). Step 2, pairs with role cards, is principle 3 directly, and the two roles on each card give principle 2. Step 3, anchors then a round, covers principle 1 (each anchor pairs a page-level probe with a field-level one), principle 4 (pick the anchors from what the room raised, not in list order) and principle 5 (one sentence from everyone). The If asked answers above are for the presenter, not for reading out: the brief's "not an additional lecture" means two sentences, then a question back.
- **How to credit a commentary.** The brief asks presenters to use the commentaries and to credit "the students who raised them," and commentary integration carries 4 of the 18 points (Part 1.1). On the slide: the student's own words, verbatim, with the name. In the room: read one sentence aloud, name the author, and ask them to take the first position on the tension it feeds ("You wrote this; where do you stand now?"). Never paraphrase a commentary into a stronger claim. If the student is absent, read it and ask the room to answer it. Note who spoke: the presenter grades commentaries and participation after class (course brief).
- **The critics' criteria, in their own words.** Zimmerman, Forlizzi and Evenson 2007 (Gaver's [34]) proposed four lenses for evaluating research through design: process (rigour and rationale of methods, documented so the process can be reproduced), invention ("a novel integration of various subject matters"), relevance ("Instead of validity, the benchmark for interaction design research should be relevance"), and extensibility (the community can build on the process or the artefacts) (CHI 2007, p. 499 to 500). Useful when someone says the critics only wanted metrics.
- **Lakatos's vocabulary for "who counts fertility."** A programme is progressive when each new theory has excess empirical content over its predecessor and some of that content is corroborated, and degenerating if not (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Imre Lakatos," section 2.2). Gaver uses "productive" and "fertility" (p. 941) but not "degenerating."
