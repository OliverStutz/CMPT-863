# 07 How the essay argues

**Purpose:** Answer the course question "how was the research conducted" for a paper with no study, and show the class what counts as evidence in an essay of this kind. **Slides:** 1. **Time:** 1:30. **Speaker:** Oliver. **Previous section:** 06 Annotation. **Next section:** 08 Strengths and limitations.

## Slide 07.1: How the essay argues: method, evidence, reflexivity

**Time:** 1:30 · **Visual:** three columns headed Method, Evidence, Reflexivity, each item with its printed page; one "we argue" line across the bottom. No figure.

**On the slide**
- **Method**
  - Popper versus Lakatos, chosen "to complicate notions of what makes theories 'scientific'" (p. 938)
  - One observation, three readings (p. 941 to 943)
  - One sketched example, Rams, in one figure (p. 944 to 945)
- **Evidence**
  - The critics, quoted (p. 937)
  - Six design theories; two are Gaver's own [13, 14] (p. 938 to 941)
  - Ambiguity claim: test reasoned, not run (p. 940)
- **Reflexivity**
  - "a contribution to meta-theory" (p. 939)
  - Argues for annotated examples; contains one
  - Reviewer R4: "an elephant in the room" (p. 946)
- **We argue: judge it as argument, not as a study.**

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** How was this research conducted? There is no study, so the real question is what counts as evidence. Gaver's method is argument by contrast. He picks Popper and Lakatos, chosen, he says, "to complicate notions of what makes theories 'scientific'." He reads one observation, that the field does not converge, three ways. And he closes on one sketched example, Rams. His evidence is quotation. The critics speak in their own words. His examples of design theory are six works, two of them his own. The ambiguity claim gets a thought experiment: he reasons through what a fair test would need and concludes it cannot be built. Then reflexivity. Gaver calls the essay "a contribution to meta-theory." We note an irony: an essay arguing that theory should annotate examples contains one example. And the studio's other publications, such as the Drift Table, already look like the portfolio he recommends. None of this is unusual for a CHI essay in a critical perspectives session. So we argue: judge it as argument. Whose premises? Does each move follow? What would change his mind? **[classmate]**
- **Transition:** Judged as argument, then, what does the essay do well, and where does it fall short?
- **Watch out:**
  - "Where is the evidence?" is a fair question, not a gotcha. The honest answer: evidence here is the accuracy of the quotations, the fairness to the opponents, and whether each step follows. Gaver also shows his own quality signals: the concession, "I have some sympathy with these concerns" (p. 937); the hedges, that Popper and Lakatos are not "particularly representative of Philosophy of Science" and that the verdict "depends on the account of science one adopts" (p. 941); and the limit, "I am not proposing here that annotated portfolios subsume all other forms of design theory" (p. 945).
  - Do not say the examples all come from Gaver's friends. Two are his own [13, 14]; Sengers [30] is thanked in the acknowledgements (p. 946); but "Designing for the Self" [36] is by John Zimmerman, a co-author of the very papers Gaver answers [33, 34, 35]. So the sample also draws on the other side. Say "partly from his own work," nothing stronger.
  - "Manifesto": the essay does not call itself one. It calls itself "a contribution to meta-theory" and says such meta-theoretical treatments "can be considered as wide-ranging manifestos" (p. 939). That it is a manifesto by its own taxonomy is our inference; say "we argue."
  - The ambiguity claim is Gaver's own earlier paper, Gaver, Beaver and Benford 2003 [14]. He grants it is "in principle a falsifiable statement" (p. 940), then argues a fair comparison of "comparable ambiguous and unambiguous systems" cannot be built. He runs no test; he argues one is impossible.
  - The Drift Table is not mentioned in this paper. It comes from the studio's other publications (CHI 2004). Do not imply a motive in the "irony" point; it is an observation.
  - Reviewer R4 shows the essay went through review and that the scientism reading was recognised there. It does not show the reviewers agreed with all of it.
  - **[classmate]** slot: credit any commentary that asked "where is the evidence" or treated the essay as opinion.
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: What kind of paper is this, and how would CHI have reviewed it?* A: It calls itself an essay three times in the opening pages (p. 937, p. 938). CHI 2012 sorted papers and notes into eight subcommittees, and the Design subcommittee's remit named "critique, and theory" among its research issues. The paper does not say which subcommittee handled it. The acknowledgements thank "reviewers" in the plural and single out "the anonymous R4" (p. 946), so it was peer reviewed like any full paper.
  - *Q: What else was in the Critical Perspectives on Design session?* A: Four papers, on 8 May 2012, chaired by Peter Wright of Newcastle University. Gaver's essay; Brynjarsdottir, Håkansson, Pierce, Baumer, DiSalvo and Sengers, "Sustainably Unpersuaded," a critique of persuasive technology; Pierce, "Undesigning Technology"; and Kaptelinin and Nardi, "Affordances in HCI: Toward a Mediated Action Perspective." We read it as a session of conceptual and critical papers, none of them a user study. So the essay form was the norm in that room, not the exception.
  - *Q: Why Popper and Lakatos? Why not an account from the humanities?* A: Gaver grants that accounts from the humanities "can also shed valuable light on design [2]" (p. 939). He gives two reasons for using philosophy of science: it offers "sophisticated and well-developed discussions of the nature of theory," and it deals with how to tell scientific fields from non-scientific ones (p. 939). And the pair was chosen as a contrast, "to complicate notions of what makes theories 'scientific'" (p. 938). Reference [2] is a CHI 2012 panel on the humanities and HCI on which Gaver himself sat (p. 946).
  - *Q: Isn't picking two philosophers cherry-picking?* A: Gaver says so himself: he is "not presenting Popper's and Lakatos' accounts as particularly representative of Philosophy of Science" (p. 941). The two are a foil, not a survey. Kuhn also appears, on puzzle-solving (p. 939) and as the frame for the convergence section (p. 941 to 942). The fair test, which we argue: does the conclusion need these two in particular, or would any account that values fertility over refutation do the same work?
  - *Q: What evidence would change Gaver's mind?* A: The paper does not say directly. The nearest text is the ambiguity claim: he grants it is "in principle a falsifiable statement" and says the problem is building ambiguous and unambiguous systems that are truly "comparable" (p. 940). So a convincing, realistic comparison of that kind would challenge his argument that design theory cannot be tested. He also concedes that "greater consensus may be emerging in research through design than is sometimes acknowledged" (p. 945).
  - *Q: He is a party to this debate. Isn't relying on his own work a conflict of interest?* A: He is open about his side: "others (including myself) believe that theory should be allowed to emerge from situated design practice" (p. 942). Taking a side is normal in an essay. The fair check is whether he states the other side accurately, and he lets the critics speak in their own words (p. 937). Two of six example theories are his own [13, 14], one is by Zimmerman [36], from the other side.
  - *Q: Did Gaver ever publish the annotated portfolio that this essay lacks?* A: Not in this paper, but in the same year. Gaver and Bowers, "Annotated Portfolios," appeared in interactions 19(4), 2012, pp. 40 to 49. Bowers, "The Logic of Annotated Portfolios," at DIS 2012, pp. 68 to 77, annotates nine pieces from the Interaction Research Studio and draws on Feyerabend. Footnote 1 of this essay says many of its ideas about annotated portfolios "have been developed in collaboration with John Bowers" (p. 944). So the one-example irony is real for this text, and answered elsewhere.
  - *Q: Did he come back to the science question later?* A: Yes. Gaver, "Science and Design: The Implications of Different Forms of Accountability," in Olson and Kellogg, eds., Ways of Knowing in HCI, Springer, 2014, pp. 143 to 165. There he writes that "Science is defined by epistemological accountability," having to defend the basis of its claimed knowledge, while design "works with aesthetic accountability," having to show "that one's design works" (p. 147 of the chapter). He cites the 2012 essay in a footnote (p. 145) and concludes that "Design is not a poor cousin of science" (p. 163). Koskinen and Krogh 2015 trace the distinction back to Gaver, Hooker and Dunne 2001.
  - *Q: How influential has the essay been?* A: Widely. On 19 October 2026 Crossref counted about 880 citing works, OpenAlex about 1,140 and Semantic Scholar about 1,220, so "about 900 to 1,200 depending on the index." Treat all three as approximate; each index counts differently. Google Scholar was not checked.

## Background for the presenter

- **The venue.** CHI 2012 ran in Austin, Texas, 5 to 10 May 2012 (p. 937 header). The call expected "over 1,800 Papers and Notes" and split review into eight subcommittees. The Design subcommittee covered "critique, and theory" among other research issues; its chairs were Carl DiSalvo, Jodi Forlizzi, Ron Wakkary and Steve Harrison, and Jeff and Shaowen Bardzell were among its associate chairs. Forlizzi is an author of five of the works Gaver answers or discusses [9, 10, 33, 34, 35] (p. 946). The paper does not say which subcommittee reviewed it, so do not draw any conclusion from this; it only shows how small and interlinked the community was.
- **The session.** "Critical Perspectives on Design," 8 May 2012, 09:30, room 16AB, chair Peter Wright (Newcastle University). The other three papers: Brynjarsdottir et al., "Sustainably Unpersuaded: How Persuasion Narrows our Vision of Sustainability," which analyses persuasive technology as a modernist approach to social problems; Pierce, "Undesigning Technology: Considering the Negation of Design by Design," pp. 957 to 966, a framework for intentionally negating technology; and Kaptelinin and Nardi, "Affordances in HCI: Toward a Mediated Action Perspective." We read the last as a neat link: affordances are Gaver's own example of borrowed theory (p. 938), and in the same session two other authors were reworking that very concept. Sengers, a co-author of "Sustainably Unpersuaded," is thanked in Gaver's acknowledgements (p. 946).
- **The studio's methods, in one line each.** Cultural probes: kits of tasks and materials given to participants, designed to provoke inspirational responses, introduced in Gaver, Dunne and Pacenti, "Design: Cultural Probes," interactions 6(1), 1999, pp. 21 to 29. Design workbooks: in Gaver's words, "collections of design proposals and related materials," treated as both a method and a methodology (CHI 2011, pp. 1551 to 1560); this is reference [12], the source Gaver cites for designs "creating a design space around themselves" (p. 944). Batch production: making research devices in numbers for field trials, for example 130 Datacatchers, location-aware devices showing a running feed of sociopolitical statements about where they are (Boucher and Gaver, TEI 2017, pp. 243 to 251). None of these is described in this essay; they come from the studio's other publications.
- **The annotated portfolio follow-ups.** Gaver and Bowers 2012 (interactions 19(4), pp. 40 to 49) presented the studio's own artefacts with brief annotations; a later DIS 2021 paper by Hoggenmuller et al. describes the annotations as outlining "similarities and family resemblances within the works." Bowers 2012 (DIS, pp. 68 to 77) annotates nine studio pieces and, drawing on Feyerabend, suggests that design research "might build its own 'limited rationality'" rather than be brought in line with outside norms for rigour (abstract). We read this as the portfolio half of the 2012 argument, which the CHI essay only sketches with Rams.
- **The sequel.** Gaver 2014, "Science and Design: The Implications of Different Forms of Accountability" (Ways of Knowing in HCI, Springer, pp. 143 to 165). It distinguishes science and design by what each must defend: science must "explain and defend the basis of one's claimed knowledge," design must show "that one's design works" (p. 147). The idea is older; Koskinen and Krogh 2015 quote Gaver, Hooker and Dunne 2001: "The question is whether a design 'works,' not whether the methods used to devise them are sound." We read this as the place where Gaver turns the 2012 essay's hedge, that the verdict "depends on the account of science one adopts" (p. 941), into a positive account of design's own standard.
