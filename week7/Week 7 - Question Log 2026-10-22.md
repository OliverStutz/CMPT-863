## Questions and answers

### 1. What is the idea on slide 2, with the Drift Table?
One concrete piece of research through design, Gaver's own, built in 2004 at the Royal College of Art, shown before any theory. The room votes "Is this research?" (Yes / No / Depends what counts), the counts go on a card, and slide 20 asks again. The critics who later asked for standards called the table "a well known example of critical design in HCI" (Zimmerman, Forlizzi and Evenson 2007, p. 496), so the object is not in dispute; what counts as research, and by whose rules, is. The table is not in the 2012 paper. Say "Gaver and colleagues", never "the studio", for 2004.

### 2. How many fields think they own design?
No settled number; our count is about nine, depending on splits. The paper names HCI (via Fallman, p. 938), art and design schools (Frayling), and "the arts, architecture, fashion and product design" (p. 943). Our wider list with founding texts: art and design research as a discipline (Cross 1982, Design Research Society 1966); architecture and planning (Alexander 1977, Schön 1983, Rittel and Webber 1973); engineering and design science (Simon 1969); information systems (Hevner et al. 2004); computer science and HCI (Fallman 2003, Winograd 1996, Zimmerman et al. 2007); psychology (Norman 1988, Gibson); social science and STS (participatory design, Latour 2008); management (Buchanan 1992, Brown 2008); the design professions themselves. Only Cross, Fallman and the paper's own lines were verified today.

### 3. Slide 3: what did each of the four dots add?
Frayling 1993: the vocabulary, research into, through and for art and design. Fallman 2003: the diagnosis that HCI "has emerged as a design-oriented field" with almost no account of what design is; three accounts (conservative, romantic, pragmatic); Gaver cites him for HCI "as a design discipline" (p. 938). Bødker 2006: the setting shift, technology "spreads from the workplace to our homes and everyday lives and culture" (abstract). Zimmerman, Forlizzi and Evenson 2007: the named method with a model (true, how, real knowledge; "the right thing") and four criteria. Thread: a name, a diagnosis, a changed setting, a method with criteria; once there are criteria, "whose criteria" follows.

### 4. Did art schools invent "research through design" because they got no funding?
Half right. Frayling himself writes that the redefinition of research arose "largely as the pragmatic result of decisions about government funding of higher education" (1993), after the 1992 reforms and the first RAE to assess art and design as its own category. But he did not invent the category to win money: he adapted Herbert Read's distinction about art education, he opens the analysis with "Assuming that a little more than politics and money-raising are going on", he refuses research degrees for work where the art is said to "speak for itself", and he says "There must be some differentiation." Design research existed before 1993 (Archer at the RCA, the Design Research Society, Cross). The RCA was never a polytechnic. Source: `assets/frayling-1993-text.txt`.

### 5. Did Frayling predict Gaver in 1993?
No, and Gaver is not his opposite. Frayling's worry was artists wanting "research with a big 'r'" for status. But his thorny third category, research for art and design, where "the thinking is, so to speak, embodied in the artefact", is nearly Gaver's "primarily, of artefacts and systems" (p. 937) and "embody ... do not encode" (p. 944), under Frayling's word "through". Our reading: the annotated portfolio is an answer to Frayling's thorny category, artefacts plus words with "neither dominates" (p. 945). Both authors keep a brake: Frayling's "differentiation", Gaver's "moderate expectations" and norms as "political acts to be approached with care" (p. 945).

### 6. Was Fallman the one introducing design research into HCI? Was Bødker making it industrial?
No on both. Fallman described something that had already happened (participatory design in the 1970s and 1980s, Norman, DIS from 1995, Winograd 1996, which Gaver cites as reference 4). Bødker is the other way round: the second wave was the workplace; the third wave is the move out of the office into homes and everyday life. She is the workplace tradition watching its methods stretch. The Drift Table is a third-wave object with no task measure, which is the gap the 2007 criteria and Gaver's essay try to fill.

### 7. What did Zimmerman add in 2007?
A name and a venue at CHI; a model of the contribution (designers integrate "true", "how" and "real" knowledge to make "the right thing" that moves the world to a "preferred state", pp. 493 and 497); and four lenses: process ("no expectation that reproducing the process will produce the same results", p. 499), invention, relevance ("Instead of validity, the benchmark for interaction design research should be relevance", p. 499), extensibility. They say "the idea is not new" (p. 499). The lenses are the beginning of the standards call, but relevance replaces validity, so they were not asking for science; Gaver's hedge "may adopt, or be interpreted in terms of" (p. 938) fits.

### 8. The three waves of HCI, and are there more?
First wave: the lab, one user and one machine, human factors and cognitive models. Second: the workplace, groups, participatory design and field studies. Third: homes, everyday life and culture; experience, values, several right readings. Sources: Bødker 2006 (abstract names only the second and third), Harrison, Tatar and Sengers 2007 (three paradigms; "informal histories of HCI commonly identify two major intellectual waves"). Candidates for a fourth, none agreed: Bødker 2015 asked; Ashby et al. 2019 (politics, values, ethics); Frauenberger 2019 (entanglement). Widely accepted as a periodisation, not a theory. Citation counts (OpenAlex, October 2026): Bødker 2006 about 920; Harrison et al. about 390; Bødker 2015 about 330. Written up as optional slide 3b in `slides3/extra-3b-Waves.md` (1:15, 60 on-slide words, checks pass).

### 9. Are the waves heavily associated with research through design?
No, and not by either author. Neither names it. Harrison and colleagues list participatory design, value-sensitive design, user experience design, ethnomethodology, embodied interaction, interaction analysis and critical design as the third paradigm's approaches, say "design is not at home in a single paradigm", and cite Gaver's work four times. Gaver's kind of design research is third-wave; design as such is in all three. The only link in the paper is the grant name ThirdWaveHCI (p. 946).

### 10. Is the mess of manifestos (slide 4) what happened in the third wave?
Partly. All five of Gaver's examples date from 2004 to 2009 and are third-wave in subject. Both papers use the word proliferation. But Harrison and colleagues read the proliferation as one coherent paradigm, and Gaver, in his Kuhnian reading, as design research without one; the difference is mostly one of level (what interaction is, versus values, methods and "standards for evaluation", p. 942), with a real disagreement on "methodologies and legitimation". The manifesto habit is older than the third wave (p. 943). Usable line, ours: "Gaver's dozens of manifestos are the design-research face of what Harrison, Tatar and Sengers called HCI's third paradigm."

### 11. The five programmes on slide 4
Reflective Design (Sengers, Boehner, David, Kaye 2005): questioning the hidden values built into computing; Gaver quotes their abstract (p. 938). Ludic design (Gaver, 2004 to 2009): curiosity and play, "an antidote to assumptions that technology should provide clear, efficient solutions" (p. 938); example the Drift Table. Supple Interfaces (Isbister and Höök 2007, a CHI workshop proposal): richer connections and emotional experience; Gaver gives only the citation. Designing for the Self (Zimmerman 2009): products that help people become who they want to be. Product Ecology (Forlizzi 2008): a framework, not a manifesto (p. 938); example a year with families given a Roomba. Two of the five come from the critics on slide 5.

### 12. Designing for the Self: what does it mean? Is it what Apple did?
Zimmerman's "role enhancement": "the design of products that help people move closer to their idealized sense of self in a specific role through their interaction with the product" (CHI 2009, p. 395). Theory base: product attachment and identity (James, Goffman, Belk, Kleine, McAdams). Six of more than twenty products: Cherish, Smart Bag, Ensure, Magonote, Reverse Alarm Clock, Shared Moments (p. 398). Six framing constructs: role engagement, control, affiliation, ability versus bad habit, long-term goals, ritual (p. 400). He concedes the outcome is "a very difficult thing to measure" (p. 403), which is Gaver's unfalsifiability point made by the critic himself. Apple: not the same thing. The paper never mentions Apple. Same attachment theory; different mechanism: Apple works through choosing, owning and being seen with a branded object; Zimmerman designs "the product's behavior" (p. 396) at the moment a role is lived. Do not say "Apple did research through design".

### 13. Was the Prayer Companion Gaver's too?
Yes: Gaver, Blythe, Boucher, Jarvis, Bowers and Wright, CHI 2010, from the Goldsmiths studio. A device showing a stream of RSS news and social-network snippets to suggest topics for prayer, for the Poor Clares, fourteen sisters at the start, most in their eighties. Installed spring 2009; designed from "two photographs of the hallway table" because the designers could not enter the enclosure; studied through interviews over two years and the nuns' own accounts, "the ten months they have lived with it". The paper calls itself "an example of research through design" with wording close to Gaver's 2012 definition. Same "build it, live with it" move as the Drift Table, one community and far longer. Not in the 2012 paper.

### 14. The five kinds of theory, and why they matter
Gaver does not number them; five is the deck's count from his order on p. 938 plus his four-word summary on p. 939 and "a final genre". Articulations of practice (embodied movement, from a body of designs); borrowed theory (affordances become "irresistables"); manifestos (Reflective Design, ludic design); frameworks (Product Ecology, which "downplay" the normative stance); meta-theory (Zimmerman and Forlizzi, Stolterman, this essay). Why they matter: the field already produces plenty of theory, none of it in scientific form; kinds one to four are unfalsifiable, so a verification standard would discard them all, and what is at stake is "their ability to inspire new designs" (p. 941); kind five is the only one that sets rules, which is why the essay is meta-theory and ends with a form rather than a rule. Closest to the annotated portfolio: articulations of practice (our reading).

### 15. Did Zimmerman push the standards idea alone, 2007 to 2011?
No. Gaver cites four works for the call (p. 937): the 2011 CHI panel (Forlizzi, DiSalvo, Bardzell, Koskinen, Wensveen); Stolterman 2008 (cited, not quoted, and not a call for standards); Zimmerman and Forlizzi 2008; Zimmerman, Stolterman and Forlizzi 2010. Zimmerman is on two of four; Forlizzi on three. Our reading: a small circle inside the design community plus a five-person panel; Gaver answers "tendencies" (p. 937), not a person. Do not say "Gaver attacks Zimmerman" or "they wanted design to be a science".

### 16. Are the four criteria on slide 7 in the paper?
Only partly. Gaver never lists process, invention, relevance and extensibility. He cites the 2007 paper for the definition (p. 937) and "the right thing" (p. 940), and quotes "protocols, descriptions, and guidelines" (2010) and "actionable metrics" (2011) on p. 937 and "extensibility and verifiability" (2008). The four criteria come only from the 2007 paper, pp. 499 to 500.

### 17. Slide 5: does "2007 to 2011" hold?
Yes, as our reading. The 2007 paper says "there is no agreed upon standard" (p. 499) and proposes the criteria; Gaver's bracket cites 2008, 2008, 2010 and 2011; the 2011 panel is the last item before the essay. The slide is right; one Say line needs the fix listed under decisions. Four lines to say: 2007 name the method and admit no standard; 2008 "a level of extensibility and verifiability"; 2010 "develop protocols, descriptions, and guidelines"; 2011 "lacks clear expectations and standards", "actionable metrics". Bottom strip: standards "may be a route towards disciplinary legitimacy" (p. 937), Gaver's hedged guess at a motive.

### 18. Why is standardising this so hard?
Gaver's reasons: design is generative, "what might be" (p. 940), and its theories cannot be refuted ("Simply tweaking", "inconceivable", p. 940); the object is the fact, "ultimate particulars" (p. 939); no shared paradigm to anchor norms (p. 942); design fields never converge, "cumulative in the way a conversation is" (p. 943); standards do political work, "potentially repressive acts of ontological politics" (p. 943). Ours: too many owners; third-wave objects have no task; the standard-setters themselves replaced validity with relevance; the pressure is legitimacy, not epistemology (Frayling 1993, "disciplinary legitimacy" p. 937); judging design takes judgement, hence a form rather than a rule.

### 19. Who is Gaver? Verified bio
Psychologist by training; PhD UC San Diego 1988, "Everyday Listening and Auditory Icons"; SonicFinder "developed at Apple Computer" (his 1989 abstract); Rank Xerox EuroPARC, Cambridge, about 1991 to 1995 ("Technology Affordances", CHI 1991); Royal College of Art, Computer Related Design, from 1995 (Cultural Probes 1999, Drift Table 2004); Interaction Research Studio at Goldsmiths in its present form from 2006; studio moved to Northumbria late 2021, where he is Professor of Design; SIGCHI Academy 2009. About 181 works and 17,000 citations on OpenAlex. Not on the official list of SIGCHI Lifetime Research Award recipients. Nationality, birthplace, first degree and Apple job title unverified and left out. Publishes informally as Bill Gaver.

### 20. Did Gaver see the proposals to judge negatively?
Not as written. He grants the aims ("some sympathy", p. 937; integration "would make assessment of new contributions fairer and more rigorous", p. 941), turns their words into his proposal (portfolios allow "the 'extensibility and verifiability' for which some of the HCI design community have called", p. 945), and warns about the form and its enforcement ("self-policing", p. 937; "inappropriate 'scientific' models", p. 938; "misplaced", p. 942; "potentially repressive", p. 943; "consensus can come to look like constraint", p. 946). Roughly five concessions, two reframings, eleven warnings; the split is soft, so do not count aloud. He never names a critic while evaluating the call; "wrong" and "reject" appear only in the Popper passages.

### 21. Self-policing and scientism in a few words
Self-policing: the community enforcing one set of criteria on itself, "a form of self-policing that would be overly restrictive" (p. 937); the mechanism. Scientism: treating science's forms as the only form rigour can take, "insofar as HCI is prone to 'scientism'", standards "may adopt, or be interpreted in terms of, inappropriate 'scientific' models" (p. 938); the yardstick. Reviewer R4: "the move towards 'scientism' in HCI" (p. 946).

### 22. The flat-earth analogy for scientism
Advised against. In that story the dogma is about a fact and is broken by measurement, which is the method scientism privileges, so the moral the room hears is "measure more", the critics' line. Also the premise is a myth. Alternatives that keep the right sides: the BMJ 2003 parachute review ("unable to identify any randomised controlled trials"); the Drift Table given a usability test; McNamara's fallacy.

### 23. Why the two tests matter, and why mainly for scientism
Gaver says the "Contrasting accounts are chosen to complicate notions of what makes theories 'scientific'" (p. 938) and that the disagreement "destabilises the use of 'science' as shorthand" (p. 941). Popper takes away the strict yardstick; Lakatos grades the same theory well; Newton and Feynman (p. 939) show physics fails Popper too. His conclusion splits the two risks itself (p. 945): "First" scientism, "Second" norms as "political acts". Say "mainly", not "only": Lakatos returns in the convergence section (p. 941). Limit: "I am not presenting Popper's and Lakatos' accounts as particularly representative"; "Personally, I tend to think ... distinct"; "depends on the account of science one adopts" (all p. 941). Not "just examples": a strict test design fails and an accepted test it passes; any pair with that shape would do, this pair has the names the room knows. The collision is between the yardsticks and the thing measured: "Same theory, two tests, two answers. So which science did you mean?"

### 24. Table of cases where a strict test rejects valid work
Nine rows: Newton's moon (p. 939); Feynman's weak decay (p. 939); the ambiguity claim and Reflective Design under Popper (pp. 940 to 941); ultimate particulars (p. 939); the Drift Table under a usability test and the Prayer Companion under a controlled comparison (hypothetical, ours); parachutes (Smith and Pell, BMJ 2003); continental drift ("partly for lack of a mechanism"); replication, which the 2007 criteria themselves do not expect (p. 499). Pattern, ours: a test that assumes a general claim, a single outcome or a task, applied to work that is particular, plural or task-free. Caution: the wrong instrument, not anything goes; "moderate expectations" (p. 945).

### 25. Why do designers not converge? The three readings
Gaver says science converges because it assumes "a single, unitary world" so "one must be better than the other" (p. 943); design makes "what might be" (p. 940), its theories are "not falsifiable in principle" (p. 940), design fields are "cumulative in the way a conversation is" (p. 943), and design is "able to create multiple new worlds rather than describing a single existing one" (p. 943). Three readings, presented as alternatives: Kuhn, pre-paradigmatic, "must founder" (p. 942), fix: standardise, "tempting"; Latour, the invisibility of consensus, calls "misplaced" (p. 942); Law and Urry, many worlds, after the iPad "a different world, one in which the iPad exists", criteria "potentially repressive acts of ontological politics" (p. 943). His conclusions lean on the second and third. The slide's labels ("Baby science", "Messier up close", "Makes worlds") are ours; his headings are "Design as Pre-paradigmatic Research", "The Invisibility of Consensus", "The Many Worlds of Design". Why these three: three distances (centuries, the lab bench, the methods), three methods (history, ethnography, reflection on method), three verdicts, and the critics only ever looked through the first lens.

### 26. The artefact-as-fact, theory-as-annotation inversion: why it matters
Gaver says: the usual order is "theories predominating, with design examples serving as mere illustrations"; instead theory is "a form of annotation, serving to explain and point to features of 'ultimate particulars', the truths of design" (p. 944); "designed artefacts ... are not" provisional (p. 944); "If artefacts embody theory, however, they do not encode it" (p. 944); "neither dominates, and neither is subservient" (p. 945); theory's "role is limited to inspiration and annotation. It is the artefacts we create that are the definite facts" (p. 945). Why, heaviest first (ours unless marked): it defuses the Popper problem; it meets the critics' demand on Gaver's terms (p. 945, his words); it fits many worlds (partly his, p. 945); it reverses the order without discarding theory ("Theoretical writing remains important", p. 945); it says what a contribution is ("a serious form of theoretical contribution", p. 945). Not claimed: that theory is unimportant or that artefacts speak for themselves.

### 27. "If artefacts embody theory, however, they do not encode it"
Encode: a message that can be read back out whole; embody: the theory realised in the object, spread through hundreds of decisions, not extractable as a proposition. Consequences: many to many (hence "Simply tweaking" is "inconceivable", p. 940); reading needs pointing (annotation; "multiple conceptual perspectives", p. 945); the object is the fixed thing; "theory nexus" (Carroll and Kellogg, cited p. 944, missing from the reference list); patterns encode, portfolios embody ("the converse", p. 945). Deeper claim, ours: design knowledge is tacit and embodied (Polanyi, Schön), and Gaver's way out of Frayling's thorny category is object plus pointing words. Image: a chair embodies ideas about sitting; it does not state them.

### 28. Ontological politics, simply
Deciding what is real is itself a political act. Methods and practices help make the worlds they describe; when several practices make several worlds, choosing which counts favours some and suppresses others (Mol's phrase, via Law and Urry, Gaver p. 943). For Gaver, design makes worlds, so one set of criteria would decide which worlds designers may make: "potentially repressive" (p. 943), norms as "political acts to be approached with care" (p. 945).

### 29. A point and an area
All on p. 944. "If a single design occupies a point in design space, a collection of designs by the same or associated designers, a portfolio, establishes an area in that space." Comparison makes clear "a domain of design, its relevant dimensions, and the designer's opinion about the relevant places and configurations", "the dimensions along which a designer's choices may range and the invariances among them". A point shows no directions ("they do not highlight the salient or fruitful dimensions of variation"); an area shows what varies, what stays constant and where the designer chose to stand. Condition: "the same or associated designers". Second metaphor: a design "creating a design space around themselves".

### 30. Slide 12, the Rams test
The one worked example turned into a test the room runs on itself. Principles alone (p. 944: "too terse"), products alone (the look without the reasons), both together (Figure 1: notes pointing at details plus four principles). Expected answers: "No, too general" / "Partly, I could copy the look" / "More, because now I know what the look is for". "Designing in the style of" means making decisions along the dimensions he cared about, not reproducing his products. Even step three is not a recipe; the portfolio is the form that makes an apprenticeship possible at a distance. Oliver's one-device opener is recorded under decisions.

### 31. What the paper impacted
Changed: annotated portfolios as a recognised form (Gaver and Bowers 2012 and Bowers 2012 from the same studio; independent uptake in Löwgren 2013, Pierce 2014, Bardzell et al. 2016, all citing the essay); venues in parallel (RTD conference from 2013, DIS Pictorials from 2014), not proven uptake. Not changed: the standards debate (Zimmerman and Forlizzi 2014 "worked to formalize", no citation; Dalsgaard and Dindler 2014; Löwgren's "grounding"); Gaver 2014 on "productive indiscipline". 2026: about 1,100 citations; CHI 2026 Design scope names "alternatives to scientistic ways of knowing" (a scope statement, not reviewing practice); RTD returns in 2027 as RTD Roams. Say "after" and "cites", never "because of". Do not say Gaver invented portfolios alone or founded RTD.

### 32. Why highlight the Drift Table and "is it research?"
It is the dispute in miniature; it makes the title concrete (Frayling asked the same of an exhibition of paintings in 1993); it gives the talk its own measurement (two counts); every later move can be pointed back at it (Popper, usability test, third wave, point versus area, embody versus encode); it keeps the stakes personal for the "long-term practitioner" (p. 937); it fits the hook-object style.

### 33. Do the five tensions have enough depth?
Deep: 1 (whose interest do standards serve; four seats, the student is one of them), 3 (which reading; the vote gives three committed camps), 4 (would you accept a portfolio as a CHI paper; a decision, not an opinion; the strongest for involvement). Deep only with an object: 2 (fertility; tie it to Tangible Bits and Zooids). Risky in plain form: 5 (who won; a quiz unless flipped to "did the form win while the warning faded?"). Momentum comes from stakes in their own projects, roles that remove the cost of being wrong, an object always on the table, the room's own data, and a paper that does not resolve. Suggested order: 4 or 1 first, 3 while the vote is fresh, 2 in reserve, 5 last and flipped.
