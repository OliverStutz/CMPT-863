## Background for the presenter

1. **Frayling 1993.** "Research in Art and Design," Royal College of Art Research Papers vol. 1 no. 1 (p. 946, ref. 11; the RCA catalogue dates it 1993/4). A 2021 RCA conference paper summarises the three modes this way: research into art and design is historical, aesthetic or theoretical research leading to a thesis; research through art and design covers materials research, development work and action research, with studio work plus a report; research for art and design is work where thinking is embodied in the artefact, which Frayling called thorny because it is not verbally communicable. We read Gaver's definition (p. 937) as closest to "through," with the artefact weighted as heavily as in "for."
2. **Zimmerman, Forlizzi and Evenson 2007** (CHI, pp. 493 to 502). Based on interviews with interaction designers and HCI researchers. The model: designers integrate "true" knowledge (behavioural science), "how" knowledge (engineering) and "real" knowledge (anthropology) to make "the right thing," moving the world to a "preferred state" (pp. 493, 497). The four lenses (p. 499 to 500): process (rigour and a rationale for methods, documented so it can be reproduced, with no expectation of the same result), invention (a novel integration, backed by a literature review), relevance (replaces validity; a shift "from what is true" to "what is real"), extensibility (the community can build on the process or the artefacts). OpenAlex counted about 2,300 citations on 2026-10-19 (approximate). It cites Gaver's Drift Table as "a well known example of critical design in HCI" (p. 496), so Gaver's own work was already part of the case for the method.
3. **The three studio artefacts** (from the studio's other publications; none is in this paper). Drift Table (CHI 2004, Gaver, Bowers, Boucher and others): an electronic coffee table showing slowly moving aerial photography, steered by weight placed on its surface. It was a study of ludic activities "motivated by curiosity, exploration, and reflection rather than externally-defined tasks," lived with in volunteers' homes for several weeks. History Tablecloth (DIS 2006): a screen-printed electroluminescent cloth, a grid of lace-like cells that light up under objects as a halo growing over hours. A long-term household study found it prompted reflection on how objects move through a home. Prayer Companion (CHI 2010, Gaver, Blythe, Boucher, Jarvis, Bowers, Wright): a device for a group of cloistered nuns that shows a stream of RSS news and social networking items as possible topics for prayer. After ten months of deployment, the nuns said it had a meaningful, lasting role in their prayer.
4. **Who Gaver is.** PhD in psychology, UC San Diego, 1988, thesis on everyday listening and auditory icons. "Auditory Icons" (Human-Computer Interaction 2(2), 1986, pp. 167 to 177) proposed caricatures of everyday sounds as interface feedback. The SonicFinder (Human-Computer Interaction 4(1), 1989, pp. 67 to 94), "an auditory interface I developed at Apple Computer." "Technology Affordances" (CHI 1991) brought Gibson's ecological psychology to interface design. "Design: Cultural Probes" (Gaver, Dunne and Pacenti, interactions 6(1), 1999, pp. 21 to 29), written at the Royal College of Art: packets of postcards, maps and disposable cameras given to older people in Oslo, the Bijlmer near Amsterdam and Peccioli near Pisa, for a European project on increasing the presence of the elderly in local communities, used to inspire design rather than to collect data. The studio took its present form at Goldsmiths in 2006 and moved to Northumbria University in late 2021. We read the psychology background as one reason the essay is at home in philosophy of science.
5. **Third wave HCI and the grant.** Bødker 2006 (NordiCHI, "When second wave HCI meets third wave challenges"): the second wave centred on work; in the third, use contexts broaden and mix, and technology spreads from the workplace into homes, everyday life and culture. Harrison, Tatar and Sengers, "The Three Paradigms of HCI" (alt.chi, CHI 2007): human factors, then cognitive science, then a third paradigm they call the "phenomenological matrix." The ERC grant (CORDIS 226528, ERC-2008-AdG, host Goldsmiths, 1 April 2009 to 31 December 2014, EU contribution about 2.44 million euro) aimed to "help bring to maturity the emerging paradigm of third-wave HCI" through two case studies, one on environmental awareness in the home and one giving "a behind the scenes view of local neighbourhoods," with 50 to 100 batch-produced prototypes deployed in field trials "several orders of magnitude larger than normal."
6. **A fact that would impress.** The panel Gaver quotes as wanting "actionable metrics" says in its own abstract that design knowledge develops with "fundamental differences" from scientific knowledge. And the 2007 paper that started the call for standards already replaced validity with relevance. We read this as support for Gaver's own hedge: the risk he names lies in how standards "may adopt, or be interpreted in terms of," scientific models (p. 938), not in what the critics said.

## Slide 02.1: The problem: standards, scientism, self-policing

**Time:** 1:15 · **Visual:** quote card at the top with the "self-policing" sentence (p. 937); below it two columns, "What the critics asked for" and "What Gaver fears," each item with its page; one line for the concession; tagline across the bottom. No figure from the paper.

  - "clear expectations and standards" ([9], p. 937)
  - "protocols, descriptions, and guidelines" ([35], p. 937)
  - "extensibility and verifiability" ([33], p. 937)
- **What Gaver fears:**
  - HCI is "prone to 'scientism'" (p. 938)
  - "inappropriate 'scientific' models" (p. 938)
- Gaver concedes "some sympathy" and a "route towards disciplinary legitimacy" (p. 937)
- **We argue: the fight is over who gets to define quality, not over whether quality matters.**

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** The title asks what we should expect from research through design. Gaver's target is the answer others were already giving. He quotes critics from inside the design community: a CHI 2011 panel wanting clear standards and actionable metrics, Zimmerman, Stolterman and Forlizzi wanting protocols and guidelines, Zimmerman and Forlizzi wanting theory with extensibility and verifiability. Gaver concedes ground. He has "some sympathy," and standards may bring legitimacy within HCI. His worry is the side effect. Standards might become self-policing that restricts work he values for challenging the status quo. And because HCI is prone to scientism, they might import inappropriate scientific models. Notice the hedges: "might," "may," "insofar." So we argue the problem is not whether design research needs quality. It is what quality can mean for a practice that makes things, and who decides. In 2012 designers were joining CHI. Today, every paper we submit meets a reviewer holding one of these views.
- **Transition:** Gaver answers this problem in three moves, about theory, about convergence and about annotation, and the next slide puts the whole argument on one page.
- **Watch out:**
  - Do not say the critics wanted design to become a science. They asked for standards, metrics, protocols, guidelines and extensible, verifiable theory (p. 937). "Scientism" is Gaver's word for a tendency in HCI's "cultural assumptions" (p. 938), not a label he pins on Zimmerman, Stolterman or Forlizzi. Do not call them positivists.
  - Attributions: [9] is the CHI 2011 panel (Forlizzi, DiSalvo, Bardzell, Koskinen, Wensveen), "lacks clear expectations and standards for what constitutes "good" design research" and "some actionable metrics" (p. 937). [35] is Zimmerman, Stolterman and Forlizzi, DIS 2010, "protocols, descriptions, and guidelines" and "like any other research approach" (p. 937). [33] is Zimmerman and Forlizzi 2008, "extensibility and verifiability" (p. 937).
  - The critics are insiders. Gaver calls it "an undercurrent of questioning within the design community itself" (p. 937). The debate is within design research, not design against science.
  - Keep Gaver's hedges. He says "There is a risk" and standards "might lead to" self-policing; scientism matters "insofar as" HCI is prone to it (p. 937 to 938). He does not say standards are bad.

Section 09 goes deeper.

- **If asked:**
  - *Q: What exactly does Gaver mean by "scientism"? Does he define it?* A: He does not define it. On p. 938 he says HCI is prone to it "in its cultural assumptions," and contrasts this with moves to treat HCI as humanities [2] or as a design discipline [7]. We read scientism in this paper as using the word "science" as a stand-in for good research; Gaver does not define it that way himself. The dictionary sense is close: "complete belief in scientific methods, or in the truth of scientific knowledge" (Oxford Learner's).
  - *Q: Isn't it unfair to the critics? None of them asked design to become a science.* A: Agreed, and we say so. Zimmerman, Forlizzi and Evenson 2007 [34] explicitly drop the scientific test of validity: "Instead of validity, the benchmark for interaction design research should be relevance," because there is no expectation that two designers given the same problem will produce similar artefacts (CHI 2007, p. 499). Gaver's worry is subtler: standards "may adopt, or be interpreted in terms of" scientific models (p. 938). The second half matters. The risk can come from how reviewers read the standards, not from what the critics meant.
  - *Q: What did the CHI 2011 panel [9] actually say?* A: It is a four-page panel description, pp. 823 to 826, by Forlizzi, DiSalvo, Bardzell, Koskinen and Wensveen. Its abstract says "While we have made advances in integrating design research methods, approaches, and outcomes in HCI, we still have a way to go," and puts this down to "fundamental differences in the development of design knowledge as compared to scientific knowledge." Gaver quotes the "integrating" phrase on p. 937. So the panel itself says design knowledge differs from scientific knowledge. That supports our reading that the critics were not scientistic.
  - *Q: What is the actual critique in Zimmerman, Stolterman and Forlizzi 2010 [35]?* A: They interviewed 12 leading HCI design researchers, examined three historical research through design projects that came up repeatedly in the interviews, and critiqued current practice. Gaver quotes their call for "a proper research methodology that can produce relevant and rigorous theory" (p. 937, their p. 316) and for "protocols, descriptions, and guidelines" (their p. 317). According to Jeffrey Bardzell's 2016 summary, their main asks are more projects whose method is "described and critically examined" and more attention to each other's work: "It is of the utmost importance that RtD is analyzed and critiqued in a serious and ambitious way" (their p. 317). That is a call for criticism, not for a single method. I am relying on the abstract and on Bardzell's summary here, not on the full text.

Stolterman 2008 [31] argues that "science is not the best place to look for approaches and methods" for design complexity. On p. 939 Gaver even puts him "in contrast" to Zimmerman and Forlizzi, because Stolterman stresses "ultimate particulars." Yet Stolterman also co-wrote [35]. We read the citation as showing a debate inside design about "the nature and standards" of the work (p. 937), not a single opposing camp.

Bardzell answered in 2016: "Zimmerman et al. do not call for 'policing' that I could see." So keep Gaver's hedge and do not turn it into an accusation.

DiSalvo and Jeffrey Bardzell sat on the 2011 quality panel [9], and both also sat with Gaver on the CHI 2012 panel "The humanities and/in HCI" [2] (p. 946), which Gaver cites on p. 938 as a move away from scientism. We argue this is a conversation among colleagues about what quality means, which is the point of our tagline.
  - *Q: Did Gaver later accept criteria for design papers?* A: In a way. In 2017 he and Kia Höök wrote "What Makes A Good CHI Design Paper" in ACM Interactions. It describes a few shared ingredients in the design submissions the subcommittee accepted that year, offered as guidance to authors and reviewers. We read this as consistent with the 2012 essay: criteria drawn from good examples, by designers, rather than a protocol imported from elsewhere.

## Background for the presenter

- **Scientism, the word.** Oxford Learner's Dictionary gives two senses: "a way of thinking or expressing ideas that is considered to be typical of scientists" and "complete belief in scientific methods, or in the truth of scientific knowledge." The classic critical use is Friedrich Hayek's, in "Scientism and the Study of Society," published in parts in Economica from 1942 to 1944 and collected in The Counter-Revolution of Science (1952). Hayek meant the "slavish imitation of the method and language of Science" in fields where it does not fit, and said he was not attacking science in its proper sphere. We read Gaver's use as the same move: no attack on science, a warning about borrowing its forms as a badge of quality (p. 938, p. 941).
- **The CHI 2011 panel [9].** "Quality control: a panel on the critique and criticism of design research," CHI 2011 Extended Abstracts, pp. 823 to 826, DOI 10.1145/1979742.1979497. Panelists: Jodi Forlizzi, Carl DiSalvo, Jeffrey Bardzell, Ilpo Koskinen, Stephan Wensveen. The abstract calls design research "an emerging area in design that has increasing relevance to the field of HCI," and the panel's aim was "to explore ways to develop and refine critical discussions of design research within the HCI community." The word in its title is critique. Gaver's two quotations from it are on p. 937.
- **The Carnegie Mellon line, 2007 to 2010 [34, 33, 35].** Zimmerman, Forlizzi and Evenson 2007 [34] proposed research through design as a model for interaction design research in HCI, with four "lenses" for judging a contribution: process, invention, relevance and extensibility (CHI 2007, p. 499). They say the process must be documented well enough to be reproduced, but not that it will give the same result, and they replace validity with relevance. Zimmerman and Forlizzi 2008 [33] asked for theory with "extensibility and verifiability" (p. 937). Zimmerman, Stolterman and Forlizzi 2010 [35], DIS 2010, pp. 310 to 319, interviewed 12 leading design researchers and concluded the approach has problems but can be refined. We read the line as a steady push for documentation and critique, which Gaver reads as a push towards convergence.
- **Stolterman 2008 [31].** "The nature of design practice and implications for interaction design research," International Journal of Design 2(1), pp. 55 to 65, open access. Its abstract argues that research aimed at helping practitioners "has not (always) been successful" because it lacked "a sufficient understanding of the nature of design practice," and that "science is not the best place to look for approaches and methods on how to approach design complexity." Gaver uses it for "ultimate particulars" (p. 939, p. 944) and "prepared-for-action" (p. 941). Section 06 goes deeper.
- **How CHI 2012 reviewed papers.** The CHI 2012 call expected "over 1,800 Papers and Notes." Authors picked one subcommittee to review their work; the call says papers would not be penalised for imperfect fit. The Design subcommittee covered design approaches and practices and "design research issues such as aesthetics, values, effects (such as emotion), methods, practices, critique, and theory." It was chaired by DiSalvo, Forlizzi, Wakkary and Harrison. A fact that would land in this room: two SFU faculty, Ron Wakkary and Alisa Antle, served on that subcommittee.

## Background for the presenter

1. **Gaver's own map of the essay is on p. 938, not only in the abstract.** The paragraph starting "In this essay, my intention is to temper calls for disciplinary consolidation" gives one assumption per part. Second, that "disciplinary convergence is a prerequisite for cumulative growth of understanding"; his answer is three perspectives, only one of which calls for shared understandings. If anyone asks whether the slide imposes a structure, read this paragraph.

2. **What "theory" covers.** Gaver lists genres on p. 938: articulations of practice (Djajadiningrat et al. **Time:** 1:30 · **Visual:** two-column layout. Left: the five genres as a stacked list, one example each (p. 938 to 939). **Speaker notes**

Articulations of practice, like Djajadiningrat and colleagues on embodied movement, built from reflecting on their own designs. Borrowed theory, like affordances, which Overbeeke and Wensveen turned into "irresistables." Manifestos, like Sengers's Reflective Design or Gaver's own ludic design. Frameworks, like Forlizzi's Product Ecology. And meta-theory, theory about research through design itself, where Gaver puts Zimmerman and Forlizzi, Stolterman, and this essay. Claims take the form "designing for X can lead to successful outcomes," with an implicit sometimes, and no number of failures disproves "sometimes." Second, design is generative. If designers built things to refute theories, he says, they would no longer be doing research through design. Note the conditional: unscientific if Popper's criterion is accepted. He does not treat Popper as the last word.

His answer is falsifiability. He set it out in Logik der Forschung (1935; English 1959). The chapter Gaver quotes, "Science: Conjectures and Refutations," was first given as a lecture in 1953 and opens Conjectures and Refutations (1963). Gaver's point: when a puzzle fails, it "is considered the fault of the scientist using the theory, not the fault of the theory itself" ([17] p 67, quoted on p. 939). ## Background for the presenter

1. **Popper and Kuhn, the two accounts Gaver plays off.** Karl Popper (born Vienna 1902, died 1994) made demarcation, telling science from non-science, the central problem of philosophy of science. His criterion is falsifiability: a theory is scientific if some possible observation would conflict with it. He set this out in Logik der Forschung (1935; English as The Logic of Scientific Discovery, 1959). The text Gaver quotes, "Science: Conjectures and Refutations," began as a 1953 lecture and is chapter 1 of Conjectures and Refutations (1963); Gaver cites it as ref. 24 (p. 946). Thomas Kuhn (1922 to 1996) published The Structure of Scientific Revolutions in 1962, with a second edition in 1970, with its Postscript dated 1969, the one Gaver cites (ref. 17, p. 946). Normal science is puzzle-solving inside a paradigm. Kuhn's two senses of paradigm, the broad "disciplinary matrix" and the narrow "exemplar," are what Gaver describes on p. 941 as "sometimes an exemplar of successful research, and sometimes a set of common understandings."

2. **Lakatos, and why Neptune is the textbook case.** Imre Lakatos (1922 to 1974) worked at the London School of Economics. His full account is "Falsification and the Methodology of Scientific Research Programmes" (1970). Terms: the hard core, protected "by methodological fiat"; the negative heuristic, which forbids attacking the core; the protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses; and the positive heuristic, which guides how the belt changes. A programme is progressive if each new version has excess empirical content and some of it is corroborated, and degenerating if not (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, section 2.2). Neptune is the clean success: Uranus (found 1781) misbehaved, Le Verrier and Adams each predicted an unseen planet, and Galle found it in Berlin on 23 September 1846 within about a degree of Le Verrier's position. Mercury's perihelion is the matching failure: no extra body existed, and the anomaly waited for Einstein. Ref. 19 is not the 1970 paper but the 1973 Open University radio talk "Science and pseudoscience." We read Gaver's use as a fair summary of the picture but a loose use of the test (see the novel-facts question above).

3. **Feynman, in context.** Both passages come from lecture 7, "Seeking New Laws," of The Character of Physical Law: the Messenger Lectures Feynman gave at Cornell in November 1964, recorded by the BBC, published in 1965 by the BBC and from 1967 in paperback by MIT Press. In that edition the "First we guess it" passage is on p. 156 and "You need courage to say the experiments must be wrong" on p. 163, which matches Gaver's page numbers. Two things that would impress if raised: Feynman qualifies the first passage at once, "I mean after the experiment has been checked"; and he ends the weak-decay passage with "I will explain where that courage comes from later." We read Feynman as not seeing the two passages as inconsistent; Gaver reads the tension into them (p. 939). We think that is a fair rhetorical use, not a misquotation.

4. **The three manifestos Gaver names on p. 941.** *Reflective Design* (Sengers, Boehner, David and Kaye, Critical Computing, Aarhus 2005, pp. 49 to 58): reflection on unconscious values in computing should be a core principle of design. Its six principles: designers should use reflection to uncover and alter the limitations of design practice; designers should use reflection to re-understand their own role; designers should support users in reflecting on their lives; technology should support skepticism about and reinterpretation of its own working; reflection is folded into action, not separate from it; dialogic engagement between designers and users through technology can enhance reflection. Two case studies: technology in an art museum, and Intimate Objects for couples in long-distance relationships. *Supple Interfaces* (Isbister and Höök, CHI 2007 extended abstracts, pp. 2853 to 2856, ref. 16) was a CHI 2007 workshop; its call described systems that "build richer connections between people as well as deeper emotional experiences," with "an emphasis on the quality of experience rather than outcome," for example affective systems and games. *Designing for the Self* (Zimmerman, CHI 2009, pp. 395 to 404, ref. 36) applies product attachment theory: products should help people "become the person they desire to be" in a specific role, for example an alarm clock that keeps young children in bed and helps parents feel like better parents.

5. **Two details on the slide 04.1 sources worth having ready.** First, Stolterman (International Journal of Design 2(1), 2008, pp. 55 to 65) is less anti-science than his use in this essay might suggest: on p. 60 he writes that "the argument here is not that design research cannot be done in a scientific way," and his "prepared-for-action" section on p. 61 rests on Schön's reflection-in-action. We read him as a middle position, though Gaver quotes him accurately. Second, Product Ecology (Forlizzi, International Journal of Design 2(1), 2008, pp. 11 to 20) was illustrated with a year-long study in which households received either a Roomba or an upright vacuum; the Roomba changed roles and routines in the home, the upright did not. That is a concrete answer if someone asks what a "framework" produced.

- **Kuhn, "The Structure of Scientific Revolutions" (1962; second edition 1970, the one Gaver cites as [17]).** Normal science is "puzzle-solving" inside a shared paradigm; anomalies pile up, a crisis follows, and a revolution installs a new paradigm. Chapter II, "The Route to Normal Science," describes the pre-paradigm period: rival schools, fact-gathering that is "a far more nearly random activity," and writers who each "felt forced to build his field anew from its foundations" (pp. 13 and 15). Gaver's "from the very basics every time" (p. 942) is a close paraphrase of that line. Kuhn's other example is electricity before Franklin, where theories had "no more than a family resemblance" (p. 14). The schools usually end, Kuhn says, through "the triumph of one of the pre-paradigm schools" (p. 17), not through agreed rules.
- **Two Kuhn passages that would impress if raised.** First, on painting: "For many centuries ... painting was regarded as the cumulative discipline," while its goal was representation (p. 161). Second, in the Postscript: difficulties around "style in the arts may vanish if paintings can be seen to be modeled on one another rather than produced in conformity to some abstracted canons of style" (pp. 208 to 209). We read this as Kuhn himself pointing at Gaver's later move: learning from examples rather than from abstract rules. And on p. 160 Kuhn asks of the debates over whether some field is a science: "Can very much depend upon a definition of 'science'?" That is close to Gaver's "the futility of debating whether design is or should be a science" (p. 945).
- **Latour, "Science in Action" (Harvard University Press, 1987).** Its first rule of method is "We study science in action and not ready made science or technology." The two cases in the introduction are the DNA double helix and a Data General minicomputer, each followed back from a closed black box to the controversy that made it. Gaver cites Latour twice: for controversy on p. 942, and on p. 943 ("c.f. [20]") for the point that debates in design cannot use "evidence from a single, independent world as an ally." Latour's Rule 3 is the source of that idea: "Since the settlement of a controversy is the cause of Nature's representation, not its consequence, we can never use this consequence, Nature, to explain how and why a controversy has been settled." We read Gaver as going one step further for design: there is no single world for a settled controversy to point back to.
- **Law and Urry, "Enacting the social" (Economy and Society 33(3), 2004, pp. 390 to 410), and Mol.** Three claims: methods are performative, they "help to bring into being what they also discover"; if so, research is in "ontological politics"; and current social science methods still tend to make "a single world" fitted to nineteenth-century, nation-state politics. Their examples include labelling theory, British studies of inequality, the 1854 London cholera cases mapped around the Broad Street pump, and Durkheim on suicide rates. The phrase "ontological politics" they credit to Annemarie Mol (1999, in Law and Hassard, "Actor Network Theory and After," pp. 74 to 89). Worth knowing: Gaver uses [21] once before this section, on p. 940, where design "changes the context of its own activities [21]." We read that citation as the hinge between Move 1 and Move 2: the same generativity that makes design theory hard to falsify is what makes the field diverge.
- **Osborne and Rose (1999) and Collins (1994).** Osborne and Rose, "Do the social sciences create phenomena? The example of public opinion research," British Journal of Sociology 50(3), pp. 367 to 396: public opinion became a real, measurable object through survey methods and the representative sample, though such creation is hard and rarely succeeds. Collins, "Why the Social Sciences Won't Become High-Consensus, Rapid-Discovery Science," Sociological Forum 9(2), pp. 155 to 177: consensus in the natural sciences comes from lineages of research technology, which the social sciences lack. In two sentences: rapid discovery and consensus are driven by equipment that keeps producing new phenomena, not by better method or less ideology. Without such equipment, the social sciences will stay low-consensus.
- **Examples from the arts and architecture.** Gaver's own are new artistic movements, hemlines going up and down, and the redesign of household fittings (p. 943). A ready one from architecture: Mies van der Rohe's "Less is more" was answered by Robert Venturi's "Less is a bore," from "Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture," published by the Museum of Modern Art in 1966 and widely read as a founding text of the turn away from International Style modernism. Neither side refuted the other; each made buildings, which fits Gaver's "reactions, reconsiderations and fresh beginnings" (p. 943). For the iPad: announced 27 January 2010, on sale in the US 3 April 2010, about two years before CHI 2012 (5 to 10 May 2012).

## Background for the presenter

1. **Stolterman 2008, "The nature of design practice and implications for interaction design research," International Journal of Design 2(1), pp. 55 to 65.** Argument: HCI research meant to support design practice has not always succeeded because it lacks an understanding of what design practice is. Science deals with "the universal and the existing," design with "the specific, intentional and nonexisting" (Stolterman p. 59), so science is not the best place to borrow methods for design complexity. The "ultimate particular" (Stolterman p. 59) is the final designed outcome in its use context; Stolterman takes the "dignity" line from Nelson and Stolterman, The Design Way (2003). His section "Acting Designerly with Discipline and Rigor" argues design has its own rigour (rationality, judgment, argumentation, sketching). "Prepared-for-action, not guided-in-action" (Stolterman p. 61): education and tools can prepare a designer but cannot prescribe action in a complex situation; Schön's reflection-in-action is his example of an approach that works. Gaver draws on him on pp. 937, 938, 939, 941, 943 and 944: he is one of the four sources of the "undercurrent" of questioning [9, 31, 33, 35] (p. 937) and also a main source for Gaver's own answer.
2. **Carroll and Kellogg 1989 and the task-artifact cycle.** "Artifact as theory-nexus: Hermeneutics meets theory-based design," CHI 1989, pp. 7 to 14, DOI 10.1145/67449.67452. Claim: HCI designs embody multiple, distinct psychological claims, and artefacts are perhaps the most effective medium for theory development in HCI. The related "task-artifact cycle" (Carroll, Kellogg and Rosson, 1991, in Designing Interaction, Cambridge University Press) says tasks set requirements for artefacts, and new artefacts change the tasks, so the two co-evolve; Carroll and Rosson 1992 (ACM TOIS 10(2), pp. 181 to 212) turned this into "claims" analysis and scenario-based design. A fact that may land: CHI 1989, where "theory nexus" was introduced, was held in Austin, Texas, the same city as CHI 2012.
3. **Djajadiningrat, Matthews and Stienstra 2007, "Easy doesn't do it: skill and expression in tangible aesthetics," Personal and Ubiquitous Computing 11(8), pp. 657 to 676.** Argument: product interaction has neglected movement; button-based products burden cognition and "deny us the opportunity of building bodily skill," and the movement of products themselves could be expressive. "Skill" means bodily, perceptual-motor skill built through use; "expression" means the expressiveness of product movement and form. Buttons give a narrow input "bandwidth"; their remedy is analogue controls and control of several parameters at once by one skilled action. Four examples: an interactive toy, a mobile phone, the microwave oven, a programmable heating controller. Gaver uses this paper three times: as an articulation of practice about embodied movement (p. 938), as the "simultaneous analogue control" example (p. 940), and for the microwave (p. 944).
4. **Rams and Figure 1.** Vitsoe dates them to the late 1970s and says Rams began the storage system behind the company in 1957; Vitsoe was founded in 1959. A detail that supports the "who annotates" point: the current Vitsoe page itself is a small annotated portfolio, pairing each principle with one product, and principle 8, "thorough down to the last detail," is illustrated with the ET 66 calculator (1987) by Dietrich Lubs, not by Rams (Vitsoe page as archived in January 2026, not as Gaver saw it in 2012). Gaver says vitsoe.com "emphasises his design principles over the portfolio of products themselves" (p. 945).
5. **Alexander, Ishikawa and Silverstein 1977, A Pattern Language (Oxford University Press).** 253 patterns from towns down to construction details, each in a fixed format (photograph, context, problem headline, body, "Therefore" solution, diagram, links to smaller patterns). A pattern names a recurring problem and the core of a solution that can be used "a million times over" without doing it the same way twice (the book's introduction, quoted by Fincher 2012). We read the real contrast as this: a pattern's photograph is one archetypal example standing for a solution many builders have found, while Gaver's portfolio is one designer's set, kept as a set. Note Gaver's reference [1] lists only Alexander and Ishikawa (p. 946).
6. **Where annotated portfolios went next.** Gaver 2011 (workbooks, [12]) is the methodological root: collections of many concrete proposals let issues emerge "rather than being theory-driven" and create a design space instead of describing one. Gaver and Bowers 2012 (Interactions) and Bowers 2012 (DIS, nine studio pieces, "family resemblances," Feyerabend) develop the idea; footnote 1 already credits Bowers (p. 944). Löwgren 2013 ("Annotated portfolios and other forms of intermediate-level knowledge," Interactions 20(1)) places annotated portfolios between particular artefacts and general theory, next to patterns, strong concepts and experiential qualities. We read the 2012 essay as the argument and the Interactions and DIS papers as the worked examples; section 10 covers them.

## Background for the presenter

1. **Schön 1983, The Reflective Practitioner (Basic Books).** Schön looks at five professions: engineering, architecture, management, psychotherapy and town planning. His target is "technical rationality," the idea that professionals apply theory from science to practical problems. Against it he puts knowing-in-action, tacit skill that practitioners cannot fully put into words, and reflection-in-action, rethinking a situation while acting in it and testing the new framing on the spot. Chapter 3, "Design as a reflective conversation with the situation," follows an architecture studio review between a teacher, Quist, and a student, Petra, where the drawing "talks back." We read this as the missing ancestor of Gaver's view that theory should "emerge from situated design practice" (p. 942); Gaver lists the book as [28] but never cites it (p. 946).
2. **Cross 1982, "Designerly ways of knowing" (Design Studies 3(4), pp. 221 to 227).** Building on the Royal College of Art "Design in general education" report, Cross contrasts three cultures (Cross p. 221 to 222): the sciences study the natural world by experiment and value objectivity and "truth"; the humanities study human experience by analogy and criticism and value "justice"; design studies the man-made world by modelling, pattern-formation and synthesis and values practicality, ingenuity, empathy and "appropriateness." His five aspects of designerly knowing (Cross p. 226): designers tackle "ill-defined" problems; their problem-solving is "solution-focused"; their thinking is "constructive"; they use "codes" that turn abstract requirements into concrete objects; and they "read" and "write" in "object languages." The fact that impresses: Cross ends by calling for a Lakatosian research programme for design (Cross p. 226), the same philosopher Gaver leans on in 2012.
3. **Pullin 2010 and Koskinen et al. 2011, two near neighbours.** Pullin's "Curating and creating design collections" (Design and Culture 2(3), pp. 309 to 328) defines a design collection as objects by several designers, "conceived as an entity at the time of creation," set, in the abstract's words, "in the context of collections by Hussein Chalayan, Dunne & Raby, the Royal National Institute for Deaf People, and the Museum of Jurassic Technology"; collections "imply a landscape of alternatives." Koskinen, Zimmerman, Binder, Redström and Wensveen's Design Research Through Practice (Morgan Kaufmann, 2011) maps "constructive design research" onto Lab (can design be studied in a laboratory), Field (following design through society) and Showroom (research meeting design and art). We read Pullin as an earlier version of the portfolio idea and the book as the critics' own fuller account of the field.
4. **Höök and Löwgren 2012, strong concepts (ACM TOCHI 19(3), article 23).** A strong concept is intermediate-level knowledge: generative, carrying a core design idea across use situations and domains; about interactive behaviour, not static appearance; part of an artefact that also speaks of use over time; and above the level of single instances. Their examples are social navigation and seamfulness, assessed as "contestable, defensible, and substantive." Löwgren's 2013 Interactions piece, "Annotated portfolios and other forms of intermediate-level knowledge," then placed Gaver's form beside strong concepts. Section 10 picks this up.
5. **Sengers 2006 and Gaver 2014, before and after.** Sengers 2006 is a DIS 2006 workshop paper that Gaver cites for the claim that what "scientific" means is under question (p. 941, [29]); we could not find its text, so say only what Gaver uses it for. Gaver's 2014 chapter, "Science and Design: The Implications of Different Forms of Accountability" (in Olson and Kellogg, eds., Ways of Knowing in HCI, Springer, pp. 143 to 165), is the nearest he comes to limitation 2: it asks what science and design must each defend: science, "the basis of one's claimed knowledge"; design, "that one's design works" (p. 147). The distinction is older: Koskinen and Krogh 2015 quote Gaver, Hooker and Dunne 2001, "Science is epistemologically accountable," "Design is aesthetically accountable."
6. **"Who benefits" is our argument, not a finding.** We found no verified source on how design papers or PhDs were actually judged around 2012, so present it as a question for the room. The textual anchors are Gaver's worry about self-policing that is "overly restrictive" (p. 937 to 938) and his line that "consensus can come to look like constraint" (p. 946). The paper never mentions reviewing, funding or examining.

## Background for the presenter

**Olsen 2007, "Evaluating User Interface Systems Research."** The argument: UI systems research has stalled, partly for lack of "articulate criteria for evaluating systems architectures" (pre-print p. 1). Usability tests assume walk-up-and-use, a standardised task and a one- or two-hour test, and toolkit research rarely meets any of these ("The usability trap," pp. 2 to 3). His full list of claims, each with its own evidence: importance; problem not previously solved; generality; reduced solution viscosity (flexibility, expressive leverage, expressive match); empowering new design participants; addressing a new problem; power in combination (inductive combination, simplifying interconnection, ease of combination); and "Can it scale up?" (pp. 3 to 6). Just before the quoted sentence: "The best proof of a generality claim is the diversity of the Xi for which a solution is demonstrated" (pp. 3 to 4). Probes: would a portfolio pass "importance," which comes "Before all other claims" (p. 3)? Who is the "population" for a design portfolio? Best objection: Olsen's generality is one tool used many times; Gaver's portfolio is many artefacts. A line that impresses: "if a tool requires careful statistical analysis to prove its value, the innovation is frequently not important enough to make a difference" (p. 3).

**Ishii and Ullmer 1997, "Tangible Bits."** The argument: GUIs show everything as "painted bits" on screens (p. 240); the paper couples bits with graspable objects in the foreground and ambient media in the background, through interactive surfaces, coupling of bits and atoms, and ambient media (p. 235). The platforms: the metaDESK, a back-projected desk with an arm-mounted "activeLENS," a transparent "passiveLENS" and "phicons," shown with the Tangible Geospace map of MIT (pp. 237 to 238); the ambientROOM, a 6 by 8 foot Steelcase Personal Harbor with MIDI-controllable light, sound, airflow and water, where web hits play as rain or ceiling ripples (pp. 238 to 239); the transBOARD, a networked whiteboard on a SoftBoard whose barcode-tagged "hyperCARDs" store strokes (pp. 239 to 240). Just before the quotation: "we realized that metaphors which bridge physical and digital worlds are particularly interesting" (p. 240). Probes: is a portfolio with one weak member still a portfolio? Is "optics" an annotation or a design rule? Best objection: the metaphor came after the artefacts ("We first implicitly invoked this metaphor," p. 240), so it may be a rationalisation. We read that as Gaver's point: annotation follows the artefacts.

**Greenberg and Buxton 2008, "Usability Evaluation Considered Harmful (Some of the Time)."** Main claims (p. 112): scientific methods do not necessarily make science; early evaluation kills promising ideas and narrows the search to one; usability tests cannot predict cultural adoption. Evidence: Barkhuus and Rode's count, about 70 percent of CHI evaluations quantitative and 25 percent qualitative (p. 113), and visions no test could have judged: Marconi, the car, Memex, Sketchpad, NLS (pp. 116 to 117). Remedies (p. 118): usability evaluation as one method among many; ask whether a test would mean anything; drop the blanket demand; risky tests for the mainstream; learn from the studio crit. Alternatives: design rationale, a vision of what could be, scenarios of use, reflections, case studies, participatory critique (p. 118), and in the conclusion design critiques, design alternatives and cultural probes (p. 119). Probes: is the crit a standard in the sense of [9]? Would Gaver accept their split cure? Best objection: they evaluate systems, not theory, and footnote 2 says "It would be just as inappropriate to drop usability testing altogether" (p. 119).

**Wobbrock and Kientz 2016, "Research Contributions in Human-Computer Interaction."** The argument: HCI knowledge comes in a few contribution types, each with its own way of being judged; the types come from Wobbrock's 2012 whitepaper and fed CHI 2016's keywords (pp. 40, 43). The seven: empirical (observation and data); artifact ("generative design-driven activities (invention)"); methodological (how we research or design); theoretical (judged by "novelty, soundness, and power to describe, predict, and explain"); dataset (a shared corpus); survey (synthesis of a mature topic); opinion (essays that "seek to change the minds of readers through persuasion") (pp. 40 to 42). CHI 2016 split empirical in two. Shares of 2,316 submissions: empirical study of system use 44.0 percent, of people 28.4, artifact or system 24.2, method 19.2, theory 6.1, essay or argument 3.8, survey 2.4, dataset 1.3; more than one type could be ticked (p. 44, Figure 2). Probes: is Gaver's essay theory or opinion on their map? Does "Theory validation is almost always achieved through empirical work" (p. 41) leave room for annotation? Best objection: a map is not a demand for convergence, and the level of research activity "should not be confused with the value placed on particular contribution types" (p. 44).

**Wobbrock, Morris and Wilson 2009, and the 2020 review.** Method: 20 non-technical participants (9 female, mean age 43.2) saw the effects of 27 commands on a Microsoft Surface and performed a gesture to cause each, one- and two-handed, thinking aloud (p. 1085). The 1,080 gestures were classified in a four-part taxonomy (p. 1086) and grouped to score agreement. The largest groups formed a conflict-free set covering 57.0 percent of proposals (p. 1087), 48 gestures in all (p. 1088). Each author's own gestures matched only 43.5 percent of the user set (p. 1090). The review: 216 studies, 5,458 participants, 148,340 gestures (p. 855), 10,752 of them agreed upon (p. 864); studies still rising through 2019, "the peak of GES [gesture elicitation studies] has not been reached yet" (p. 858). Probes: is a consensus gesture set a theory, a method or an artefact? Does legacy bias (p. 863) make consensus conservative, Gaver's "consensus can come to look like constraint" (p. 946)? Best objection: Wobbrock co-authored the review, so its upbeat framing is not independent.

**Shin et al. 2019, "Slow Robots for Unobtrusive Posture Correction."** The argument: a robot-arm monitor moved below the perception threshold can correct posture without interrupting work (contributions, p. 2). Study 1: 6 students, 32 conditions (8 motions, 4 speeds) during reading, key press on noticing, about 1.5 hours; at 0.5 mm/s noticing took 64.22 seconds on average (p. 3); the design then used 1.0 mm/s (p. 4). Study 2: 12 students and office workers (mean age 24.55), four hours on two consecutive days, actuation only on day 2, morning and afternoon groups; measures were posture from three cameras, motion logs, a modified NASA-TLX, an annoyance scale and an exit interview (p. 5). Results: unbalanced time fell (t = 3.54, p = 0.002), corrections rose about 66 percent, performance and focus unchanged (p. 7). Probes: would a counterbalanced, blind version still be ecologically valid? Is "slow robots" a "designing for X" claim (Gaver p. 940)? Best objection: Gaver means tests of design theory, and Shin et al. test a device. We read their concept claim (p. 8) as theory of Gaver's kind, which keeps the row fair.

## Background for the presenter

**Gaver and Bowers, "Annotated portfolios," Interactions 2012.** The magazine version of the idea, written in the same studio. They propose annotated portfolios "as a way to communicate design research" and as an alternative to the view that design must "engage in some sort of theory formation" to count as research. They add that one should "grow design's identity as research from its existing practices and reasoning" rather than mutate design "to become more like 'real' research" (both as quoted by Löwgren 2013; the Goldsmiths full text is restricted). Their worked example starts with two pieces for older people, with annotations such as "influencing autonomous drift," then adds three earlier studio pieces (Löwgren's summary). What it adds to Gaver 2012: a worked portfolio, which the essay only sketched with Rams (p. 945).

**Bowers, "The logic of annotated portfolios," DIS 2012.** Bowers reads research through design as an orientation to "Third Wave" HCI and discusses the "disciplinary anxieties" it raised. Drawing on Feyerabend and the sociology of science, he argues design research might build its own "limited rationality" rather than follow outside norms of rigour. The portfolio captures "family resemblances" across a collection, shown on "nine well-known pieces created by the Goldsmiths Interaction Research Studio." What it adds: the philosophical backing that the essay's footnote 1 credits to Bowers (p. 944).

**Löwgren, "Annotated portfolios and other forms of intermediate-level knowledge," Interactions 2013.** Löwgren places portfolios on a scale from single artefacts to general theory and lists other intermediate forms, roughly in date order: design methods and tools; design guidelines and usability heuristics; patterns; concepts (the Dynabook) and strong concepts; experiential qualities; and criticism, with an "interaction criticism" discipline. He agrees general theory is of limited value but says theory "can be generative for design at times." He suggests rigour for such knowledge could come from "horizontal and vertical grounding." What it takes from Gaver: portfolios become one form among several, which is our "both" lane.

**Höök and Löwgren, "Strong concepts," TOCHI 2012.** Strong concepts are intermediate-level knowledge "more abstracted than particular instances, without aspiring to be at the scope of generalized theories." They are generative, carry a core design idea across use situations and domains, concern interactive behaviour rather than static appearance, and sit above particular instances. The examples are social navigation and seamfulness; the criteria are "contestable, defensible, and substantive." The paper cites Gaver 2012. We read its stated aim, "an academic culture of discursive knowledge construction," as close to Gaver's "discursiveness and elaboration" (p. 946), though it seeks knowledge above single designs.

**Dalsgaard and Dindler, "Between theory and practice: bridging concepts in HCI research," CHI 2014.** A bridging concept is intermediary knowledge between theory and practice with three parts: "a theoretical foundation, a set of design articulations and a range of exemplars." The paper compares it with strong concepts and conceptual constructs, and works through "peepholes." What it adds: an explicit tie to theory, which Gaver treats as secondary to artefacts (p. 945).

**Zimmerman and Forlizzi, "Research Through Design in HCI," Ways of Knowing in HCI, 2014.** The chapter tells the history of their 2007 model, says CHI now has "two technical papers committees devoted to design research" (p. 178), and that they "worked to formalize this research approach" (p. 178). It recommends five steps: select, design, evaluate, reflect and disseminate, repeat (p. 184). Teams choose a practice from Koskinen et al.'s Lab, Field and Showroom (p. 184) and find exemplars. The chapter does not cite Gaver 2012, but it names the Drift Table and Prayer Companion as exemplars (pp. 175, 186 to 187; from the studio's other publications, pitfall 12).

**Gaver, "Science and Design: The Implications of Different Forms of Accountability," same volume, 2014.** Science is defined by "epistemological accountability," being able to "explain and defend the basis of one's claimed knowledge"; design by "aesthetic accountability," showing "that one's design works," where "aesthetic" means how well many features are composed (p. 147). He argues methodological frameworks can cost design its "productive indiscipline" (p. 162) and states that "Annotated portfolios may capture best what we can know through design" (p. 161). We read it as the essay's sequel.

**Research Through Design (RTD) conference.** Biennial: 2013 at the BALTIC in Gateshead, 2015 Cambridge, 2017 Edinburgh, 2019 Delft and Rotterdam. Its format is "a curated exhibition of design research artefacts accompanied by round-table discussions in 'Rooms of Interest'," which the organisers say is inspired by the design school "crit." It paused after 2019 and returns in 2027 as RTD Roams in York, Melbourne and Tokyo.

**DIS Pictorials.** Introduced at DIS 2014 in Vancouver. Rules in the DIS 2026 call: at most 12 pages excluding references, an abstract of at most 150 words, the DIS pictorial template, dual anonymous review, ACM archival status, the same quality as papers. Visuals lead: "text is used to annotate the visuals." TEI and Creativity and Cognition also run pictorial tracks.

**Pierce, "On the presentation and production of design research artifacts in HCI," DIS 2014.** Pierce reviews examples of research through design and sorts design research artefacts into three types: operational prototypes and products, conceptual and material design studies and experiments, and design proposals. He separates verbal articulation, design articulation and "concept-things," and ends with three provocations: "thingly publications, pure design research, and making design research artifacts more public." It cites Gaver 2012. What it adds: a push to publish the artefact itself, not just a paper about it.

**Bardzell, Bardzell, Dalsgaard, Gross and Halskov, "Documenting the Research Through Design Process," DIS 2016.** From two contrasting cases they build "a framework for planning and evaluating RtD documentation" with three core concerns: "the medium of documentation, the performativity of documentation, and providing equal support for both research and design." It cites Gaver 2012. We read it as procedure for the process, which is what the 2010 call asked for (p. 937), without a scientific model.

**Frauenberger, "Entanglement HCI The Next Wave?," TOCHI 2019.** Our entanglement with technology raises "ontological uncertainties, epistemological diffusion and ethical conundrums." Drawing on Actor-Network Theory, post-phenomenology, object-oriented ontology and agential realism, he gives four perspectives: (a) the performative relationship between humans and technology; (b) knowledge generation reframed around phenomena; (c) tracing accountabilities, responsibilities and ethical encounters; (d) design and mattering practices beyond user-centred design.

**Beaudouin-Lafon, Bødker and Mackay, "Generative Theories of Interaction," TOCHI 2021.** A generative theory draws on empirical theories of human behaviour to define concepts and "actionable principles" for analysing, critiquing and constructing new artefacts. Their three examples are Instrumental Interaction, Human-Computer Partnerships, and Communities and Common Objects, with tables of questions for analysis, critique and construction. Gaver's "generative" (p. 940) means making new worlds; theirs means theory that guides design.

(2) CHI 2026 had eighteen subcommittees and 6,730 paper submissions; Design had 485, with 37.9% moving to revise and resubmit.

## Background for the presenter

- **The course brief's five discussion principles.** The brief (`Instructions Week 7.md`) says the second half is "a conversation, not an additional lecture," and that strong discussions often: (1) move between "high-level questions" about the paper's significance and "specific questions" about its methods, design or evidence; (2) ask questions "that allow for multiple defensible perspectives rather than simple factual answers"; (3) use "brief pair or small-group discussions"; (4) "follow interesting ideas that emerge rather than rigidly working through a list of prepared questions"; (5) "encourage participation across the class rather than allowing a few students to dominate." The presenter's role is to "facilitate": keep it moving, probe, follow up, redirect.
- **How the three steps map onto them.** We read the plan this way. Step 1, hands in three, is a specific question with three defensible answers (principles 2 and 5: everyone votes). Step 2, pairs with role cards, is principle 3 directly, and the two roles on each card give principle 2. Step 3, anchors then a round, covers principle 1 (each anchor pairs a page-level probe with a field-level one), principle 4 (pick the anchors from what the room raised, not in list order) and principle 5 (one sentence from everyone). The If asked answers above are for the presenter, not for reading out: the brief's "not an additional lecture" means two sentences, then a question back.
- **How to credit a commentary.** The brief asks presenters to use the commentaries and to credit "the students who raised them," and commentary integration carries 4 of the 18 points (Part 1.1). On the slide: the student's own words, verbatim, with the name. In the room: read one sentence aloud, name the author, and ask them to take the first position on the tension it feeds ("You wrote this; where do you stand now?"). Never paraphrase a commentary into a stronger claim. If the student is absent, read it and ask the room to answer it. Note who spoke: the presenter grades commentaries and participation after class (course brief).
- **The critics' criteria, in their own words.** Zimmerman, Forlizzi and Evenson 2007 (Gaver's [34]) proposed four lenses for evaluating research through design: process (rigour and rationale of methods, documented so the process can be reproduced), invention ("a novel integration of various subject matters"), relevance ("Instead of validity, the benchmark for interaction design research should be relevance"), and extensibility (the community can build on the process or the artefacts) (CHI 2007, p. 499 to 500). Useful when someone says the critics only wanted metrics.
- **Lakatos's vocabulary for "who counts fertility."** A programme is progressive when each new theory has excess empirical content over its predecessor and some of that content is corroborated, and degenerating if not (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Imre Lakatos," section 2.2). Gaver uses "productive" and "fertility" (p. 941) but not "degenerating."
