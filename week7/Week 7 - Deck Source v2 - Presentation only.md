**Time:** 1:30 · **Visual:** two cards side by side, "Popper" and "Lakatos," each with a one-sentence test in plain words. Between them, a short line: "They disagree about what science is." The quotation across the bottom as the only quote on the slide.

**On the slide**

# 03 Two tests: Popper and Lakatos

**Purpose:** Run the two borrowed tests on design theory as a pair of scorecards, so the room sees a verdict rather than a philosophy lecture, and plant our one pushback (who judges "productive"?) for the end. **Slides:** 2. **Time:** 3:30. **Speaker:** Oliver. **Previous section:** 02 The fight. **Next section:** 04 The puzzle.

Written 2026-10-21 for version 2. Quotations reused from `../slides/04-Theory.md`, verified there; the ambiguity passage re-read in `../assets/paper-text.txt`.

## Slide 03.1: Test 1, Popper: could you prove a design rule wrong?

**Time:** 2:00 · **Visual:** a scorecard in three panels. Left panel: a design rule as one sentence with the word "sometimes" highlighted. Middle panel, headed "To refute it you would need": two identical system icons differing only by a question mark. Right panel: a large stamp reading "Fails Popper." Beneath, one line in small type with the two-way underspecification.

**On the slide**
- Every design rule hides a word: "X will *sometimes* lead to successful outcomes."
- Gaver's test case is his own 2003 claim: ambiguity in a design makes people engage more deeply.
- To refute it you would need two systems identical except for ambiguity. "Simply tweaking" one misrepresents how designers work.
- **Verdict: fails Popper.** Theory underspecifies design, and design underspecifies theory.

## Slide 03.2: Test 2, Lakatos: does it breed new work?

**Time:** 1:30 · **Visual:** three small cards, "Reflective Design," "Supple Interfaces," "Designing for the Self," each with "refutable" struck through and "inspires designs" ticked. A stamp reading "Passes Lakatos." Beneath, the three adjectives in large type. Bottom line in our colour: the pushback.

**On the slide**
- Reflective Design, Supple Interfaces, Designing for the Self: none can be refuted. What counts is whether they inspire new designs.
- What Popper would reject as "'merely' confirmatory" is, seen Lakatos's way, "testimony to the fertility of the overarching theory."
- **Verdict: passes Lakatos.** Design theory tends to be "provisional, contingent, and aspirational": theory that is "sometimes right."
- We argue: "productive" is also a standard. Who judges it?

## Slide 04.1: Why can't designers agree? Pick a diagnosis.

**Time:** 2:30 · **Visual:** three cards across the slide, A, B, C, each with a two-word name in large type, one plain sentence, the thinker in small type, and a coloured tag at the bottom: "Fix: standardise," "Fix: nothing," "Fix: let it multiply." Under the cards, the iPad sentence as the one full-sentence quotation. The three "Fix:" tags are built after the vote is counted, since they give the answer away. Footer in large type: "Vote: which is HCI as you have met it?"

**On the slide**
- Dozens of manifestos, no agreed methods. Why?
- **A. Baby science.** No shared paradigm yet; it "must founder." Fix: standardise.
- **B. Messier up close.** Science may be just as contested day to day; "most of us" seem to share core values. Fix: nothing.
- **C. Makes worlds, not maps.** After the iPad, you design "for a different world." Fix: let it multiply.
- **Vote: which is design research in HCI, as you have met it?**

## Slide 05.2: What that flips: the object is the fact, theory is the caption

**Time:** 1:30 · **Visual:** two rows. Top row, labelled "Usual order": the word THEORY in large type, a small system icon beneath it captioned "illustration." Bottom row, labelled "Gaver's order": a large artefact icon, the word "theory" in small type beside it captioned "annotation." Right third of the slide: a scatter of seven dots inside a loose outline, captioned "one design is a point; a portfolio marks out an area."

**On the slide**
- Usual order: theory leads, the system illustrates.
- Gaver's order: the artefacts are the "definite facts." Theory is the caption.
- Objects embody theory but do not encode it. The caption makes the choices readable.
- One design is a point in design space. A portfolio marks out an area: what varied, what never did.
- In several respects the converse of a pattern language: patterns abstract across examples, portfolios keep each one particular.
