## 1. Weiser 1991, "The Computer for the 21st Century"

### W1. A vision plus three artefacts: a portfolio before the word
- **Reading:** "Prototype tabs, pads and boards are just the beginning of ubiquitous computing. The real power of the concept comes not from any one of these devices; it emerges from the interaction of all of them." (Guelph PDF p. 5)
- **Gaver:** "If a single design occupies a point in design space, a collection of designs by the same or associated designers ... establishes an area in that space" (p. 944); manifestos' "primary function is to build an account of a practice to be pursued in the future" (p. 938).
- **Direction:** instance.
- **So what:** Weiser's essay is a manifesto whose claim rests on a family of related artefacts, not on any one of them, which is the structure Gaver recommends in 2012. That supports Gaver's claim that the form is legitimate in HCI, but it also suggests the form is old, so the essay's novelty lies in naming it as theory, not in inventing it.
- **Slide line:** Weiser 1991: tabs, pads, boards; power "emerges from the interaction of all of them." A portfolio area (p. 944).

### W2. Building as a way of knowing
- **Reading:** "By building and using these boards, researchers start to experience and so understand a world in which computer interaction casually enhances every room." (Guelph PDF p. 5)
- **Gaver:** "most of us agree that the practice of making is a route to discovery" (p. 942), inside his hedged "invisible consensus" list.
- **Direction:** supports (an instance of a value Gaver says the field shares).
- **So what:** At PARC, a computer science lab, knowledge was claimed from building and living with prototypes, which is evidence that Gaver's "most of us" value reaches beyond design schools. It also cuts the other way: if engineers share the value, it cannot be what makes research through design distinct.
- **Slide line:** Weiser: "By building and using these boards, researchers start to ... understand." Making as discovery (p. 942).

### W3. A world that cannot be predicted from its fragments
- **Reading:** "To extrapolate from today's rudimentary fragments of embodied virtuality resembles an attempt to predict the publication of Finnegan's Wake after just having invented writing on clay tablets." (Guelph PDF p. 8)
- **Gaver:** design "is concerned with creating what might be" (p. 940); after the iPad, anyone developing tablets "is designing for a different world, one in which the iPad exists" (p. 943).
- **Direction:** instance.
- **So what:** Weiser concedes that his vision cannot be checked against the world it aims to create, which is Gaver's reason why generative theory is "aspirational" rather than verifiable (p. 938). We argue Weiser's pads are a cleaner case for Gaver's world-making point than the iPad, because the class read the vision before the world arrived; do not claim a causal line from Weiser to the iPad.
- **Slide line:** Weiser: predicting ubicomp is like predicting Finnegan's Wake from clay tablets. Theory as aspiration (p. 940, 943).

### W4. Borrowed theory to justify a design stance
- **Reading:** "Such a disappearance is a fundamental consequence not of technology, but of human psychology." Weiser then lists Polanyi's "tacit dimension," Heidegger's "ready-to-hand" and Brown's "periphery." (Guelph PDF p. 1)
- **Gaver:** "Borrowed theories (or concepts) are often used both to inspire new designs and to articulate existing ones" (p. 938); manifestos "borrow theories to justify it" (p. 938).
- **Direction:** instance.
- **So what:** Weiser's psychology and philosophy do the justifying work Gaver assigns to borrowed theory, and nobody tests whether Heidegger is right about tools. That is Gaver's point that such theory is judged by what it inspires, not by refutation.
- **Slide line:** Weiser borrows Polanyi and Heidegger to justify invisibility: borrowed theory, in Gaver's taxonomy (p. 938).

## 2. Wobbrock and Kientz 2016, "Research Contributions in Human-Computer Interaction"

### K1. Popper is the official test for theory
- **Reading:** "Theories should be testable and falsifiable; if they are not, they do not qualify as scientific theories [6]." (p. 41; endnote 6 is Popper, The Logic of Scientific Discovery, p. 44)
- **Gaver:** "Research through design is clearly unscientific if Popper's [24] criterion of falsifiablity is accepted" (p. 940, "falsifiablity" sic); unfalsifiable manifestos are still valuable for "their ability to inspire new designs" (p. 941).
- **Direction:** challenges (and shows Gaver's worry was real).
- **So what:** Four years after the essay, the taxonomy behind the CHI 2016 contribution types defines theory by Popper's criterion, so Gaver was not arguing against a straw man. By that rule none of the manifestos Gaver lists counts as a theoretical contribution, which either confirms his fear of "inappropriate 'scientific' models" (p. 938) or shows that his "theory" means something CHI files under another heading.
- **Slide line:** Wobbrock and Kientz: theories "should be testable and falsifiable" (p. 41). Gaver's Popper test, made policy (p. 940).

### K2. Artifacts are generative and carry knowledge
- **Reading:** "artifact contributions arise from generative design-driven activities (invention)"; "New knowledge is embedded in and manifested by artifacts and the supporting materials that describe them"; artifacts "are often accompanied by empirical studies but do not have to be, and sometimes should not be [4]"; design expressions "are evaluated by how insightful, compelling, and innovative is their portrayal." (all p. 40; [4] is Greenberg and Buxton)
- **Gaver:** "Design, and research through design, is generative" (p. 940); "artefacts embody the myriad choices made by their designers" (p. 944).
- **Direction:** supports.
- **So what:** The taxonomy concedes both of Gaver's premises, generative design and knowledge embedded in artefacts, and already names a non-scientific standard for design expressions. We argue an annotated portfolio would sit under "artifact" with the annotations as the "supporting materials," which suggests Gaver's proposal fits CHI's map better than his essay implies.
- **Slide line:** Wobbrock and Kientz: knowledge "embedded in and manifested by artifacts and the supporting materials" (p. 40). Annotations, in Gaver's terms.

### K3. Same caution, opposite conclusion
- **Reading:** "while we should be wary of constraining our imaginations, we should embrace giving definition to the knowledge we produce." (p. 44); HCI types took decades "to emerge, converge, and stabilize" (p. 40).
- **Gaver:** "the design research community should be wary of impulses towards convergence and standardisation" (p. 937); "convergence may not be the only or best model for" progress (p. 945 to 946).
- **Direction:** challenges.
- **So what:** Both papers say "wary," but Wobbrock and Kientz treat definition as the safeguard and convergence as maturity, while Gaver treats convergence as the risk. This is the clearest place to show the room that the disagreement is about whether definition constrains, not about whether imagination matters.
- **Slide line:** Both say "wary": Gaver of convergence (p. 937); Wobbrock and Kientz of constraint, yet "embrace giving definition" (p. 44).

### K4. A published yardstick for Gaver's own essay
- **Reading:** "Opinion research contributions are evaluated on the strength of their argument. Strong arguments credibly use supporting evidence and fairly consider opposing perspectives." (p. 42). At CHI 2016, Essay/Argument papers had a 21.6 percent acceptance rate (p. 44).
- **Gaver:** quotes his opponents in their own words and concedes "I have some sympathy with these concerns" (p. 937); limits his claim: "I am not proposing here that annotated portfolios subsume all other forms of design theory" (p. 945).
- **Direction:** instance (a lens for judging the essay).
- **So what:** By this yardstick the essay scores well on considering opposing views, since it quotes [9], [33] and [35] directly. It scores less well on supporting evidence, since its one worked example is a sketch of Rams's portfolio (p. 945), not a portfolio annotated and used by researchers.
- **Slide line:** Judge the essay as an essay: "strength of their argument," "fairly consider opposing perspectives" (Wobbrock and Kientz, p. 42).

## 3. Greenberg and Buxton 2008, "Usability Evaluation Considered Harmful (Some of the Time)"

### G1. The same Popper diagnosis, a split cure
- **Reading:** "The scientific method advocates risky hypothesis testing: the more the test tries to refute the hypothesis, the more powerful it is." "the existence proof as used in HCI is confirmative hypothesis testing, for the evaluator is seeking confirmatory evidence." (p. 113). Cure: "For really novel innovations, existence proofs are likely appropriate. For mainstream systems or modest variations of established ideas, we should likely favor risky hypothesis testing." (p. 118)
- **Gaver:** "practice is usually intended to confirm theories, not falsify them" (p. 940); "What Popper would reject as 'merely' confirmatory evidence is, from this point of view, testimony to the fertility of the overarching theory" (p. 941).
- **Direction:** supports the diagnosis, challenges the cure.
- **So what:** Greenberg and Buxton hold up Popper's mirror to all of HCI evaluation, which supports Gaver's point that confirmation-seeking is not a design-only weakness. But their remedy is a split standard, existence proofs for the new and refutation for the mainstream, while Gaver's Lakatos move drops refutation as the test for design theory altogether.
- **Slide line:** Greenberg and Buxton: HCI tests seek "confirmatory evidence" (p. 113). Same diagnosis as Gaver (p. 940); split cure (p. 118).

### G2. A rigour without metrics: the crit
- **Reading:** quoting Snodgrass and Coyne on architecture: "An absence of defined criteria and procedural rules does not, therefore, give free rein to merely individual responses, since these have already been structured within the framework of what is taken as significant and valid by the design community." (p. 114). Their own words: "There is a rigour. There is a discipline. It is just not the same rigour and discipline that we currently encourage, teach, practice or accept." (p. 118)
- **Gaver:** "most of us working in research through design seem to share a set of common values" (p. 942), hedged: "Perhaps we do not all agree with all of these assumptions all of the time" (p. 942).
- **Direction:** supports.
- **So what:** This supplies the mechanism Gaver's "invisible consensus" lacks: a community judges quality through tacit norms and the design crit, without written metrics. It also shows that the critics' "actionable metrics" (p. 937) and Gaver's tacit consensus are two answers to the same reviewing problem, and the crit is a third.
- **Slide line:** Greenberg and Buxton: "There is a rigour ... just not the same rigour" (p. 118). Gaver's invisible consensus (p. 942).

### G3. Why convergence can constrain
- **Reading:** "we converge on that which we can measure, which is almost always that which we are already familiar with." (p. 115); researchers "first choose the method (e.g., controlled study) and then concoct a problem that fits that method." (p. 113)
- **Gaver:** standards risk "a form of self-policing that would be overly restrictive" (p. 937 to 938); "consensus can come to look like constraint" (p. 946).
- **Direction:** supports.
- **So what:** Greenberg and Buxton describe a concrete route by which shared evaluation norms narrow what gets studied, which turns Gaver's general worry into a checkable claim about reviewing. Their evidence is still mostly anecdote and the Barkhuus and Rode count they cite, so it strengthens Gaver's plausibility, not his proof.
- **Slide line:** Greenberg and Buxton: "we converge on that which we can measure" (p. 115). Gaver: consensus as "constraint" (p. 946).

### G4. Visions count, and so do Gaver's probes
- **Reading:** on Memex, NLS and Sketchpad: "It is the vision ... as well as the technology that was critical." (p. 117; dashed clause "even if inaccurate" omitted). They name "a vision of what could be" among valid alternatives to usability tests (p. 118) and cite Gaver's own studio: "Gaver et. al. are developing methods that probe cultural reactions and technology uptake by niche cultures [11]." (p. 119; [11] is Gaver, Dunne and Pacenti, "Design: Cultural probes," 1999)
- **Gaver:** what matters for manifestos is "their ability to inspire new designs" (p. 941).
- **Direction:** supports.
- **So what:** Greenberg and Buxton already value visions by what they set in motion, which is Gaver's fertility criterion in historical form. The citation of cultural probes shows the two papers belong to one argument: in 2008 Gaver's studio was the example of an alternative method; in 2012 Gaver argues for an alternative form of theory.
- **Slide line:** Greenberg and Buxton cite Gaver's cultural probes as an alternative to usability tests (p. 119).

## 4. Olsen 2007, "Evaluating User Interface Systems Research" (pre-print; section names)

### O1. Generality as the area between examples
- **Reading:** "If one has used the tool to solve three diverse problems then one can argue that the tool solves most of the problems lying in the space between the demonstrated solutions. The greater the diversity and the larger the number of demonstrated solutions, the stronger the claim." (section "Generality," pre-print p. 4)
- **Gaver:** "If a single design occupies a point in design space, a collection of designs ... establishes an area in that space" (p. 944); portfolios allow "the 'extensibility and verifiability'" the critics asked for (p. 945).
- **Direction:** supports (the same spatial argument, from systems research).
- **So what:** Olsen and Gaver use the same logic: diverse examples establish an area, and the area carries the general claim. Gaver asserts on p. 945 that portfolios give extensibility but does not say how; Olsen's criterion, diversity and number of examples, is one way to state that claim, and it comes from the camp Gaver might be expected to resist.
- **Slide line:** Olsen: three diverse solutions cover "the space between" them. Gaver: a portfolio "establishes an area" (p. 944).

### O2. Standards built to escape the measurable
- **Reading:** "There are those who respond with 'If it can't be measured it is not research.' This reduces research to the study of the trivially measurable." ("The usability trap," pre-print p. 3). "This paper shows a variety of alternative standards by which complex systems can be compared and evaluated." ("Summary," pre-print p. 7). But also: "User interface technology, like any other science, moves forward based on the ability to evaluate new improvements to ensure that progress is being made." ("Summary," pre-print p. 6)
- **Gaver:** standards "may adopt, or be interpreted in terms of, inappropriate 'scientific' models of research and theory for the field" (p. 938).
- **Direction:** challenges.
- **So what:** Olsen is a counterexample to the step from "standards" to "scientism": his criteria (importance, generality, viscosity, expressive match) are argued claims, not controlled experiments. Yet he calls UI technology a science and ties progress to evaluation, so a class can defend both readings: standards for a non-science, or the cumulative model Gaver says may not be "the only or best" (p. 945).
- **Slide line:** Olsen: "alternative standards," not usability tests. Standards without scientism, or Gaver's fear? (p. 938)

### O3. Complexity defeats the controlled experiment
- **Reading:** "Complex systems generally do not yield to simple controlled experimentation. This is mostly due to the fact that good systems conquer complexity and complexity confounds controlled experimentation." ("Summary," pre-print pp. 6 to 7)
- **Gaver:** "the synthetic nature of design is incompatible with the controlled experiments useful for theory testing" (p. 940).
- **Direction:** supports.
- **So what:** An engineer reaches Gaver's conclusion about controlled tests for a different kind of artefact, so the argument is not special pleading for design. The difference is that Olsen replaces the experiment with other criteria, while Gaver replaces criteria with annotation.
- **Slide line:** Olsen: "complexity confounds controlled experimentation." Gaver: synthesis defeats controlled tests (p. 940).

## 5. Wobbrock, Morris and Wilson 2009, "User-Defined Gestures for Surface Computing"

### M1. Consensus as the design method
- **Reading:** the agreement score "reflects, in a single number, the degree of consensus among participants" (p. 1087); "User-centered design is a cornerstone of human-computer interaction. But users are not designers" (p. 1085); "about 72% of gestures were mouse-like one-point touches or paths" and a participant: "I'm a child of the mouse." (p. 1090)
- **Gaver:** "most pursue some variation on user-centred design" (p. 942); "consensus can come to look like constraint" (p. 946); designing changes the world one designs for (p. 943).
- **Direction:** challenges (and illustrates Gaver's risk).
- **So what:** Here consensus is not a threat to design but its criterion, and the result is coherent and adoptable. The same data show Gaver's risk at small scale: agreement among 2008 users reproduced the desktop world they lived in, which is the world-making point (p. 943) seen from the user's side.
- **Slide line:** Wobbrock et al.: consensus "in a single number" (p. 1087); 72% mouse-like (p. 1090). Consensus as constraint (p. 946)?

### M2. An empirical paper passes Gaver's fertility test
- **Reading:** the paper presents "an approach to designing tabletop gestures that relies on eliciting gestures from non-technical users" (p. 1083) and names its own next step: "An important next step is to validate our user-defined gesture set." (p. 1091). Outside fact: a DIS 2020 systematic review covers "N=216 gesture elicitation studies" (Villarreal-Narvaez, Vanderdonckt, Vatavu and Wobbrock, DIS 2020, p. 855).
- **Gaver:** with Lakatos, programmes that "predict novel facts" (p. 940); confirmations are "testimony to the fertility of the overarching theory" (p. 941); what matters is leading "to productive research programmes" (p. 941).
- **Direction:** challenges.
- **So what:** By Gaver's own Lakatos test, one of the most productive readings in the course is an empirical method paper (say "216 studies reviewed," not "216 studies descended from this paper"), so fertility does not separate design theory from the scientific model he contrasts it with. Fertility shows a programme is alive; it does not show which form of knowledge produced it.
- **Slide line:** Wobbrock et al. 2009: by 2020, a review of 216 elicitation studies. Gaver's fertility test (p. 941) fits empirical methods too.

### M3. Designer judgment: bias or embodied theory?
- **Reading:** designer-made gestures result in "somewhat arbitrary gesture sets whose members may be chosen out of concern for reliable recognition [19]" (p. 1083); of the authors' own gestures that no participant tried: "the authors are either thinking creatively or are hopelessly lost!" (p. 1090)
- **Gaver:** an artefact is a "theory nexus": "the choices made by designers reveal both the issues they think are important, and their beliefs about the right way to address those issues" (p. 944).
- **Direction:** challenges.
- **So what:** Gaver treats designer choices as theory embodied in the artefact; Wobbrock and colleagues treat the same choices as a bias to be corrected by users. The paper shows that the beliefs a "theory nexus" carries can be checked against people, which is an evaluation Gaver's annotation account does not mention.
- **Slide line:** Wobbrock et al.: designer gestures "somewhat arbitrary" (p. 1083). Gaver: designer choices are embodied theory (p. 944).

## 6. Ishii and Ullmer 1997, "Tangible Bits"

### T1. One metaphor annotating three platforms
- **Reading:** "we would like to discuss one user interface metaphor that can be applied seamlessly across the metaDESK, ambientROOM, and transBOARD platforms. We found the metaphor of light, shadow, and optics in general to be particularly compelling for interfaces spanning virtual and physical space." (p. 240)
- **Gaver:** an annotated portfolio balances "descriptions of specific, detailed examples of design practice, and articulations of the issues, values and themes which characterise the relations among the collection" (p. 944); portfolios articulate "the ideas and issues that join and differentiate them" (p. 945).
- **Direction:** instance.
- **So what:** The "Optical metaphors" section is an annotation in Gaver's exact sense: a theme that joins three artefacts without abstracting them away. We argue this is the clearest annotated portfolio in the course, published fifteen years before Gaver named the form, which supports his proposal and undercuts any reading that it is new.
- **Slide line:** Tangible Bits: one optical metaphor "across the metaDESK, ambientROOM, and transBOARD" (p. 240). An annotated portfolio (p. 944).

### T2. A view, not a solution
- **Reading:** "This paper is not intended to propose a solution to any one single problem. Rather, we will propose a new view of interface and raise a set of new research questions to go beyond GUI." (p. 234). Wobbrock and Kientz list it as their "Envisionment" example (p. 41).
- **Gaver:** manifestos' "primary function is to build an account of a practice to be pursued in the future" (p. 938); what matters is "their ability to inspire new designs" (p. 941).
- **Oliver's commentary** (`week5/tangiblebits.md`, notes): "The paper defined tangible user interfaces as a field and says openly that it proposes no solution, which is why the missing study is a Wish here and cost Zooids a point."
- **Direction:** instance.
- **So what:** Oliver already graded by Gaver's rule: a vision earned 5/5 without a study, while a platform paper lost a point for the missing study. Gaver gives that intuition a principle, judge manifestos by fertility, and the class can test whether the double standard is fair.
- **Slide line:** Ishii and Ullmer: "not intended to propose a solution" (p. 234). A manifesto, judged by fertility (p. 941).

### T3. Ambiguity designed out, ambiguity designed in
- **Reading:** "To resolve this ambiguity, we designed a rotation-constraint instrument made of two cylinders mechanically coupled by a sliding bar ... By building in these physical constraints, we resolve the question of ambiguity in this particular case." (p. 238)
- **Gaver:** quotes his own claim for ambiguity as a resource: "By impelling people to interpret situations for themselves, [ambiguity] encourages them to start grappling conceptually with systems and their contexts" ([14], quoted p. 940); claims of the form "designing for X" carry "an implicit sometimes" (p. 940).
- **Oliver's commentary** (`week5/tangiblebits.md`): "Here the object is physical, so the designer can build the expectation into the object, and nobody needs to be asked."
- **Direction:** challenges, and illustrates Gaver's unfalsifiability point.
- **So what:** Two respected design positions point in opposite directions, remove ambiguity and exploit it, and each is "sometimes" right, which is exactly why Gaver says such claims cannot be refuted or made to converge. Ishii and Ullmer's own hedge, "in this particular case," is Stolterman's "ultimate particular" (p. 944) in practice.
- **Slide line:** Ishii: remove ambiguity "in this particular case" (p. 238). Gaver: ambiguity as resource (p. 940). Both "sometimes" right.

### T4. Progress by parting ways
- **Reading:** "Our work has been stimulated by Weiser's vision, but it is also marked by important differences." "Thus, our vision is not about making "computers" ubiquitous per se, but rather about awakening richly-afforded physical objects, instruments, surfaces, and spaces to computational mediation" (p. 236). The paper also credits precedents: "Interactions with many media artists and product designers have also influenced the development of our vision." (p. 235)
- **Gaver:** development in generative fields takes "the form of reactions, reconsiderations and fresh beginnings" (p. 943); "We may build on one another's results, but we can also usefully subvert them, suggest alternatives, or establish entirely new constructions" (p. 946); "designers often turn to existing examples of design" (p. 943).
- **Oliver's commentary** (`week5/tangiblebits.md`): "Read in 2026, the Tab, Pad and Board are the phone, the tablet and the wall display, so the industry took the path the authors set aside".
- **Direction:** instance (supports the "many worlds" reading).
- **So what:** Tangible Bits advances by reacting to Weiser rather than converging with him, and both lines produced lasting work, which is Gaver's picture of non-convergent progress. Oliver's observation adds that the market picked one world, a reminder that proliferation in research coexists with selection outside it.
- **Slide line:** Ishii and Ullmer part ways with Weiser (p. 236): Gaver's "reactions, reconsiderations and fresh beginnings" (p. 943).

## 7. Le Goc et al. 2016, "Zooids: Building Blocks for Swarm User Interfaces"

### Z1. A programme Lakatos would recognise
- **Reading:** "Swarm user interfaces can be seen as a coarse-grained version of Sutherland's and Ishii's futuristic visions of user interfaces based on programmable matter." (p. 98); the paper hopes to "bring us closer to Sutherland and Ishii's visions of the ultimate user interface" (p. 105).
- **Gaver:** confirmatory examples are "testimony to the fertility of the overarching theory" (p. 941); a Lakatos programme has a "hard core" and is "a dynamic machine for generating new knowledge" (p. 939, 940).
- **Oliver's commentary** (`week5/zooids.md`): "I read Zooids as foundational research for the dynamic formation of objects we can actually touch."
- **Direction:** instance.
- **So what:** Within the course reading list, Ishii's vision passes Gaver's Lakatos test: nineteen years later a UIST paper places itself inside that programme and calls itself a step toward it. This is the best in-course example of what Gaver means by fertility, and it pairs with M2 to show that fertility is common to both design and empirical lines.
- **Slide line:** Zooids: "a coarse-grained version of Sutherland's and Ishii's ... visions" (p. 98). Fertility, Lakatos style (p. 941).

### Z2. The design space as annotation
- **Reading:** "Figure 8 gives an overview of the design space of Swarm Interfaces. They can be organized into an interaction aspect ..., a display aspect, and an environment aspect" (p. 103; caption: "Design Space explored with Zooids").
- **Gaver:** designs create "a design space around themselves" (p. 944); "if they occupy a point in a design space, they do not highlight the salient or fruitful dimensions of variation that space offers" (p. 944).
- **Direction:** instance, with a caveat.
- **So what:** The design-space section names the dimensions that a set of demonstrations varies along, which is the job Gaver gives annotation. But it is presented as a framework meant to generalise beyond the authors' examples, so it sits between Gaver's annotation and the critics' "extensible" theory (p. 937) and shows the boundary is blurrier than the essay suggests.
- **Slide line:** Zooids' Figure 8 maps the design space (p. 103). Annotation or framework? Gaver's line blurs (p. 944).

### Z3. Demonstrator or designed artefact?
- **Reading:** "Although we have not implemented specific applications, we have begun to experiment with in-the-wild scenarios" (p. 101); "It is also important to better understand the benefits and drawbacks of swarm user interfaces compared to traditional GUIs." (p. 105)
- **Gaver:** "Theories may be provisional, but designed artefacts (as opposed to demonstrators, for example) are not." (p. 944)
- **Oliver's commentary** (`week5/zooids.md`): "The concept I liked most is zooids as physical pixels, yet at their current size I see no real application for it."
- **Direction:** challenges the scope of Gaver's account.
- **So what:** Gaver's claim that artefacts are "the definite facts of research through design" (p. 945) holds for finished designs, and he explicitly sets demonstrators aside. Most HCI artefacts in this course, Zooids included, are platforms or demonstrators, so we argue the essay's account covers less of CHI than it seems to, and Oliver's "what is it for?" question is one Gaver's framework does not answer.
- **Slide line:** Gaver exempts "demonstrators" (p. 944). Zooids is one: "we have not implemented specific applications" (p. 101).

## 8. Breazeal 2003, "Toward sociable robots"

### B1. A generative artefact with stated criteria
- **Reading:** "One could imagine devising a sort of test for sociability, where the behavior of both the human and the robot are evaluated in order to determine success." (p. 169). The evaluation uses three criteria: "These are inherently subjective, yet quantifiable, measures that evaluate the quality and ease of interaction between human and robot." (p. 171). Four naive subjects (p. 171); clean turns averaged 82.5 percent (Table 3, p. 172).
- **Gaver:** the critics want "clear expectations and standards" ([9], quoted p. 937); "tests which are simultaneously unambiguous and ecologically valid are inconceivable" (p. 940).
- **Oliver's commentary** (`week6/Toward Sociable Robots.md`): "It would have been great to see their actual data in the paper to understand what simple fundamentals they used to really achieve this behaviour and how it could evolve."
- **Direction:** challenges.
- **So what:** Kismet is a designed creature built to explore, yet Breazeal states criteria in advance and measures interaction quality, so generative work and declared standards can coexist. Oliver's wish for more data puts him with the critics on this paper, which makes a good discussion hook: would an annotated portfolio of Kismet have satisfied him?
- **Slide line:** Breazeal imagines "a sort of test for sociability" (p. 169). Generative artefact, stated criteria. Gaver's critics, answered?

### B2. A body of work around one artefact
- **Reading:** "Taking this body of work as a whole, we argue that endowing a robot with social skills and capabilities has benefits far beyond the interface value for the person who interacts with it." "It is our hope that our experience in this case study (among those described in our other works) will be helpful to others in future efforts to design and evaluate sociable robots." (p. 174)
- **Gaver:** "As artefacts are to theory, from this perspective, design portfolios are to research programmes." (p. 944)
- **Direction:** instance, with a twist.
- **So what:** Breazeal rests her claim on a body of work, not on the nine-page case study, which matches Gaver's link between portfolios and programmes. The twist is that her portfolio is many studies of one artefact rather than many artefacts, which raises a question the essay does not address: is a single richly documented "ultimate particular" (p. 944) a portfolio?
- **Slide line:** Breazeal: "Taking this body of work as a whole" (p. 174). Portfolios as research programmes (p. 944).

### B3. Borrowed theory used as a rule of thumb
- **Reading:** "Well studied by discourse theorists, humans employ a variety of para-linguistic social cues, called envelope displays, to regulate the exchange of speaking turns [11]." (p. 170); "Kismet does not exhibit displays according to a rigid schedule, but rather applies them more as a rule-of-thumb." (p. 171)
- **Gaver:** borrowed perspectives "are usually translated for use by designers" (p. 938); Stolterman: "designers can be prepared-for-action but not guided-in-action by detailed prescriptive procedures" (p. 941; the PDF line-breaks "guided-in-action").
- **Direction:** instance.
- **So what:** Kismet's turn-taking takes a theory from discourse studies and turns it into a rule of thumb inside an artefact, which is Gaver's account of borrowed theory and of theory that prepares rather than prescribes. The robot then works as a "theory nexus" (p. 944) for that borrowed theory, which makes it a concrete example for slide 06.1.
- **Slide line:** Kismet applies discourse theory "as a rule-of-thumb" (p. 171). Prepared, not guided, by theory (p. 941).

## 9. Shin et al. 2019, "Slow Robots for Unobtrusive Posture Correction"

### S1. Realistic and controlled, nearly
- **Reading:** "We conducted the second study in a real-life setting for ecological validity and observed posture correction for four hours." (p. 5); paired t-tests showed "significant decreases (t= 3.54, p= 0.002)" in unbalanced posture (p. 7). But: "Although we did not explicitly clarify that the study was about posture correction, all of the participants guessed that the monitor was trying to correct their posture, except P2" (p. 7).
- **Gaver:** "tests which are simultaneously unambiguous and ecologically valid are inconceivable" (p. 940).
- **Oliver's commentary** (`week6/Toward Sociable Robots.md`, Slow Robots): "Such an extensive setup might have given the people a clear hint that this is about ergonomics and how the screen with the robotic arm can support it."
- **Direction:** challenges in part, supports in part.
- **So what:** Shin and colleagues test a design claim in a realistic four-hour setting with a within-subject comparison, so for functional claims Gaver's "inconceivable" looks too strong. Yet nearly every participant guessed the purpose, the confound Oliver predicted, so the test was not fully unambiguous, and Gaver's point survives for claims about meaning rather than behaviour.
- **Slide line:** Shin et al.: tested "for ecological validity" (p. 5); most guessed the purpose (p. 7). Gaver half right (p. 940).

### S2. One demonstrator, one "designing for X"
- **Reading:** "posture correction is a sample application of slow and unobtrusive interaction." (p. 2); "We introduced the concept of slow robots that move below the perception threshold without noise, vibration, and intrusiveness. ... We believe that it also can be applied in other furniture items (e.g. desks, chairs, and kitchen countertops) and other domains" (p. 8)
- **Gaver:** theories "take the form designing for X can lead to successful outcomes" (p. 940); "designed artefacts (as opposed to demonstrators, for example)" (p. 944).
- **Direction:** instance.
- **So what:** The paper's general output is a "designing for X" concept (X is motion below the perception threshold) carried by one demonstrator, and its extension to furniture and surgery is stated as belief, which is Gaver's "aspirational" theory in a CHI paper with statistics. That shows Gaver's genres describe empirical design papers too, not only studio work.
- **Slide line:** Shin et al.: posture is "a sample application" of slow robots (p. 2). Gaver's "designing for X" (p. 940).

### S3. Making turns up what was not predicted
- **Reading:** "In contrast to the smooth motion observed in the speed perception study, here we found that participants mostly corrected their posture with a quick motion, yet without noticeable disruption." (p. 6)
- **Gaver:** "the practice of making is a route to discovery, and that the synthetic nature of design allows for richer and more situated understandings than those produced through more analytic means" (p. 942).
- **Oliver's commentary** (`week6/Toward Sociable Robots.md`, Slow Robots): "It is quite surprising that the people found again a balanced posture without any further instructions."
- **Direction:** supports.
- **So what:** The swift correction was found by deploying the artefact, not predicted by the first study, which is evidence for Gaver's claim that making discovers. It was found inside a controlled study, so making and testing were partners here, not alternatives.
- **Slide line:** Shin et al.: deployment revealed unpredicted "quick" corrections (p. 6). Making as discovery (p. 942).

1. **O1, Olsen: generality as the area between examples.** The most surprising match: a systems-evaluation criterion states Gaver's portfolio logic, and gives him the missing account of how a portfolio could be "extensible" (p. 944, 945).
2. **T1, Tangible Bits: one optical metaphor across three platforms.** The clearest annotated portfolio in the course, in a paper the class read, so the room can picture Gaver's proposal (p. 944).
3. **G1, Greenberg and Buxton: same Popper diagnosis, split cure.** Shows Popper's mirror catches all of HCI evaluation, and separates where Gaver agrees with insiders from where he goes further (p. 940, 941).
4. **K3, Wobbrock and Kientz: both "wary," opposite conclusions.** One word, two directions; it states the debate about definition versus constraint in a single line (p. 937, 946).
5. **M2, Wobbrock, Morris and Wilson: an empirical paper passes the fertility test.** Turns Gaver's own Lakatos criterion into a test of his argument, using Oliver's Week 4 paper (p. 941).
6. **S1, Shin et al.: realistic and controlled, nearly.** The sharpest test of Gaver's "inconceivable" claim, and it builds on Oliver's own Week 6 criticism (p. 940).

First alternate if Oliver wants a Weiser line: **W1** (tabs, pads, boards as a portfolio). If he wants a Week 6 robot line instead of S1: **B1** (a test for sociability, with his wish for data).

## Weakest connections, and why

- **W4 (Weiser, borrowed theory).** Almost any paper borrows theory; the link shows Gaver's taxonomy applies, not that Weiser tells us anything about Gaver's argument.
- **K4 (Wobbrock and Kientz as a yardstick).** A lens for judging the essay rather than a connection of content; better in section 07 (method) than on the connections slide.
- **Z2 (Zooids design space).** Gaver's "design space" is a metaphor (p. 944, "see [12]"), while Zooids' Figure 8 is a classification; equating them risks a word-level match.
- **B3 (Kismet rule of thumb).** Accurate, but the Stolterman link is interpretive and the point is small; useful as an example for section 06 rather than here.
- **S2 (slow robots as "designing for X").** Depends on our reading of the conclusion; Shin and colleagues do not frame their work as research through design.
- **G4 (visions count).** Gaver does not discuss vision papers or Memex; the cultural-probes citation is a good fact, but the conceptual link is loose.
- **M3 (designer judgment as bias).** Fair, but Gaver's "theory nexus" is about what artefacts reveal, not about whether designer choices are correct, so a sharp classmate can say the two papers are not talking about the same thing.
