- **Is this research?**
- Yes · No · Depends what counts

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** Before the paper, an object. This is the Drift Table, built in 2004 by William Gaver and colleagues, then at the Royal College of Art. Gaver wrote this week's paper. It is a coffee table with a small round screen set into the top. The screen shows aerial photographs of England and Wales, and they drift. Put a heavy book on one side and the landscape slides that way. There is no task to complete. Volunteers lived with it in their homes for weeks, and the team wrote up what people did with it. Hands up: is this research? Yes. No. Depends what counts. [pause] Keep your answer in mind, because I will ask again at the end. Now the odd part. The researchers who later asked design research to adopt standards cite this exact table, in their 2007 paper, as "a well known example of critical design in HCI." The critics themselves hold the table up as good work. The fight is about what rules it should be judged by, and who gets to write them. That is the heart of the paper. It takes ten pages because the answer is not obvious, and because the person writing it has a stake. (about 195 words)
- **Transition:** So who is fighting whom?
- **Watch out:**
  - The Drift Table is not mentioned in Gaver's paper. The CHI 2004 paper gives Gaver's affiliation as the Royal College of Art, with Bowers at KTH Stockholm and co-authors at Lancaster and UCL; the Goldsmiths repository lists it under the Interaction Research Studio. Say "Gaver and colleagues," not "the studio."

## Slide 02.1: The fight: "be rigorous" versus "whose rigour?"

**Time:** 2:00 · **Visual:** two speech bubbles facing each other across the slide. Left bubble headed "The critics, 2007 to 2011" with the two quoted phrases, and the names Zimmerman, Forlizzi, Stolterman and the CHI 2011 panel in small type beneath them. Right bubble headed "Gaver, 2012" with two quoted phrases. A thin footer strip with reviewer R4's line. Bottom right, in a different colour, the "why you care" line.

**On the slide**
- **The critics, 2007 to 2011:** design research "lacks clear expectations and standards." Give it protocols, guidelines, and theory with "a level of extensibility and verifiability."
- **Gaver, 2012:** standards risk "self-policing," insofar as HCI is "prone to 'scientism.'"
- A reviewer: the essay addresses "an elephant in the room"
- **Why you care:** "Where is your evaluation?" is this fight in four words.

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** Between 2007 and 2011 a group of design researchers inside HCI, John Zimmerman, Jodi Forlizzi, Erik Stolterman and others, made a case. Design research had arrived at CHI, and in their words it "lacks clear expectations and standards." So let us write down what good design research looks like, they said: protocols, guidelines, criteria. A 2011 CHI panel called "Quality control" asked for "actionable metrics." Gaver grants that standards may be a route to legitimacy: with them, design counts as research like any other. Gaver, who had been doing this work for about twenty years, answers in 2012. He says he has "some sympathy." Then he names two risks. First, standards might become self-policing: the community restricts itself. Second, and we think this is the sharper point. Insofar as HCI is prone to scientism, as he puts it, any standards it adopts may be read in terms of scientific models of research, which he calls inappropriate for design. One of his reviewers wrote that the essay addresses "an elephant in the room." Gaver put that in his acknowledgements. Why should you care? Because you will write a paper with a built thing in it, and a reviewer will ask "where is your evaluation?" That question, in four words, is this fight. The essay is an argument about whether the reviewer is right to ask. (about 215 words)
- **Transition:** His first move is not to defend design. It is to ask which science.
- **Watch out:**
  - The critics did not ask for design to become a science. They asked for standards; Gaver's reading is that HCI's scientism would shape those standards. Keep the two apart (pitfall 2 in v1).
  - Say the hedge "some sympathy" (p. 937). Gaver is not dismissive, and the thesis on slide 11 depends on his concessions.
  - "Scientism" is in scare quotes in the original (p. 938). R4's line is on p. 946.
  - "About twenty years": Gaver's HCI publications go back to the late 1980s (the SonicFinder, 1989, verified in v1 section 01). Say "about."
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: What exactly did the critics propose?* A: Zimmerman, Forlizzi and Evenson 2007 proposed four criteria for evaluating research through design: process (a rationale for the methods, documented so it can be reproduced, with no expectation of the same result), invention (a novel integration, backed by a literature review), relevance (in place of validity), and extensibility (others can build on it). Zimmerman, Stolterman and Forlizzi 2010 asked the community to "develop protocols, descriptions, and guidelines" (quoted p. 937).
  - *Q: Is Gaver attacking Zimmerman personally?* A: No. He says he has "some sympathy with these concerns" (p. 937), and Zimmerman's "Designing for the Self" is one of his examples of a fertile manifesto (p. 941). Zimmerman's group cites Gaver's Drift Table as an exemplar. This is an argument among colleagues.
  - *Q: Who was R4?* A: An anonymous reviewer. Gaver thanks "the anonymous R4, who recognised that this essay addresses, in part, 'an elephant in the room...: the move towards "scientism" in HCI'" (p. 946).
  - *Q: Where else does HCI get called scientistic?* A: Gaver cites Sengers 2006, "Must design become 'scientific'?" (p. 941), so he is not the first; v1's first strength says only that he names it.
- **Sources:** paper p. 937 (critics quoted: [9] "lacks clear expectations and standards," "actionable metrics"; [35] "develop protocols, descriptions, and guidelines"; [33] "extensibility and verifiability"; "some sympathy"; "self-policing"), p. 938 ("prone to 'scientism'"), p. 946 (R4). Zimmerman et al. 2007 criteria: `../slides/01-Background.md`, background item 2.

## Slide 02.2: Gaver's judo move: you want us scientific? Which science?

**Time:** 1:30 · **Visual:** two cards side by side, "Popper" and "Lakatos," each with a one-sentence test in plain words. Between them, a short line: "They disagree about what science is." The quotation across the bottom as the only quote on the slide.

**On the slide**
- Gaver never says design is science. He asks: which science?
- **Popper:** scientific if you could prove it wrong.
- **Lakatos:** scientific if it keeps producing new findings.
- "It is difficult to call for research to be more scientific if what it means to be scientific is under question."
- How the essay works: no study. Two borrowed tests, three readings of one puzzle, one sketched example.

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** Which science? First, how the essay works. It has no study, no data and no system, so the course question "how was the research conducted" has an unusual answer. Gaver borrows two tests from the philosophy of science and runs design theory through both. Then he reads one puzzle three ways, and closes on one sketched example. Those are the next five slides. Karl Popper's test: a theory is scientific if you could, in principle, prove it wrong. Imre Lakatos's test: a research programme is scientific if it keeps generating new findings, new puzzles, new work. Gaver chose these two on purpose because they disagree. That is the judo move. He does not argue that design is scientific. He shows that the philosophers cannot agree what "scientific" means, and then says: it is difficult to call for research to be more scientific if what it means to be scientific is under question. Keep an eye on that move. It is strong, and at the end we will ask whether it proves too much. (about 175 words)
- **Transition:** Test one. Could you prove a design rule wrong?
- **Watch out:**
  - Gaver says the two accounts are not "particularly representative of Philosophy of Science" (p. 941). He picked them to "complicate notions of what makes theories 'scientific'" (p. 938). Say "chosen because they disagree," which is his stated reason.
  - "Proves too much": the worry, ours, is that if no account of science is settled, no field can ever be asked to be rigorous. Gaver's reply is that a field should set its standards "on its own terms" (p. 945). Raise it on slide 11 or in discussion, not here.
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: Who were Popper and Lakatos?* A: Karl Popper (1902 to 1994), born in Vienna, made falsifiability the mark of science; the chapter Gaver quotes, "Science: Conjectures and Refutations," began as a 1953 lecture. Imre Lakatos (1922 to 1974), Hungarian, later at the London School of Economics, answered Popper with "research programmes": a hard core of commitments, a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses, and a test of whether the programme predicts novel facts. Gaver quotes Lakatos's phrases on p. 939 to 940.
  - *Q: Why not Kuhn?* A: Kuhn comes in mainly on the next question, convergence (p. 941 to 942). Gaver uses Popper and Lakatos for "what is a theory," and Kuhn, Latour, and Law and Urry for "why no agreement."
  - *Q: Is this a fair way to argue?* A: We argue: judge it as an argument, not a study. The evidence is the critics' own words, six design theories (two of them Gaver's), and one sketched example. That is the material an essay has.
  - *Q: Where does Gaver himself stand?* A: He says it: "Personally, I tend to think that the nature of design practice and research is distinct from the ideals, and often practice, of science," but whether research through design counts as a science "depends on the account of science one adopts" (both p. 941).
- **Sources:** paper p. 938 ("complicate notions"), p. 939 (Popper's criterion, "the criterion of the scientific status of a theory is its falsifiability"), p. 939 to 940 (Lakatos: "hard core," "protective belt," "predict novel facts"), p. 941 ("not ... particularly representative"; "It is difficult to call for research to be more scientific ..."; "Personally, I tend to think"). Dates for Popper and Lakatos: `../slides/04-Theory.md`, If asked and background.

## Slide 03.1: Test 1, Popper: could you prove a design rule wrong?

**Time:** 2:00 · **Visual:** a scorecard in three panels. Left panel: a design rule as one sentence with the word "sometimes" highlighted. Middle panel, headed "To refute it you would need": two identical system icons differing only by a question mark. Right panel: a large stamp reading "Fails Popper." Beneath, one line in small type with the two-way underspecification.

**On the slide**
- Every design rule hides a word: "X will *sometimes* lead to successful outcomes."
- Gaver's test case is his own 2003 claim: ambiguity in a design makes people engage more deeply.
- To refute it you would need two systems identical except for ambiguity. "Simply tweaking" one misrepresents how designers work.
- **Verdict: fails Popper.** Theory underspecifies design, and design underspecifies theory.

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** Take any design rule. "Make it ambiguous and people will engage more deeply." Gaver points out that every such rule carries an invisible word: sometimes. Ambiguity will sometimes lead to engagement. A sometimes claim cannot be proven wrong, because any failure is just one of the other times. That is the in-principle problem. Then the in-practice problem, and here he picks on his own work, which I think is fair of him. His 2003 paper with Beaver and Benford claimed that ambiguity encourages people to grapple conceptually with a system. In principle, falsifiable. But to test it you would need an ambiguous system and an unambiguous one that are otherwise identical. Simply tweaking a finished design to add ambiguity would not represent how designers actually work. So the test is either unfair or unreal. Verdict: by Popper's rule, design theory fails. Gaver's phrase is that theory underspecifies design: a rule leaves a thousand decisions open. And the reverse holds: design underspecifies theory, because most of what makes a good design work is never in the rule. We argue one thing here. His logical point only covers a bare "sometimes" claim. A stronger claim, "ambiguity works more often than not," could be tested. His practical point, that a fair comparison is nearly impossible, is the one that carries the weight. (about 225 words)
- **Transition:** If you cannot refute it, what is it good for? Test two.
- **Watch out:**
  - The "implicit sometimes" sentence is p. 940. The ambiguity claim is quoted from Gaver, Beaver and Benford, CHI 2003 [14], on p. 940. "Simply tweaking an unambiguous system to be ambiguous" is p. 940. "tests which are simultaneously unambiguous and ecologically valid are inconceivable" is p. 940 and is the line Shin et al. (slide 10) will push against.
  - "Theory is underspecified by design" is on p. 944, in the annotation section, not in the Popper section. The slide pairs the two halves; the notes keep the pages apart.
  - Do not say Gaver thinks design theory is worthless under Popper. He says Popper's mirror is "potentially unflattering" and that the failure "reveals two important characteristics" of design theory (p. 940).
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: Is a "sometimes" claim really untestable? You could test whether X raises the success rate.* A: Fair. Gaver's logical point is about the bare form "X will sometimes lead to successful outcomes" (p. 940). A claim that X works more often than not-X is stronger and could be tested. His answer is the practical argument: "Underspecification makes falsification difficult in practice as well as in principle" (p. 940).
  - *Q: What were the five kinds of design theory he sorts?* A: Articulations of practice (Djajadiningrat and colleagues on embodied movement), borrowed theory (affordances becoming "irresistables," as the paper's text spells it), manifestos (Reflective Design, ludic design), frameworks (Forlizzi's Product Ecology) and meta-theory, including this essay (p. 938 to 939). Version 1 of the deck has a full slide on these.
  - *Q: Does Popper himself say science works by refutation alone?* A: Gaver notes Popper's account "is widely criticised both in Philosophy of Science and by practicing scientists" (p. 940), and quotes Kuhn: when a puzzle fails it "is considered the fault of the scientist using the theory, not the fault of the theory itself" (p. 939).
  - *Q: What is in the ambiguity paper?* A: Three kinds of ambiguity: of information (in the artefact), of context (in the cultural discourses used to read it) and of relationship (in the person's own stance). CHI 2003, pp. 233 to 240.
- **Sources:** paper p. 939 (Popper's criterion; Kuhn quotation), p. 940 ("implicit sometimes"; ambiguity claim; "Simply tweaking"; "inconceivable"; "unflattering mirror"; "reveals two important characteristics"), p. 944 ("theory is underspecified by design"). Ambiguity paper contents: `../slides/04-Theory.md`, If asked.

## Slide 03.2: Test 2, Lakatos: does it breed new work?

**Time:** 1:30 · **Visual:** three small cards, "Reflective Design," "Supple Interfaces," "Designing for the Self," each with "refutable" struck through and "inspires designs" ticked. A stamp reading "Passes Lakatos." Beneath, the three adjectives in large type. Bottom line in our colour: the pushback.

**On the slide**
- Reflective Design, Supple Interfaces, Designing for the Self: none can be refuted. What counts is whether they inspire new designs.
- What Popper would reject as "'merely' confirmatory" is, seen Lakatos's way, "testimony to the fertility of the overarching theory."
- **Verdict: passes Lakatos.** Design theory tends to be "provisional, contingent, and aspirational": theory that is "sometimes right."
- We argue: "productive" is also a standard. Who judges it?

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** Lakatos asks a different question. Not "can you refute it" but "does it keep producing?" Gaver lines up three design manifestos: Sengers's Reflective Design, Isbister and Höök's Supple Interfaces, and Zimmerman's Designing for the Self. We would add his own ludic design, which fits the same pattern. None is falsifiable. What matters, he says, is their ability to inspire new designs. Under Popper, the designs that follow a manifesto are merely confirmatory, weak as evidence. Read Lakatos's way, Gaver says, the same designs are testimony to the fertility of the theory. So the inability to refute design theory is not a flaw, as long as the theory leads to productive work. Both tests together give Gaver his three adjectives: design theory tends to be provisional, contingent and aspirational. It is theory that is sometimes right. He hedges: these two philosophers are not representative of the field, and whether design is a science depends on which account you pick. We argue one thing back. "Productive" is itself a standard. Gaver names Lakatos's criterion, the relative productivity of competing programmes, and never says who applies it in design, or when. Hold that thought for the end. (about 190 words)
- **Transition:** Two tests, two verdicts. Now the puzzle the critics built their case on.
- **Watch out:**
  - The three manifestos are named together on p. 941: "'Reflective Design' [30] or 'Supple Interfaces' [16] or 'Designing for the Self' [36]." Ludic design is Gaver's own manifesto on p. 938 [13]; the Say adds it as a fourth that fits the same pattern. Designing for the Self is by Zimmerman, one of the critics, so Gaver credits a critic's manifesto with fertility.
  - "theories that are sometimes right" is p. 941 (top of the page, continuing from p. 940). "provisional, contingent, and aspirational" is p. 937 (abstract) and p. 941; p. 938 and p. 945 have it without the serial comma.
  - Gaver's hedges: "not ... particularly representative" and "depends on the account of science one adopts," both p. 941. Say them; they matter for slide 11.
  - "relative productivity of competing programmes" is Gaver's wording for Lakatos's criterion, p. 941. The limitation is that nobody is named to apply it, not that it is absent.
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: What does Lakatos's test look like in science?* A: A programme is progressive if it predicts "novel facts" that rivals did not (quoted p. 940), and degenerating if it only patches itself after the fact. Gaver does not use "degenerating"; v1 took it from the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy.
  - *Q: Does "sometimes right" theory deserve the name theory?* A: Gaver's position is yes, and he cites Stolterman: designers "can be prepared-for-action but not guided-in-action by detailed prescriptive procedures" (p. 941). The discussion anchor "is sometimes-right theory worth publishing" takes this up.
  - *Q: Could an empirical programme pass the same test?* A: Yes, and slide 10 uses one: the gesture-elicitation method used by Wobbrock, Morris and Wilson 2009 dates from 2005, and a 2020 review traces it through 216 studies. Fertility does not separate design from science; it only rescues design theory from Popper.
- **Sources:** paper p. 937 and p. 941 ("provisional, contingent, and aspirational"; p. 938 and p. 945 without the serial comma), p. 940 ("predict novel facts"), p. 941 (three manifestos; "testimony to the fertility of the overarching theory"; "theories that are sometimes right"; Stolterman quotation; "relative productivity"; both hedges). Ludic design [13] on p. 938.

## Slide 04.1: Why can't designers agree? Pick a diagnosis.

**Time:** 2:30 · **Visual:** three cards across the slide, A, B, C, each with a two-word name in large type, one plain sentence, the thinker in small type, and a coloured tag at the bottom: "Fix: standardise," "Fix: nothing," "Fix: let it multiply." Under the cards, the iPad sentence as the one full-sentence quotation. The three "Fix:" tags are built after the vote is counted, since they give the answer away. Footer in large type: "Vote: which is HCI as you have met it?"

**On the slide**
- Dozens of manifestos, no agreed methods. Why?
- **A. Baby science.** No shared paradigm yet; it "must founder." Fix: standardise.
- **B. Messier up close.** Science may be just as contested day to day; "most of us" seem to share core values. Fix: nothing.
- **C. Makes worlds, not maps.** After the iPad, you design "for a different world." Fix: let it multiply.
- **Vote: which is design research in HCI, as you have met it?**

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** Here is the observation Gaver starts from, and the one the critics' case rests on. There must be dozens of design manifestos, each with its own goals, methods and showcase projects, and no agreement on how to evaluate any of them. Why? Gaver offers three diagnoses, and I want you to pick one. A: design research is a baby science. Kuhn's pre-paradigm stage: everyone argues from first principles, findings do not add up, and in Gaver's words such research must founder. If you believe A, the critics are right: write standards, grow up. B: it only looks messy because you are standing close. Latour's point: real science is full of controversy day to day. Gaver adds that the unity we see may come from popular accounts and hindsight. He also says most of us seem to share values: contact with the people we design for, a wide exploration of options, craft, and making as a route to discovery. If you believe B, there is no problem to fix. C: design does not describe one world, it makes new ones. His example: once the iPad exists, everybody designing tablets is designing for a different world. Science presumes one world to be right about, and that, Gaver says, seems to drive convergence. Design has as many worlds as designers build. If you believe C, calls for shared standards are, in his phrase, potentially repressive acts of ontological politics. Which of these is design research in HCI, as you have met it? One hand each. Hands up for A. For B. For C. Notice: only A supports the critics. Gaver leans to B and C. And the person next to you may have just disagreed with you about what field you are in. (about 280 words)
- **Transition:** So if the field will not converge, what is theory for?
- **Watch out:**
  - "Baby science," "messier up close" and "makes worlds, not maps" are our labels. Gaver's are "pre-paradigmatic" (p. 941), the Latour reading (p. 942), and the "performative" or many-worlds reading from Law and Urry (p. 943).
  - "must founder" is Gaver's wording (p. 942), stronger than Kuhn's own; say "in Gaver's words."
  - The "most of us" list (p. 942) is hedged twice: "most of us ... seem to share," and "Perhaps we do not all agree with all of these assumptions all of the time." Say at least the first hedge.
  - The iPad quotation is p. 943: "anybody seeking to research or develop tablet computers ... is designing for a different world, one in which the iPad exists."
  - "potentially repressive acts of ontological politics" is p. 943, and it is Gaver's phrase, hedged with "potentially."
  - Gaver's own verdict is on p. 938: one reading implies working toward shared understandings, "the other two imply either that there is no problem to solve or that progress in this area will be marked by proliferation rather than agreement." Say "leans," not "chooses."
  - Count the vote and note the split; discussion anchor 3 uses it. If the room is mostly A, ask a B or C voter to defend before moving on; if mostly C, ask what their own project assumes about the world.
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: What does Kuhn actually say about pre-paradigm fields?* A: That early inquiry is marked by competing schools, each with its own view of the field, and that progress comes once one paradigm wins. Gaver summarises this on p. 941 to 942 and adds that without agreed standards of importance "there is little sense of priority or order to the findings that are amassed." Kuhn's two senses of paradigm, an exemplar and a set of shared understandings, are both on p. 941.
  - *Q: Isn't B just saying "science is messy too"? How does that answer the critics?* A: It answers them in two ways. If scientific discourse "may well exhibit just as much uncertainty, interpretation and debate as does research through design" (p. 942), then disagreement is not proof of immaturity. And if designers already share the values on the list, there may be more consensus than the call for standards assumes.
  - *Q: Where does the "many worlds" idea come from?* A: Law and Urry 2004, "Enacting the social," quoted on p. 943: "different research practices might be making multiple worlds, and that such worlds might be equally valid, equally true, but simply unlike one another." Osborne and Rose 1999 on public opinion research is the second source: the social sciences create the phenomena they study.
  - *Q: Which reading does Gaver believe?* A: He does not pick one outright. On p. 938 he says the first implies the community should work toward shared understandings and the other two imply no problem or proliferation. His closing advice, to be "wary of impulses towards convergence and standardisation" (p. 937), fits B and C.
  - *Q: Can natural science ever be "many worlds"?* A: Gaver says natural science works under "a strong presumption that the object of study is a single, unitary world that pre-exists and is independent of its observers" (p. 943), and that this belief "seems naturally to lead to convergence."

## Slide 05.2: What that flips: the object is the fact, theory is the caption

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** Normally in HCI the theory comes first and the system illustrates it. Gaver flips that. The artefacts, he says, are the definite facts of research through design.
