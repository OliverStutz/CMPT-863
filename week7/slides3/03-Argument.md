## Slide 9: Which science? Gaver runs design theory through two tests

**Time:** 1:30 · **Visual:** a two-column scorecard under the headline. Left column headed "Popper" in our colour, right column headed "Lakatos" in the second colour; each has its test as a question in large type, one line on design theory, and a verdict stamp ("Fails", "Passes"). Across the bottom, the result line, then the pull quote in italic with its attribution in small type.
**Images:** none
**Build:** the headline alone, then [click] the Popper column, [click] the Lakatos column, [click] the result line and the pull quote.

**On the slide**
- **Popper:** could you prove it wrong? Rules hide an "implicit sometimes". **Fails.**
- **Lakatos:** does it breed new work? Manifestos inspire designs. **Passes.**
- Result: design theory "tends to be provisional, contingent and aspirational"
- Quote: "It is difficult to call for research to be more scientific if what it means to be scientific is under question" (Gaver, p. 941)

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** His first move is what we call a judo move. He never argues that design is a science. He asks: which science? He borrows two tests that disagree. [click] Karl Popper: a theory is scientific if you could, in principle, prove it wrong. Design theory fails. Every design rule hides what Gaver calls an implicit sometimes. Ambiguity will sometimes deepen engagement. No failure disproves "sometimes," because the next attempt might work. And a fair test would need two systems identical except for ambiguity. Simply tweaking one, he says, would not represent how designers work. [click] Imre Lakatos: a research programme is scientific if it keeps producing, if it predicts novel facts. Here design theory passes. Reflective Design cannot be refuted, but it inspires new designs. Gaver writes, from this Lakatosian view, that what Popper would reject as merely confirmatory evidence is testimony to the fertility of the theory. [click] So design theory tends to be provisional, contingent and aspirational: theories that are sometimes right. Hence his line: it is difficult to call for research to be more scientific if what it means to be scientific is under question. (about 187 words)
- **Transition:** If the philosophers of science cannot agree, why should designers? Gaver turns to the fact the critics started from: dozens of manifestos.
- **Watch out:**
  - "Judo move" is our label, not Gaver's. Say it as ours.
  - Gaver chose the two accounts "to complicate notions of what makes theories 'scientific'" (p. 938) and says "I am not presenting Popper's and Lakatos' accounts as particularly representative of Philosophy of Science" (p. 941). Say "chosen because they disagree," which is his stated reason.
  - Popper's criterion as Gaver quotes it: "the criterion of the scientific status of a theory is its falsifiability, or refutability, or testability" (p. 939). Gaver's verdict is conditional: "Research through design is clearly unscientific if Popper's [24] criterion of falsifiablity is accepted" (p. 940, the paper's spelling). Never say "Gaver says design is unscientific."
  - "there is always an implicit sometimes in statements about how to design successfully" is p. 940. The ambiguity claim is quoted from Gaver, Beaver and Benford 2003 [14] on p. 940; "Simply tweaking an unambiguous system to be ambiguous" is p. 940. On the slide, the full stop sits outside the quotation marks to keep the quotation exact.
  - The Lakatos sentence is Gaver's own, written from a Lakatosian view (P5): "What Popper would reject as 'merely' confirmatory evidence is, from this point of view, testimony to the fertility of the overarching theory" (p. 941). Never "Lakatos says." "predict novel facts" is Lakatos quoted by Gaver (p. 940).
  - The slide's result line is p. 938, which has the hedge "tends to be" and no serial comma. P. 941 says "it is provisional, contingent, and aspirational" (serial comma); p. 937 says "likely to produce theories that are provisional, contingent, and aspirational." Keep "tends to be" on the slide and in the Say (P15).
  - "theories that are sometimes right" runs from p. 940 to the top of p. 941.
  - Let the pull quote sit for a beat before the transition. The "who judges productive?" pushback belongs to slide 15; do not raise it here.
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: Where does Gaver himself stand?* A: "Personally, I tend to think that the nature of design practice and research is distinct from the ideals, and often practice, of science," but whether research through design "can be considered a science depends on the account of science one adopts" (both p. 941).
  - *Q: Is a "sometimes" claim really untestable? You could test whether X raises the success rate.* A: Fair. His logical point covers the bare form "X will sometimes lead to successful outcomes" (p. 940). A claim that X works more often than not X is stronger and testable. His answer is practical: "Underspecification makes falsification difficult in practice as well as in principle" (p. 940).
  - *Q: Who were Popper and Lakatos?* A: Karl Popper (1902 to 1994), born in Vienna, made falsifiability the mark of science; the chapter Gaver quotes began as a 1953 lecture. Imre Lakatos (1922 to 1974), at the London School of Economics, answered with research programmes: a "'hard core'", a "'protective belt'" of auxiliary hypotheses, and a "'powerful problem-solving mechanism'" (p. 939). His test is whether a programme predicts novel facts (p. 940); Neptune is Gaver's example (p. 940).
  - *Q: Lakatos asks for novel facts. What novel facts has design predicted?* A: The strongest challenge. Gaver loosens the test: on p. 940 it is "predict novel facts"; on p. 941 what is at stake is a theory's "ability to inspire new designs." He does not argue the two are equivalent; his hedge on p. 941 lets him make the move.
  - *Q: Does Gaver name a criterion for judging programmes?* A: Yes, Lakatos's: "the relative productivity of competing programmes" (p. 941), and theory is fine "as long as that theory can lead to productive research programmes" (p. 941). Who applies it in design is not said (P13); slide 15 takes this up.
  - *Q: Is the slide's pull quote his whole point?* A: It ends "(see [29])", which is Sengers 2006 on whether design must become "scientific" (p. 941). Gaver adds that the disagreements about science subvert "any easy scientism in HCI" (p. 941).

## Slide 11: What Gaver proposes: the object is the fact, theory annotates it

**Time:** 1:30 · **Visual:** two rows on the left two thirds. Top row, labelled "Usual order": THEORY in large type above a small system icon captioned "illustration". Bottom row, labelled "Gaver's order": a large artefact icon with "theory" in small type beside it, captioned "annotation". Right third: one dot labelled "one design: a point", then seven dots inside a loose outline labelled "a portfolio: an area". Pull quote across the bottom.
**Images:** none
**Build:** the two rows, the first three bullets and the pull quote at once; [click] the dots and the portfolio line.

**On the slide**
- Usual order: theory leads, the system illustrates.
- Gaver's order: artefacts are "the definite facts"; theory annotates them.
- An artefact: an "ultimate particular" (Stolterman), a "theory nexus" (Carroll and Kellogg).
- One design: a point in design space. A portfolio "establishes an area".
- Quote: "If artefacts embody theory, however, they do not encode it" (Gaver, p. 944)

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** Here is the flip. In most HCI papers the theory leads and the system illustrates it. Gaver reverses that. The artefacts, he says, are the definite facts of research through design. Theories may be provisional; a finished design is not. He quotes Stolterman: the designed thing is an ultimate particular. And Carroll and Kellogg: an artefact is a theory nexus. Every object carries its designers' choices about values, function, users and form. Then the key sentence, at the bottom of the slide. Artefacts embody theory, but they do not encode it. You cannot read off the object which choices mattered. So theory's job, Gaver says, is annotation: to explain and point to features of ultimate particulars. [click] Then from one to many. A single design occupies a point in design space. A portfolio, several designs by the same or associated designers, establishes an area in that space. Comparing them shows the dimensions a designer's choices range over, and the invariances among them: what varied, and what never did. That is easier shown than said, so let us try it. (about 180 words)
- **Transition:** Gaver's one example is not research through design at all. It is a product designer.
- **Watch out:**
  - "the definite facts of research through design" is p. 945: "It is the artefacts we create that are the definite facts of research through design." "Theories may be provisional, but designed artefacts (as opposed to demonstrators, for example) are not" is p. 944; the parenthetical matters, since many HCI prototypes are demonstrators.
  - "ultimate particular" is Stolterman's [31], quoted by Gaver with "the same dignity and importance as truth in science" ([31] p. 59, on p. 944). Say "Gaver quotes."
  - "theory nexus" is Carroll and Kellogg's, quoted by Gaver on p. 944. The in-text "[3]" points at Collins 1994, and Carroll and Kellogg are missing from the reference list (P9). Mention only if asked. Gaver lists the implicit theories as philosophical, functional, social and aesthetic (p. 944); the Say's "values, function, users and form" glosses his four parenthetical questions.
  - "If artefacts embody theory, however, they do not encode it" is p. 944. The annotation sentence is p. 944: "design theory is best considered a form of annotation, serving to explain and point to features of 'ultimate particulars', the truths of design."
  - The portfolio sentence on p. 944 has a dash pair around "a portfolio" in the original. Quote only "establishes an area in that space" and paraphrase the rest; never reproduce the dashes. "the invariances among them" is p. 944.
  - "The flip" and the "usual order" row are ours. Gaver's own wording for the usual order is "theories predominating, with design examples serving as mere illustrations" (p. 944); use it if challenged.
  - Do not say Gaver rejects theory. Its role "is limited to inspiration and annotation" (p. 945), and "Theoretical writing remains important" (p. 945). Slide 13 says this.
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: What is a "theory nexus"?* A: Carroll and Kellogg, "Artifact as theory-nexus: Hermeneutics meets theory-based design," CHI 1989, pp. 7 to 14: HCI designs embody several distinct psychological claims at once. Gaver widens it: designers' choices "reveal both the issues they think are important, and their beliefs about the right way to address those issues" (p. 944). Verified in `../slides/06-Annotation.md`.
  - *Q: What does "ultimate particular" mean?* A: Stolterman's term for the final designed outcome in its use context; even a mass-produced object is one "in its specific use context," so it is not about uniqueness (Stolterman 2008 p. 59, in `../slides/06-Annotation.md`).
  - *Q: Why do designers look at examples rather than theory?* A: Gaver says designers "often turn to existing examples of design" (p. 943), "both because of the provisional nature of theory, and the definite nature of designed artefacts" (p. 943). The claim that most designers do not engage with HCI theory is secondhand, "[27] cited in [31]" (p. 943).
  - *Q: Is there a concrete example of theory pointing at a design?* A: Yes, on p. 944: to understand "simultaneous control over multiple functions," "it is useful to consider specific designs such as a rotary controller for a microwave oven [6]" (Djajadiningrat, Matthews and Stienstra 2007).
  - *Q: "Design space" in what sense?* A: Gaver adds that designs may be "perhaps more accurately creating a design space around themselves (see [12])" (p. 944), citing his 2011 workbooks paper. Designers can move around a design's point "or even by jumping away from it along understood dimensions" (p. 944).
  - *Q: Where did the portfolio idea come from?* A: Footnote 1: "Many of the ideas here about annotated portfolios have been developed in collaboration with John Bowers" (p. 944).
