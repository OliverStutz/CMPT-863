# 04 Judging the proposal

**Purpose:** Weigh the annotated portfolio fairly: six upsides anchored to the page, six limitations argued as ours with Gaver's best reply to each, then our verdict in one sentence. **Slides:** 14 to 16. **Time:** 4:00. **Speaker:** Oliver. **Previous:** 03 Argument (slide 13, how to make one, and what it is not). **Next:** 05 Afterwards (slide 17, what the paper changed).

## Slide 14: Six things the annotated portfolio gets right

**Time:** 1:30 · **Visual:** six short rows in two columns of three, each a bold label in our colour and a plain line after it. The sixth row carries a small "ours" tag in the second colour. One pull quote across the bottom in large italic type. No figure.
**Images:** none
**Build:** rows appear one per click, the quote with the first row.

**On the slide**
- **Respects the particular:** the object stays the fact.
- **Grounds theory:** ideas tied to real designs.
- **Checkable, he says:** notes can be held against the pieces.
- **Many readings:** one portfolio, several perspectives.
- **How designers work:** they learn from examples.
- **No new method (ours):** a way of presenting.
- Quote: "annotated portfolios respect the 'ultimate particular'" (Gaver, p. 945)

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** First, it respects the particular. Gaver's strongest claim for the portfolio is that it respects the "ultimate particular" of the designed artefact. The object stays the fact, and theory points at it. [click] Second, it keeps provisional theory honest. By yoking ideas to particular designs, the portfolio grounds and specifies them. A note like "unobtrusive" means something when you can see the products it describes. [click] Third, the claim from the last slide: extensibility and verifiability. He does not spell out how. Our reading: you can hold each note against the objects and see whether it fits, and you can add a piece to the area. [click] Fourth, a portfolio can support multiple conceptual perspectives. The same objects can carry different notes, and Gaver calls the field's lack of convergence a virtue here. [click] Fifth, it matches how designers already work. He observes that designers turn to existing examples of design more than to major theories. [click] Sixth, and this one is ours: it costs no new method. It is a way of presenting work a studio already has, not a procedure to learn. (about 187 words)
- **Transition:** Those are the strengths on paper. On paper is the catch, because the essay shows its method exactly once.
- **Watch out:**
  - The full sentence behind the quote and rows 1 and 3 (p. 945): "Most fundamentally, annotated portfolios respect the 'ultimate particular' of the designed artefact, rather than abstracting across instances as pattern languages do, while allowing for the 'extensibility and verifiability' for which some of the HCI design community have called." "Ultimate particular" is Stolterman's term, which Gaver quotes on p. 944; say "Gaver, borrowing Stolterman's term."
  - Row 2 (p. 945): "Juxtaposing designs with annotations supports appreciation of the conceptual dimensions of designs on the one hand, and, by yoking them to particular design manifestations, grounds and specifies theoretical concepts on the other." "Keeps provisional theory honest" is our plain-words gloss of "grounds and specifies."
  - Row 3: "extensibility and verifiability" is the critics' phrase in Gaver's inner quotation marks; the critics' own words are "a level of extensibility and verifiability" (p. 937, quoting [33], P3). Gaver asserts portfolios allow it and does not say how. "Hold each note against the objects, add a piece" is our reading; flag it as ours. Slide 16 returns to this gap.
  - Row 4 (p. 945): "Portfolios can support multiple conceptual perspectives, and similar perspectives can be applied to different portfolios, reflecting the lack of convergence in the field as a virtue."
  - Row 5 (p. 943): "Most practicing designers, however, do not engage with major theoretical approaches in HCI. Instead, they use a more eclectic mix of design techniques and orienting concepts ([27] cited in [31]). In addition, designers often turn to existing examples of design to inform the development of their own ideas." The evidence is second-hand (Rogers 2004, as cited by Stolterman 2008); say "he observes," not "he shows."
  - Row 6 is ours, and it holds only for the form. Gaver supports it: "The notion of annotated portfolios is not a formal one" (p. 944). The artefacts themselves are costly; slide 15's last point takes that up, so do not oversell "costs nothing."
  - Gaver's own limit on the proposal (P8): "I am not proposing here that annotated portfolios subsume all other forms of design theory" (p. 945). If someone says he wants to replace theory, quote it.
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: He says portfolios allow "extensibility and verifiability," and in the same conclusion asks us to "moderate expectations of creating extensible and verifiable theory." Isn't that a contradiction?* A: A sharp catch. Both are on p. 945. Our reading: the first is about checking notes against particular artefacts, the second about general theory that travels without them. The paper does not reconcile the two in so many words.
  - *Q: How exactly would one verify a portfolio?* A: The essay does not say. Löwgren 2013 later suggested rigour for intermediate-level knowledge could come from "horizontal and vertical grounding" (`../slides/10-What-Followed.md`, background). That is Löwgren's proposal, not Gaver's.
  - *Q: Isn't "matches how designers work" just a description? Why is it an upside?* A: Because a form that fits existing practice is more likely to be used. Gaver's version: theory is provisional and artefacts are definite, which is "why" designers turn to examples (p. 943). Whether practice should set the standard is fair game for the discussion.
  - *Q: Isn't the portfolio just Alexander's pattern language with pictures?* A: Gaver says the opposite: portfolios are "in several respects, the converse of Alexander's [1] design patterns." They "maintain the particularity of individual examples" instead of abstracting regularities (p. 945, P15).
- **Sources:** paper p. 937 ("a level of extensibility and verifiability," quoting [33]); p. 943 ("turn to existing examples of design"; "definite nature of designed artefacts"); p. 944 ("ultimate particular," Stolterman; "not a formal one"); p. 945 (the "Most fundamentally" sentence; "yoking"; "multiple conceptual perspectives"; "converse of Alexander's"; "not proposing here"; first conclusion). `../slides/08-Strengths-Limitations.md` slide 08.1; `../slides/10-What-Followed.md` (Löwgren 2013).

## Slide 15: Where we push back

- **We argue:**

- 2. "Productive research programmes": who judges, and when?
- 3. Reviewing, funding, examining: no procedure.
- 4. The "most of us" list already works as criteria.

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** Each of these is our argument, not Gaver's. [click] One. The only portfolio in the essay is a sketch of Rams's work for Braun and Vitsoe. That is product design, so the method is illustrated, not shown on research through design. [click] Two. His test for design theory is whether it leads to productive research programmes. That is a standard. He names Lakatos's criterion but not who applies it in design, or when. [click] Three. Summing up the critics' case, Gaver writes that greater integration would make assessment fairer and more rigorous, then never returns to it. We extend the problem to funding and examining. How does a committee decide? [click] Four. His list of what most of us seem to share, user contact, wide exploration, craft, making as discovery, already works as criteria. Standards, just not metrics. [click] Five. By his own taxonomy, we would call the essay a manifesto with one example, though he calls it meta-theory. [click] Six. Who annotates? Here Rams's principles annotate Rams's products. And built portfolios cost money; this essay was funded by a European Research Council Advanced Investigator Award. **[classmate]** (about 189 words)
- **Transition:** Put those six together with the six upsides and you get our verdict, in one sentence.
- **Watch out:**
  - Every row is "we argue." Never state a motive (Part 1, rule 3). Gaver's best reply to each, in order:
  - Row 1, reply: the caption says portfolios "are not defined by their graphic presentation" (p. 945); a fuller treatment "might well take the form of an illustrated essay" (p. 945); footnote 1 says the ideas were developed with John Bowers (p. 944), and Gaver and Bowers published a worked studio portfolio two months later (slide 17). Our claim is narrow: not demonstrated in this paper. Say "Rams's work," not "commercial products": one of the seven items is a prototype "never produced" (p. 945, P7).
  - Row 2, reply: Lakatos's own answer is the community over time, "the relative productivity of competing programmes as the grounds for scientists' choice amongst them" (p. 941), and the conclusion says such theory "may nonetheless inspire thriving research programmes" (p. 945). So the criterion is named; what is missing is who applies it in design and over what span (P13). If someone says "the community, over time," agree, then ask how a reviewer applies it to one paper this year.
  - Row 3, reply: the essay is about what theory can be, not committee procedure; norms are "political acts to be approached with care" (p. 945); the field should pursue research "on its own terms" (p. 945). The text we lean on is his: "Greater integration would make assessment of new contributions fairer and more rigorous" (p. 941). "Funding" and "examining" are our extension of the critics' problem (P14); neither word appears in the essay. The critics' own phrase was "actionable metrics" (p. 937).
  - Row 4, reply: shared values are not the agreed methods and outputs the critics asked for. Keep both hedges (P6): "most of us working in research through design seem to share a set of common values" and "Perhaps we do not all agree with all of these assumptions all of the time" (p. 942). Gaver draws the conclusion himself: "we already share many of the attributes" (p. 942) "of a research paradigm" (p. 943). So this is his reading, not a gotcha.
  - Row 5, reply: in the same sentence he says the essay "addresses the more specific, content-oriented theories described earlier" (p. 939), so it is commentary on theory, not a programme for practice. Our inference rests on his line that such treatments "can be considered as wide-ranging manifestos" (p. 939); he calls this essay "a contribution to meta-theory" (p. 939). Weakest row; do not lead with it.
  - Row 6, reply (in Gaver's spirit, not in the text): his worry is self-policing that would be "overly restrictive" (pp. 937 to 938), and restriction falls hardest on newcomers doing unconventional work. On who annotates: the figure's notes speak of Rams in the third person and the paper does not say who wrote them (p. 945); a portfolio may be "a collection of designs by the same or associated designers" (p. 944). The grant is on p. 946: "European Research Council's Advanced Investigator Award no. 226528, 'ThirdWaveHCI'." That portfolios favour such labs is our question, never his motive.
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: How big was the grant?* A: CORDIS lists an EU contribution of 2,439,757 euros, April 2009 to December 2014, held at Goldsmiths, with the aim of deploying 50 to 100 batch-produced prototypes (`../slides/10-What-Followed.md`, https://cordis.europa.eu/project/id/226528). That shows the studio's scale; the link to who benefits is ours.
  - *Q: Did the critics have criteria Gaver ignored?* A: Zimmerman, Forlizzi and Evenson 2007 offered four lenses: process, invention, relevance, extensibility (their p. 499, via `../slides/11-Discussion-Bridge.md`). Gaver cites that paper for making "the 'right thing'" (p. 940) but does not discuss the lenses.
  - *Q: Are there limitations you left off?* A: Missing voices: Schön and Pullin are in the reference list and never cited (p. 946); Cross 1982 is absent (`../slides/08-Strengths-Limitations.md`). And he mostly reads the call for standards as a call for one paradigm: "a tempting conclusion, one seemingly behind recent calls for formalism and standardisation [9, 35]" (p. 942), though he does answer standards directly on p. 942 and p. 943.
  - *Q: Did Gaver answer the reviewing question later?* A: Partly. His 2014 chapter says for design "The basic question here is 'does it work?'" (Ways of Knowing in HCI, p. 147, via `../slides/08-Strengths-Limitations.md`). A principle, still not a procedure.
- **Sources:** paper p. 937 ("actionable metrics"; "overly restrictive" across pp. 937 to 938), p. 939 (manifestos; "a contribution to meta-theory"), p. 941 ("productive research programmes"; "relative productivity"; "fairer and more rigorous"), p. 942 ("most of us" list and hedges; "tempting conclusion"), p. 944 (footnote 1; "same or associated designers"), p. 945 (Figure 1 and caption; "illustrated essay"; "political acts"; "own terms"; "thriving research programmes"), p. 946 (grant; references 25 and 28). `../slides2/08-Verdict.md`; `../slides/08-Strengths-Limitations.md` slide 08.2 and background; `../slides/10-What-Followed.md` (CORDIS figures).

## Slide 16: Our verdict, in one sentence

**On the slide**
- **Gaver wins the argument against scientism. He concedes more to his critics than his conclusion admits, and never says how a field without shared criteria decides what to publish, fund and examine.**
- 1. He accepts articulated standards, shared practice.
- 2. "Productive research programmes" names no judge.

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** Gaver wins the argument against scientism. He concedes more to his critics than his conclusion admits, and never says how a field without shared criteria decides what to publish, fund and examine. Set that beside his own three conclusions: moderate expectations of creating extensible and verifiable theory; treat attempts to establish disciplinary norms as political acts to be approached with care; recognise annotated portfolios as a way of valuing the particular nature of design. [click] Three supports, two from the last slide. He accepts clearly articulating the standards one might want to advocate, and a practice most of us seem to share. His test names no judge. And the portfolio is a form, not a criterion; his caption says portfolios are not defined by their presentation. **[classmate]** (about 128 words)
- **Transition:** That is the verdict on the page. What the field did with it is a different story, and Gaver himself wrote the next chapter.
- **Watch out:**
  - The [click] for the three supports comes after Gaver's three conclusions are read out.
  - Gaver's three conclusions, exact (P17): "moderate expectations of creating extensible and verifiable theory" (p. 945); "attempts to establish disciplinary norms of process or outcome are political acts to be approached with care" (p. 945); "the recognition of annotated portfolios as a way of reflecting and valuing the particular nature of design theory and practice" (p. 946). The Say shortens the third; if quoting, use the full wording. His closing line: "consensus can come to look like constraint" (p. 946).
  - The thesis is a candidate for Oliver to accept, sharpen or replace. Say "concedes more than his conclusion admits," never "contradicts himself."
  - Support 1 (p. 941): "what this discussion suggests is the desirability of clearly articulating the methodological and conceptual features of current research through design practice, as well as the standards one might want to advocate for them." Shared practice (p. 942): "most of us working in research through design seem to share a set of common values," with the hedge "Perhaps we do not all agree with all of these assumptions all of the time."
  - Support 2: "productive research programmes" (p. 941). He names Lakatos's criterion, "the relative productivity of competing programmes" (p. 941); what is missing is the judge (P13).
  - Support 3: caption "(note that annotated portfolios are not defined by their graphic presentation)" (p. 945); "The notion of annotated portfolios is not a formal one" (p. 944).
  - "Wins against scientism" means the p. 941 argument is sound: "It is difficult to call for research to be more scientific if what it means to be scientific is under question." It does not mean HCI is scientistic: Gaver writes "insofar as HCI is prone to 'scientism'" (p. 938). He cites Sengers 2006 on the question (p. 941), and R4 says the essay addresses the elephant "in part" (p. 946, P18).
  - "Publish, fund and examine" is our framing (P14). The essay mentions none of the three.
  - The strongest text against us (p. 943): "even the assumption that shared standards are necessary, possible or desirable, are potentially repressive acts of ontological politics." Concede it. Page 941 accepts articulated standards and page 943 doubts shared ones; that gap is what "concedes more" names.
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: Isn't "articulating standards" Gaver's alternative to the science label, not a concession?* A: Agreed on the context: in the left column it follows "(see [29])" and opens with "Instead" (p. 941). But the alternative includes "the standards one might want to advocate," and the critics asked for "protocols, descriptions, and guidelines" (p. 937). Description is common ground. We say the gap is narrower than his tone (`../slides/11-Discussion-Bridge.md`).
  - *Q: Isn't the judo move self-defeating? If nobody can define science, nobody can be asked for rigour.* A: Gaver's answer is that the field should reflect on how to pursue research "on its own terms" (p. 945). Ours: that moves the problem rather than solving it, since "its own terms" still have to be written by someone.
  - *Q: Your position in one line?* A: Gaver is right that design should not be judged as if it were a science; we think he still owes the field a way to choose between papers. Then hand it back: "Who would answer him for us?"
  - *Q: Does he say portfolios help with rigour?* A: Yes, hedged: they "may provide a way to further our ambitions to produce relevant and rigorous theoretical work" (p. 946), echoing the critics' "relevant and rigorous theory" (p. 937, quoting [35]). The echo is our reading.
