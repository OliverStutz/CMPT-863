## Slide 1: What Should We Expect From Research Through Design?

*Time cap 0:30, about 72 words*

Good morning. In 2012 William Gaver, at the Interaction Research Studio at Goldsmiths in London, answered colleagues who wanted research through design to adopt standards. Twenty-five minutes from me, in six parts: where the field came from, what people had tried, what Gaver argues, whether it holds, what became of it, and what it is worth now. I will need your hands four times. We start with a definition and an object.

**Then:** First, what research through design is, and a coffee table.

## Slide 2: What is research through design? Here is an object to argue about.

*Time cap 1:30, about 176 words*

What is research through design? In 1993 Christopher Frayling, at the Royal College of Art, separated research into design, research for design, and research through design. This paper is about the third. Gaver's definition is on the slide. [click] Here is one. The Drift Table, built in 2004 by Gaver and colleagues, then at the Royal College of Art. It is a coffee table with a round screen in the top. The screen shows aerial photographs of England and Wales, and they drift as weight shifts on the table. There is no task. Volunteers lived with it at home for several weeks. One hand each: is this research? Hands if yes. [pause] No. [pause] Depends what counts. [pause] I am writing the counts on a card, and I will ask again at the end. [click] One more fact. In 2007, the researchers who later asked for standards called this table "a well known example of critical design in HCI." Our reading: the table is not in dispute. What counts as research, and by whose rules, is.

**Then:** So how did a coffee table end up at CHI at all? Here is the route design took into HCI.

---

## Slide 3: Design got into HCI between 1993 and 2007.

*Time cap 1:00, about 127 words*

Four dots. In 1993 Frayling gave us the phrase research through design. In 2003, at CHI, Daniel Fallman argued that HCI had emerged as a design-oriented field, and asked what design is. Gaver cites him for exactly that move, characterising HCI as a design discipline. In 2006 Susanne Bødker described a third wave of HCI, moving from the workplace into homes and everyday life. And in 2007 Zimmerman, Forlizzi and Evenson made research through design a named method at CHI. By 2007 design was inside HCI, with a name. Our reading of why that made a mess: HCI's habits came from psychology and computer science, hypothesis, study, measured result. Design brought studio habits: build it, live with it, reflect. Nobody had agreed which habits judge which work.

**Then:** Once design was inside HCI, it did not speak with one voice. It spoke in manifestos.

---

## Slide 4: The mess showed itself as dozens of manifestos.

*Time cap 1:00, about 128 words*

How did the disagreement show itself? Gaver's estimate in 2012: there must be dozens of different manifestos for design, each seeking to establish a research programme. Five examples from the paper. Reflective Design, from Sengers and colleagues: reflection on unconscious values in computing should be a core principle of design. Gaver's own ludic design: an antidote to the idea that technology should give clear, efficient solutions. Supple Interfaces, Isbister and Höök, designing for richer human connections. Designing for the Self, Zimmerman, products that help people become who they want to be. Note that Zimmerman is one of the critics you meet next. And Product Ecology, Forlizzi's framework for products used together. The symptom, in Gaver's words: little agreement about values, methods, standards for evaluation or forms of output.

**Then:** That lack of agreement is what the critics wrote about, in print.

## Slide 5: The reaction, 2007 to 2011: a call for standards.

*Time cap 1:15, about 158 words*

Here is the reaction, in the critics' own words, as Gaver quotes them. [click] It starts in 2007. Zimmerman, Forlizzi and Evenson wrote that there was no agreed standard of what research through design means, nor of what a high quality contribution should be. Their four criteria come on slide 7. [click] In 2008 Zimmerman and Forlizzi asked for theory with "a level of extensibility and verifiability." [click] In 2010 Zimmerman, Stolterman and Forlizzi asked the community to "develop protocols, descriptions, and guidelines." [click] And in 2011 a CHI panel called "Quality control" said research through design "lacks clear expectations and standards," and asked for "actionable metrics." Notice what they ask for: standards, protocols, criteria. They do not ask design to become a science. [click] Why want standards at all? Gaver offers one reading, and hedges it: conformance to standards "may be a route towards disciplinary legitimacy within HCI." That is his guess, hedged, not their stated aim.

**Then:** Before Gaver answers, look at what these standards would have judged: how the work was actually being done.

## Slide 6: Research through design was being done by building things and writing theory around them.

*Time cap 1:00, about 129 words*

How was research through design being done? First, you build something. Gaver says the output is primarily artefacts and systems, sometimes with accounts of how they are used in field tests. From other publications by Gaver and colleagues: the Drift Table was lived with for several weeks, and the Prayer Companion stayed with a convent of nuns for ten months. [click] Second, people wrote theory around the things. Gaver sorts it into five kinds. Articulations of practice, like Djajadiningrat and colleagues on embodied movement, built from reflecting on their own designs. Borrowed theory, like affordances, which Overbeeke and Wensveen turned into irresistables. Manifestos, as on slide four. Frameworks, like Product Ecology. And meta-theory, theory about research through design itself, where Gaver puts Zimmerman and Forlizzi, Stolterman, and this essay.

**Then:** So that is the work. How did people propose to judge it? In 2007, with four lenses.

---

## Slide 7: People proposed to judge it by four criteria, then by protocols and metrics.

*Time cap 1:00, about 103 words*

In 2007 Zimmerman, Forlizzi and Evenson proposed four lenses for judging a research through design contribution. Process: the methods need rigour and a rationale, documented so others could reproduce them, with no expectation of getting the same result. Invention: a new integration of ideas, shown against the literature. Relevance, which they put in place of validity. In their words, instead of validity, the benchmark should be relevance. And extensibility: others can build on the work. [click] Then the push grew. The 2010 paper set out towards a formalisation of the approach. The 2011 panel wanted actionable metrics. These are the proposals Gaver answers.

**Then:** Gaver's response to these proposals begins with a concession.

---

## Slide 8: Gaver objects: standards risk self-policing and scientism.

*Time cap 1:15, about 148 words*

As a long-term practitioner of research through design, he writes, "I have some sympathy with these concerns." [click] Then he names two risks. First, standards "might lead to a form of self-policing that would be overly restrictive" of research he values for challenging status quo thinking. Second, and we think this is the sharper point: insofar as HCI is prone to scientism, standards "may adopt, or be interpreted in terms of, inappropriate 'scientific' models." Listen to the hedges: might, may, insofar. He targets a risk and a reading, not a claim the critics made. [click] One reviewer, R4, wrote that the essay addresses, in part, "an elephant in the room": the move towards scientism in HCI. Gaver prints that in his acknowledgements. Why should you care? This part is ours. "Where is your evaluation?" is this fight in four words. You will hear it about anything you build.

**Then:** How does Gaver argue back? Not with a study: there is no data in this paper. He argues as a long-term practitioner, with philosophy and sociology of science as his tools, and one sketched example.

---

## Slide 9: Which science? Gaver runs design theory through two tests

*Time cap 1:30, about 188 words*

His first move is what we call a judo move. He never argues that design is a science. He asks: which science? He borrows two tests that disagree. [click] Karl Popper: a theory is scientific if you could, in principle, prove it wrong. Design theory fails. Every design rule hides what Gaver calls an implicit sometimes. Ambiguity will sometimes deepen engagement. No failure disproves "sometimes," because the next attempt might work. And a fair test would need two systems identical except for ambiguity. Simply tweaking one, he says, would not represent how designers work. [click] Imre Lakatos: a research programme is scientific if it keeps producing, if it predicts novel facts. Here design theory passes. Reflective Design cannot be refuted, but it inspires new designs. Gaver writes, from this Lakatosian view, that what Popper would reject as merely confirmatory evidence is testimony to the fertility of the theory. [click] So design theory tends to be provisional, contingent and aspirational: theories that are sometimes right. Hence his line: it is difficult to call for research to be more scientific if what it means to be scientific is under question.

**Then:** If the philosophers of science cannot agree, why should designers? Gaver turns to the fact the critics started from: dozens of manifestos.

---

## Slide 10: Why don't designers converge? Three readings, and you vote

*Time cap 2:00, about 242 words*

Remember the dozens of manifestos on slide 4. Why no convergence? Gaver offers three readings. You will pick one. The labels are ours. A: baby science. Kuhn's pre-paradigm stage. Without a shared paradigm, everyone argues from the basics every time, and in Gaver's words such research must founder. B: messier up close. Gaver, drawing on Latour, says science is full of controversy day to day; it looks unified from a distance. And Gaver says most of us seem to share values: contact with the people we design for, exploring a wide space of designs, craft, and making as a route to discovery. He hedges: perhaps we do not all agree all of the time. C: design makes worlds. Once the iPad exists, anybody designing tablets is designing for a different world. Science presumes one world to be right about, and that, Gaver says, seems naturally to lead to convergence. Design builds many. So, from this perspective, he says our criteria, and even the assumption that shared standards are necessary, are potentially repressive acts of ontological politics. Now the vote. One hand each. Which is design research in HCI, as you have met it? Hands up for A. [pause] For B. [pause] For C. [pause] Thank you. [click] Here is what each implies. Only A says standardise, which is the critics' case. B says there is nothing to fix. C says let it multiply. We read Gaver as leaning to B and C.

**Then:** If the field will not converge on one theory, what is theory for? Gaver's answer flips the usual order of theory and object.

## Slide 11: What Gaver proposes: the object is the fact, theory annotates it

*Time cap 1:30, about 179 words*

Here is the flip. In most HCI papers the theory leads and the system illustrates it. Gaver reverses that. The artefacts, he says, are the definite facts of research through design. Theories may be provisional; a finished design is not. He quotes Stolterman: the designed thing is an ultimate particular. And Carroll and Kellogg: an artefact is a theory nexus. Every object carries its designers' choices about values, function, users and form. Then the key sentence, at the bottom of the slide. Artefacts embody theory, but they do not encode it. You cannot read off the object which choices mattered. So theory's job, Gaver says, is annotation: to explain and point to features of ultimate particulars. [click] Then from one to many. A single design occupies a point in design space. A portfolio, several designs by the same or associated designers, establishes an area in that space. Comparing them shows the dimensions a designer's choices range over, and the invariances among them: what varied, and what never did. That is easier shown than said, so let us try it.

**Then:** Gaver's one example is not research through design at all. It is a product designer.

---

## Slide 12: Could you design like Dieter Rams? A test in three steps

*Time cap 2:00, about 217 words*

Dieter Rams designed for Braun and Vitsoe and wrote ten concise principles of good design. He is Gaver's one worked example, so let us test it on ourselves. [click] Step one, the principles alone. Two that Gaver quotes: good design is unobtrusive; good design is thorough down to the last detail. Could you design in the style of Rams from these sentences? Hands if yes. [pause] Gaver grants they may seem too terse to stand on their own. [click] Step two, the products alone. Could you design in his style now? Hands if yes. [pause] You might get the look. You would not know which features matter. [click] Step three, both. This is Figure 1 of the paper: the same photographs, each with a short note pointing at a detail, and four of the ten principles in the middle. Hands if yes. [pause] If more hands went up, that is Gaver's point. [click] This is an annotated portfolio: a set of related designs plus short notes on what joins them, where, in his words, neither dominates, and neither is subservient. In our words: the products ground the words, and the words tell you where to look. And his claim: one can imagine designing in the style of Dieter Rams without the need for a detailed formal theory.

**Then:** So what makes something an annotated portfolio? Not the layout, Gaver says: the balance.

---

## Slide 13: How to make one, and what it is not

*Time cap 1:00, about 112 words*

Gaver says the notion is not a formal one. Specific, detailed examples on one side; the issues, values and themes that join them on the other. The presentation may vary with material, purpose and audience. Figure 1 is one; a website or an essay could be others. Who annotates is left open. In the text, Rams's principles annotate Rams; the figure's notes are by an unnamed hand. [click] What it is not. In several respects, it is the converse of Alexander's design patterns: patterns abstract across examples, portfolios keep each one particular. It does not replace theoretical writing. And yet Gaver claims it allows the extensibility and verifiability the critics asked for.

**Then:** So why take it seriously? Six upsides: five in Gaver's words, one in ours.

---

## Slide 14: Six things the annotated portfolio gets right

*Time cap 1:30, about 184 words*

First, it respects the particular. Gaver's strongest claim for the portfolio is that it respects the "ultimate particular" of the designed artefact. The object stays the fact, and theory points at it. [click] Second, it keeps provisional theory honest. By yoking ideas to particular designs, the portfolio grounds and specifies them. A note like "unobtrusive" means something when you can see the products it describes. [click] Third, the claim from the last slide: extensibility and verifiability. He does not spell out how. Our reading: you can hold each note against the objects and see whether it fits, and you can add a piece to the area. [click] Fourth, a portfolio can support multiple conceptual perspectives. The same objects can carry different notes, and Gaver calls the field's lack of convergence a virtue here. [click] Fifth, it matches how designers already work. He observes that designers turn to existing examples of design more than to major theories. [click] Sixth, and this one is ours: it costs no new method. It is a way of presenting work a studio already has, not a procedure to learn.

**Then:** Those are the strengths on paper. On paper is the catch, because the essay shows its method exactly once.

## Slide 15: Where we push back

*Time cap 1:30, about 187 words*

Each of these is our argument, not Gaver's. [click] One. The only portfolio in the essay is a sketch of Rams's work for Braun and Vitsoe. That is product design, so the method is illustrated, not shown on research through design. [click] Two. His test for design theory is whether it leads to productive research programmes. That is a standard. He names Lakatos's criterion but not who applies it in design, or when. [click] Three. Summing up the critics' case, Gaver writes that greater integration would make assessment fairer and more rigorous, then never returns to it. We extend the problem to funding and examining. How does a committee decide? [click] Four. His list of what most of us seem to share, user contact, wide exploration, craft, making as discovery, already works as criteria. Standards, just not metrics. [click] Five. By his own taxonomy, we would call the essay a manifesto with one example, though he calls it meta-theory. [click] Six. Who annotates? Here Rams's principles annotate Rams's products. And built portfolios cost money; this essay was funded by a European Research Council Advanced Investigator Award. **[classmate]**

**Then:** Put those six together with the six upsides and you get our verdict, in one sentence.

## Slide 16: Our verdict, in one sentence

*Time cap 1:00, about 127 words*

Gaver wins the argument against scientism. He concedes more to his critics than his conclusion admits, and never says how a field without shared criteria decides what to publish, fund and examine. Set that beside his own three conclusions: moderate expectations of creating extensible and verifiable theory; treat attempts to establish disciplinary norms as political acts to be approached with care; recognise annotated portfolios as a way of valuing the particular nature of design. [click] Three supports, two from the last slide. He accepts clearly articulating the standards one might want to advocate, and a practice most of us seem to share. His test names no judge. And the portfolio is a form, not a criterion; his caption says portfolios are not defined by their presentation. **[classmate]**

**Then:** That is the verdict on the page. What the field did with it is a different story, and Gaver himself wrote the next chapter.

## Slide 17: What the paper changed, 2012 to 2016: three lanes

*Time cap 1:30, about 168 words*

Right after CHI, Gaver and John Bowers published a short piece called "Annotated Portfolios," and Bowers argued its logic at DIS that summer. Both come from Gaver's own studio, so that is continuation, not uptake. The independent signs came next. A Research Through Design conference started in 2013 that exhibits the artefacts and discusses them at round tables. DIS added Pictorials in 2014, papers where the images carry the argument. Pierce proposed "thingly publications." We argue the form found venues, not a rule. [click] The second lane, and the lane names are ours, kept moving toward knowledge above single designs: strong concepts from Höök and Löwgren, bridging concepts from Dalsgaard and Dindler, and a 2016 framework for documenting the research through design process. Zimmerman and Forlizzi wrote in 2014 that they had worked to formalize the approach. [click] And in the middle, Löwgren filed annotated portfolios as one kind of intermediate-level knowledge. One 2014 book prints both sides. OpenAlex now counts about eleven hundred citations to the essay.

**Then:** That was the first four years. Fourteen years on, the clearest sign is at CHI itself.

## Slide 18: What is it worth in 2026?

*Time cap 1:30, about 182 words*

CHI 2026. The Design subcommittee now invites work exploring "alternatives to scientistic ways of knowing." That is close to Gaver's diagnosis. But it is a scope statement, not reviewing practice. Design drew 485 submissions and moved on to revise and resubmit at about the overall rate, just under two in five. The door is open; whether reviewing changed, we cannot say. [click] The Research Through Design conference paused after 2019 and returns in 2027 as RTD Roams, in York, Melbourne and Tokyo. Pictorials spread from DIS to TEI and Creativity and Cognition. [click] Now a question of ours. Suh and colleagues note that language models can produce "hundreds and thousands" of outputs, yet current interfaces push users "towards rapid convergence on a limited set of ideas." Davis and colleagues found graphical design-space interfaces supported ideation better than text prompts. Neither paper is about research through design. But Gaver rests his case on the definite nature of designed artefacts. What is one definite object worth when variants are nearly free? [click] And the reverse: built portfolios are expensive. Do they favour funded labs?

**Then:** Some of this was already in our own course readings, starting with Tangible Bits.

## Slide 19: Where this touches what we have read

*Time cap 1:30, about 190 words*

Gaver cites none of our readings, so every link here is ours. Tangible Bits. Ishii and Ullmer join three platforms, the metaDESK, the ambientROOM and the transBOARD, with one optical metaphor, and write about what holds them together. We think that is the clearest annotated portfolio we have read, fifteen years before Gaver named the form. [click] Greenberg and Buxton make Gaver's Popper diagnosis for HCI evaluation: our studies look for confirmation. Their cure keeps refutation for the mainstream. Gaver drops it for design theory. [click] Wobbrock, Morris and Wilson used an elicitation method that dates from 2005. By 2020 a review counted 216 studies using it. By Gaver's fertility test that is a thriving programme, and it is ordinary empirical science. Our point: fertility rescues design theory without setting design apart. [click] Shin and colleagues in week six tested a slow robot for four hours in a realistic setting. Gaver said a test both unambiguous and true to life is inconceivable. Shin got the true-to-life half. But actuation always came on day two, and eleven of twelve participants guessed the purpose. Unambiguous, no. We say Gaver is half right.

**Then:** One last look at the object we started with.
