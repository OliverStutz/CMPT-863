**Time:** 0:45 · **Visual:** five numbered questions in large type down the left two thirds; a narrow right column where a **[classmate]** credit sits beside each question a commentary raised. No figure.
**Images:** none

- 3. **Which reading of non-convergence fits design research in HCI?**
- 4. **Would you accept an annotated portfolio as a CHI paper?** What would you want added?

- **[classmate]**

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** Five tensions, each with more than one good answer. One: whose interest do standards serve? Two: is theory that is sometimes right worth publishing, and who counts fertility? Three: which reading of non-convergence fits design research in HCI as you have met it? Four: would you accept an annotated portfolio as a CHI paper, and what would you ask the authors to add? Five: fourteen years on, who won? Where your commentary raised one, your name is beside it. **[classmate]** We will not do all five; what you raise in the pairs decides which. (about 89 words)
- **Transition:** Here is how we will spend the thirty minutes.
- **Watch out:**
  - These are invitations, not conclusions. Pick anchors from what the room raised in the warm-up and the pairs, not in list order (course brief principle 4, `../slides/11-Discussion-Bridge.md` background). Credit each classmate by name, verbatim from the posted commentary; never paraphrase into a stronger claim.
  - Pages for each anchor: 1, p. 937 ("self-policing" and "disciplinary legitimacy," same page); 2, p. 941 ("theories that are sometimes right"; "productive research programmes"); 3, pp. 941 to 943 (the three section headings); 4, p. 945 ("a serious form of theoretical contribution"; "neither dominates, and neither is subservient"); 5, slides 17 and 18.
  - Each anchor below has a zoom-in probe (one page, one sentence, one artefact) and a zoom-out probe (the field, the course, your own work). Alternate between them; two sentences from you, then a question back. The course brief asks for "a conversation, not an additional lecture."
  - Reserves, if an anchor dies: does the many-worlds argument hold for your own project (p. 943)? And is this essay an annotated portfolio with one item, does it meet its own standard (p. 939, p. 945)?
  - Oliver's own project stays out (decision of 2026-10-19).
- **If asked:**
  - *Q (anchor 1): Isn't it obvious that standards protect newcomers?* A: Best fact for standards: they "may be a route towards disciplinary legitimacy within HCI, helping clarify expectations for research through design both within and outside the design community" (p. 937). Best fact against: they risk "a form of self-policing that would be overly restrictive of a form of research that I value for its ability to continually and creatively challenge status quo thinking" (pp. 937 to 938). Seats: Gaver; Zimmerman and colleagues; a PhD student who needs legible criteria; a reviewer who must say yes or no. **Zoom in:** the critics asked for "actionable metrics" (p. 937); name one metric you would accept for the Drift Table. **Zoom out:** who writes a standard, and against whom is it later used? Does the same rule weigh the same on a studio head and a first-year student? Never state Gaver's motive.
  - *Q (anchor 2): A theory that is only "sometimes right" is just vague. Why publish it?* A: For: the designer's concern "is to sometimes create good ones" (p. 940), and confirming examples are "testimony to the fertility of the overarching theory" (p. 941). Against: Popper, quoted by Gaver, "irrefutability is not a virtue of a theory (as people often think) but a vice" (p. 939). **Zoom in:** name one design idea from this course you would call fertile, and the evidence; Tangible Bits and Zooids are the obvious pair. **Zoom out:** if fertility shows only over years, is a single paper even the right unit? Gaver: "As artefacts are to theory ... design portfolios are to research programmes" (p. 944). How many years would a reviewer need?
  - *Q (anchor 3): Gaver clearly thinks the field is pre-paradigmatic; he says so.* A: He says the description is recognisable, "It is clearly possible to recognise the current state of research through design in this description of affairs," but calls the conclusion "tempting" and offers two alternatives (p. 942). Invisible consensus: "consensus is usually easier to see from a distance than it is to experience from within" (p. 942). Many worlds: the iPad (p. 943). **Zoom in:** which item on the "most of us" list (p. 942) would you strike from your own practice? Compare with the slide 10 vote. **Zoom out:** did any course system, Weiser's or Tangible Bits, make a new world in the iPad sense? Is "all three, for different parts of the field" an answer or a dodge? If someone says it, ask which part.
  - *Q (anchor 4): A portfolio is a gallery, not a paper. No reviewer could reject one.* A: For: Gaver asks for portfolios "as a serious form of theoretical contribution" (p. 945), and venues followed, the RTD conference and DIS Pictorials (slide 17). Against: the critics wanted "actionable metrics" (p. 937), and Zimmerman, Forlizzi and Evenson define extensibility as work "described and documented in a way that the community can leverage the knowledge derived from the work" (CHI 2007, p. 500, via `../slides/11-Discussion-Bridge.md`). **Zoom in:** take Tangible Bits as the submission and apply "neither dominates, and neither is subservient" (p. 945). Does it pass? What one sentence would you add so a reviewer could say no? **Zoom out:** should CHI judge a portfolio by the criteria it uses for a study, or by its own? And who annotates: the designer, or someone else?
  - *Q (anchor 5): Gaver obviously won; there is a whole conference.* A: For Gaver: the RTD conference, Pictorials at three venues, CHI 2026's Design scope naming "alternatives to scientistic ways of knowing," about 1,100 citations (slides 17 and 18). For the critics: strong concepts, bridging concepts, the 2016 documentation framework, and Zimmerman and Forlizzi's "worked to formalize" (slide 17). Gaver never asked portfolios to be the only form: "I am not proposing here that annotated portfolios subsume all other forms of design theory" (p. 945). **Zoom in:** which single fact from slides 17 and 18 would you put on the scoreboard first, and for which side? **Zoom out:** is "who won" the wrong question for an essay that ends "consensus can come to look like constraint" (p. 946)? Did the form win while the warning against convergence faded?

## Slide 22: The next thirty minutes

**Time:** 0:30 · **Visual:** the three steps as three numbered lines in large type at the top; beneath, the five role cards as five small tiles in a row, each with its two roles (the topic is printed on the card handed out); a **[classmate]** strip along the bottom in the second colour.
**Images:** none
**Build:** all at once.

**On the slide**
- 1. Hands: is your project research through design? Two voices per corner.
- 2. Pairs, five minutes, one card: a question, two roles.
- 3. One sentence each: "One thing I now expect from research through design."
- Cards: Gaver v Zimmerman · reviewer v author · student v studio head · Popper v Lakatos · artist v scientist
- **[classmate]** · **[classmate]**

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** The plan. First, hands: is your own course project research through design? Yes, no, or depends what counts. Then two voices from each corner. Second, pairs for five minutes, one card each: a question and two roles, so nobody picks a side first. Third, after the anchors, one sentence from eight to ten of you: one thing I now expect from research through design. (about 64 words)
- **Transition:** None; the discussion begins with the hands.
- **Watch out:**
  - Suggested clock, ours: warm-up 0 to 5 minutes; pairs 5 to 10; pairs report and two or three anchors 10 to 24; closing round 24 to 30. If the room is quiet after the pairs, swap one anchor for the live exercise below. If time runs short, drop an anchor, keep the round: it is the one thing every student does.
  - Warm-up: the "depends what counts" corner is where the discussion lives; ask two people from it what "counts" means for them. These answers are the room's own data for anchors 3 and the many-worlds reserve. Link back to the slide 20 count.
  - Cards (`../progress.md`): Gaver versus Zimmerman on standards; a reviewer versus an author on a portfolio submission; a student versus a studio head on who benefits from no criteria; Popper versus Lakatos on a claim from Tangible Bits (for example, that one optical metaphor "can be applied seamlessly" across three platforms, their p. 240, via `../slides/09-Connections.md`); an artist versus a scientist on many worlds. When a pair reports, call on the card, not the person: "the reviewer and the author: who won?"
  - Grading on the day, the Week 4 pattern plus one (`../progress.md`): cited the paper with a page; cited the talk with a slide; acknowledged the other side's strongest point; applied the argument to their own project. Note who spoke; commentaries and participation are graded after class.
  - Optional live annotated portfolio, six minutes (`../progress.md`): five course artefacts on one slide, Weiser's pads, the Surface gesture set, metaDESK, Zooids, Kismet. Pairs write one dimension on which they vary and one invariant. Harvest three or four. It tests whether annotation across different designers' work does anything, since Gaver's portfolio is one designer's (p. 945) and he defines it as "a collection of designs by the same or associated designers" (p. 944).
  - **[classmate]** strip: three or four short quotations from the posted commentaries, verbatim, with names; fewer if fewer fit.
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: What if nobody speaks after a question?* A: Wait. Count five in your head (Rowe's wait-time research, summarised in Allen and Tanner 2002, via `../slides/11-Discussion-Bridge.md`). If still silent, ask it narrower with a page: "Which item on the p. 942 list would you strike?"
  - *Q: What if one or two voices dominate?* A: "Let's hear from someone who has not spoken yet," or ask the partner from that person's pair. The closing round gives everyone one sentence.
  - *Q: What if the warm-up vote is lopsided?* A: Ask two people from the smallest group for one sentence each. If nobody raises a hand, go straight to the pairs; every pair has to report.
