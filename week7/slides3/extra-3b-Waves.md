## Slide 3b: HCI moved from the lab to the office to the living room. Three waves.

**Time:** 1:15 · **Visual:** one table, three columns, filling the slide. Column heads are the wave names with the rough dates in small type. Rows: Where, Who the user is, What counts, How it is studied. The third column in our colour, the other two in grey. Under the table, one line in small type in the second colour: "Bødker 2006; Harrison, Tatar and Sengers 2007." No quotation on the slide; the Bødker sentence is spoken. No pictures.
**Images:** none
**Build:** columns one and two together; [click] the third column.

**On the slide**

| | First wave, 1980s | Second wave, 1990s | Third wave, 2000s |
|---|---|---|---|
| Where | the lab | the workplace | homes, everyday life, culture |
| The user is | a man-machine coupling | a worker in a group | a person making meaning |

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** A minute of background. HCI is usually told as three waves. First wave, the eighties: one person, one machine, in a lab. Harrison, Tatar and Sengers call the metaphor man-machine coupling, and the goal is fit: fewer errors, faster tasks. Second wave, the nineties: the computer goes to work. The user is a worker in a group, and the questions are about practice and context. This is Bødker's own tradition, Scandinavian participatory design, with unions in the room. [click] Third wave, from about two thousand. In Bødker's words, technology spreads from the workplace to our homes and everyday lives and culture. The user is a person making meaning, and what counts is experience and values, with more than one right reading. Now look at the Drift Table again. No task, no workplace, no error rate. A third-wave object. The first two waves had measures. The third did not. That gap is what the rest of this talk is about. (about 150 words)
- **Transition:** Once HCI was building objects like that, people started asking what design is. Back to the timeline.
- **Watch out:**
  - The dates are rough. Bødker's abstract gives no dates; the decades are the common summary. Say "roughly".
  - Bødker's abstract names only the second wave ("the focus of the second wave on work") and the third ("Technology spreads from the workplace to our homes and everyday lives and culture"). The first-wave column comes from Harrison, Tatar and Sengers, who call the waves "paradigms". Do not attribute the first-wave row to Bødker.
  - Harrison, Tatar and Sengers name the third paradigm "situated perspectives" in the long version we read; the alt.chi 2007 version called it the "phenomenological matrix", and their footnote says the name "seems to distress many reviewers". Use "third wave" and avoid both names unless asked.
  - "Man-machine coupling" is their phrase for the first paradigm, kept as a quotation. Do not modernise it on the slide; say "their words" if it jars.
  - "Living with things, probes" in the third-wave row is our gloss, not from either source. Harrison and colleagues list "participatory design, situated action, ethnography, and value-sensitive design" as approaches the third paradigm unites, and cite Gaver's probes work.
  - Bødker is not cited by Gaver. The only link in the paper is the grant name ThirdWaveHCI (p. 946). Do not say Gaver builds on her.
  - Bødker credited Liam Bannon for the first-to-second shift, "From Human Factors to Human Actors" (1991). Mention only if asked.
  - The table is four rows by three columns at about 60 words. If it feels dense, drop the "Studied by" row and say it instead.
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: Who says there are three waves?* A: Bødker, in her NordiCHI 2006 keynote "When second wave HCI meets third wave challenges", and Harrison, Tatar and Sengers, "The Three Paradigms of HCI", alt.chi 2007, who write that "informal histories of HCI commonly identify two major intellectual waves" and argue that a third is at hand. Harrison and colleagues also say "our goal is not to argue that there are only and exactly three paradigms in HCI."
  - *Q: What are Harrison, Tatar and Sengers' three, exactly?* A: Their Table 1. Paradigm 1, human factors and engineering: "Interaction as man-machine coupling", goal "Optimizing fit between man and machine". Paradigm 2, the cognitive revolution: "Interaction as information communication", goal "Optimizing accuracy and efficiency of information transfer". Paradigm 3, situated perspectives: "Interaction as phenomenologically situated", goal "Support for situated action in the world", with the questions "What are the politics and values at the site of interaction, and how can we support those in design?"
  - *Q: What does Bødker ask in 2006?* A: Whether second-wave theory, participation, context, boundaries, can still contribute once the third wave's challenges arrive. Her abstract lists "multiplicity, context, boundaries, experience and participation". She wants the second wave's ideas kept, not thrown away.
  - *Q: Is there a fourth wave?* A: Proposed, not agreed. Bødker revisited the question in Interactions in 2015, "Third-wave HCI, 10 years later". Ashby and colleagues answered in 2019 with a fourth wave that puts politics, values and ethics first, and Frauenberger proposed "Entanglement HCI" the same year. Our reading: these are more manifestos, which is what Gaver says the field produces instead of converging (p. 941).
  - *Q: Why does this matter for Gaver?* A: Our reading. The first two waves came with measures: fit, errors, task time, efficiency. Third-wave objects do not. The 2007 criteria and the 2010 call for protocols are attempts to give the third wave a measure; Gaver's essay argues the measure should not be borrowed from science. His grant is named ThirdWaveHCI (p. 946), and the Drift Table is a third-wave object.
