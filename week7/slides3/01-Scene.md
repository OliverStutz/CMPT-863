## Slide 2: What is research through design? Here is an object to argue about.

**Time:** 1:30 · **Visual:** left third, a narrow text column: Frayling's three words in small type at the top, Gaver's definition below it as the pull quote. Right two thirds, the Drift Table photograph (a low square table on castors, white top, a round porthole in the centre) with the one-line description under it. Across the bottom, the question in large type in our colour and three pills, Yes, No, Depends what counts. The 2007 line appears last, in small type in the second colour, under the pills.
**Images:** `assets/drift-table-chi2004-fig1.png`
**Build:** definition column first; [click] photograph, description, question and pills; [click] after the count, the 2007 line.

**On the slide**
- Frayling 1993: into, through, for design
- Quote: design practice "brought to bear on situations chosen for their topical and theoretical potential", output "primarily, of artefacts and systems" (Gaver, p. 937)
- The Drift Table, 2004: aerial photos drift as you shift weight.
- **Is this research?** Yes · No · Depends what counts
- 2007 critics: "a well known example of critical design in HCI"

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** What is research through design? In 1993 Christopher Frayling, at the Royal College of Art, separated research into design, research for design, and research through design. This paper is about the third. Gaver's definition is on the slide. [click] Here is one. The Drift Table, built in 2004 by Gaver and colleagues, then at the Royal College of Art. It is a coffee table with a round screen in the top. The screen shows aerial photographs of England and Wales, and they drift as weight shifts on the table. There is no task. Volunteers lived with it at home for several weeks. One hand each: is this research? Hands if yes. [pause] No. [pause] Depends what counts. [pause] I am writing the counts on a card, and I will ask again at the end. [click] One more fact. In 2007, the researchers who later asked for standards called this table "a well known example of critical design in HCI." Our reading: the table is not in dispute. What counts as research, and by whose rules, is. (about 170 words)
- **Transition:** So how did a coffee table end up at CHI at all? Here is the route design took into HCI.
- **Watch out:**
  - Pitfall P1. The Drift Table is not in Gaver's paper. In 2004 Gaver was at the Royal College of Art, not Goldsmiths. Say "Gaver and colleagues", never "the studio", for 2004 work. The photographs are "of England and Wales" (CHI 2004 paper, p. 886).
  - Hands: call each option once, one hand per person, and write the three counts on a card. Slide 20 asks the same question; the change is the room's own data. If time allows, ask one "depends" voter what it depends on.
  - If someone says "no", ask what is missing. The answer (a hypothesis, a control, a measurement) is close to the critics' position on slide 5. If someone says "yes" at once, ask what makes it research rather than product design: Gaver's answer is that the situation was chosen for its "topical and theoretical potential" (p. 937).
  - Frayling's labels vary in secondary sources: "into, through, for" (RCA 2021 conference paper) and "in, through, for" (Biggs 2002). Gaver cites Frayling only as [11] (p. 937) and never uses the three labels himself. Do not claim Gaver's definition is Frayling's.
  - The definition sentence is Gaver's; it cites [4, 11, 34] (p. 937). The quotation crosses a column break in the PDF; the words are exact.
  - "The table is not in dispute" is our reading of the 2007 citation, not a claim that every critic praised it.
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: What did the Drift Table study find?* A: The CHI 2004 paper describes a study of ludic activities, "motivated by curiosity, exploration, and reflection rather than externally-defined tasks", lived with in volunteers' homes for several weeks. The table held "about a terabyte of photography of England and Wales" (CHI 2004, p. 886). (https://research.gold.ac.uk/id/eprint/4721)
  - *Q: Who calls it an exemplar?* A: Zimmerman, Forlizzi and Evenson, CHI 2007, p. 496: "a well known example of critical design in HCI." In 2014 Zimmerman and Forlizzi wrote that it "has been held up as one of the earliest and best examples of RtD published within HCI" (Ways of Knowing in HCI, p. 175).
  - *Q: What were Frayling's three kinds?* A: In the best secondary account we found: research into design is historical, aesthetic or theoretical work written up as a thesis; research through design covers materials research, development work and action research, documented and reported; research for design is work where the thinking is embodied in the artefact, which Frayling called the thorny one. Gaver's definition, with its weight on artefacts, sits between "through" and "for"; that is our reading.
  - *Q: Who is Gaver?* A: A psychologist by training (PhD, UC San Diego, 1988, on everyday listening and auditory icons), then auditory interfaces at Apple, cultural probes at the Royal College of Art in 1999, and in 2012 the Interaction Research Studio at Goldsmiths (p. 937). He writes "as a long-term practitioner of research through design" (p. 937).
- **Sources:** paper p. 937 (definition, [4, 11, 34], affiliation, "long-term practitioner"). `../slides/01-Background.md` slide 01.1, background items 1, 3, 4 and log; `../slides2/01-Hook.md` (Drift Table, photograph, P1). Drift Table: https://research.gold.ac.uk/id/eprint/4721 (CHI 2004, pp. 885 to 886). Zimmerman et al. 2007 p. 496: https://doi.org/10.1145/1240624.1240704. ## Slide 3: Design got into HCI between 1993 and 2007.

**Time:** 1:00 · **Visual:** one horizontal timeline across the middle of the slide with four dots, each with a year in large type and one line under it in small type, all in our colour. No pictures.
**Images:** none
**Build:** all at once.

**On the slide**
- 1993 Frayling, Royal College of Art: names research through design
- 2003 Fallman, CHI: HCI as a design-oriented field
- 2006 Bødker: third-wave HCI, from the workplace into everyday life
- 2007 Zimmerman, Forlizzi, Evenson, CHI: a named method

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** Four dots. In 1993 Frayling gave us the phrase research through design. In 2003, at CHI, Daniel Fallman argued that HCI had emerged as a design-oriented field, and asked what design is. Gaver cites him for exactly that move, characterising HCI as a design discipline. In 2006 Susanne Bødker described a third wave of HCI, moving from the workplace into homes and everyday life. And in 2007 Zimmerman, Forlizzi and Evenson made research through design a named method at CHI. By 2007 design was inside HCI, with a name. Our reading of why that made a mess: HCI's habits came from psychology and computer science, hypothesis, study, measured result. Design brought studio habits: build it, live with it, reflect. Nobody had agreed which habits judge which work. (about 125 words)
- **Transition:** Once design was inside HCI, it did not speak with one voice. It spoke in manifestos.
- **Watch out:**
  - Cross 1982 and Bødker 2006 are not cited by Gaver. Fallman 2003 is his reference [7]; Frayling is [11]; Zimmerman et al. 2007 is [34] (p. 946). Say "Gaver cites" only for Fallman, Frayling and Zimmerman.
  - Gaver's reference list spells the name "Fallmann" (p. 946, ref. 7); the author is Daniel Fallman (Fällman in Swedish). A slip in the paper, like those in P9; mention only if asked.
  - The Fallman line is our paraphrase of his abstract ("We argue that HCI has emerged as a design-oriented field of research"). Keep it a paraphrase on the slide.
  - Cross 1982 is off the slide; its content had come only from a secondary summary. If asked, v1 read the original: Cross's three cultures, pp. 221 to 222 (`../slides/08-Strengths-Limitations.md` log; https://Readings.design/PDF/cross_1982_designerlywaysofknowing.pdf).
  - The "why that made a mess" sentences in the Say are our reading, said as ours; Gaver's nearest text is HCI being "prone to 'scientism' in its cultural assumptions" (p. 938).
  - The ThirdWaveHCI link is the grant's name (p. 946). Bødker's paper is not cited in the essay; do not claim the grant's name comes from her.
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: What does Fallman 2003 actually argue?* A: His abstract says HCI "has emerged as a design-oriented field of research, directed at large towards innovation, design, and construction of new kinds of information and interaction technology", but that its philosophical, theoretical and methodological footing is "relatively poor". He contrasts three accounts of what design is, the conservative, the romantic and the pragmatic, uses sketching to show problem and solution worked out together, and separates "knowledge-generating Design-oriented Research" from "the artifact-generating conduct of Research-oriented Design" (CHI 2003, pp. 225 to 232; https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1145/642611.642652).
  - *Q: Where does Gaver use Fallman?* A: Three times: HCI characterised "as a design discipline [7]" (p. 938); design as many decisions about "potentially independent factors of an artefact" (p. 940, "c.f. [7]"); and portfolios making clear "a domain of design, its relevant dimensions" (p. 944, "c.f. [7]").
  - *Q: What about Schön?* A: Schön's The Reflective Practitioner (1983) is in Gaver's reference list as [28] (p. 946), but we found no in-text citation of [28] in the essay.
  - *Q: What is the third wave?* A: Bødker (NordiCHI 2006): the second wave centred on work; in the third, use contexts broaden into homes, everyday life and culture (abstract, via `../slides/01-Background.md`).
- **Sources:** paper p. 938, p. 940, p. 944 ([7]); p. 946 (refs 7, 11, 28, 34; grant). Fallman 2003: https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Design-oriented+human-computer+interaction+Fallman (DOI 10.1145/642611.642652, pp. 225 to 232) and https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1145/642611.642652 (abstract). Cross 1982: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/0142-694X(82)90040-0 (Design Studies 3(4), pp. 221 to 227) and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigel_Cross. Frayling, Bødker, Zimmerman et al. 2007: `../slides/01-Background.md` log.

## Slide 4: The mess showed itself as dozens of manifestos.

**Time:** 1:00 · **Visual:** the "dozens" pull quote across the top. Below it a grid of five small cards, each with a name in bold and one line, Designing for the Self marked with a small "a critic's" tag in the second colour. Across the bottom, the symptom line in our colour.
**Images:** none
**Build:** all at once.

**On the slide**
- Quote: "there must be dozens of different 'manifestos' for design, each seeking to establish a research programme" (Gaver, p. 941)
- **Reflective Design** (Sengers et al.): reflect on built-in values
- **Ludic design** (Gaver): against "clear, efficient solutions"
- **Supple Interfaces** (Isbister, Höök)
- **Designing for the Self** (Zimmerman)
- **Product Ecology** (Forlizzi): a framework
- Symptom: "little agreement" on values, methods, evaluation, output

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** How did the disagreement show itself? Gaver's estimate in 2012: there must be dozens of different manifestos for design, each seeking to establish a research programme. Five examples from the paper. Reflective Design, from Sengers and colleagues: reflection on unconscious values in computing should be a core principle of design. Gaver's own ludic design: an antidote to the idea that technology should give clear, efficient solutions. Supple Interfaces, Isbister and Höök, designing for richer human connections. Designing for the Self, Zimmerman, products that help people become who they want to be. Note that Zimmerman is one of the critics you meet next. And Product Ecology, Forlizzi's framework for products used together. The symptom, in Gaver's words: little agreement about values, methods, standards for evaluation or forms of output. (about 130 words)
- **Transition:** That lack of agreement is what the critics wrote about, in print.
- **Watch out:**
  - Pitfall P4. The manifestos Gaver lists together on p. 941 are Reflective Design, Supple Interfaces and Designing for the Self. Ludic design is his own, named as a manifesto on p. 938. Product Ecology is a framework, not a manifesto: frameworks "tend to downplay both their theoretical commitments and normative stance" (p. 938).
  - "There must be dozens" is Gaver's estimate, not a count. Do not say "Gaver counted".
  - The Supple Interfaces and Designing for the Self lines are paraphrases of their titles in Gaver's reference list (p. 946, refs 16 and 36); Gaver does not describe them in the text.
  - The symptom sentence (p. 942) comes from Gaver's account of the Kuhnian, pre-paradigmatic reading, the first of three readings on slide 10. He says "It is clearly possible to recognise the current state of research through design in this description"; do not present it as his final verdict on the field.
  - Slide 6 lists manifestos again, as one of five kinds of theory. Point back here rather than repeat the examples.
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: What exactly does each manifesto claim?* A: Reflective Design: "reflection on unconscious values embedded in computing and the practices that it supports can and should be a core principle of technology design" (Sengers et al., quoted p. 938). Ludic design: "an antidote to assumptions that technology should provide clear, efficient solutions to practical problems" (p. 938). Supple Interfaces: "designing and evaluating for richer human connections and experiences" (ref. 16, CHI 2007 extended abstracts). Designing for the Self: "Making products that help people become the person they desire to be" (ref. 36, CHI 2009). Product Ecology: factors "involved in designing for products to be used together" (p. 938).
  - *Q: What does a manifesto do, for Gaver?* A: Manifestos "suggest certain approaches to design as both as desirable and productive of future practice" (p. 938, the paper's wording); "their primary function is to build an account of a practice to be pursued in the future" (p. 938).
  - *Q: Is Gaver criticising Zimmerman's manifesto?* A: No. On p. 941 it is one of his examples of theory whose value is "their ability to inspire new designs". Gaver's point is that the critics, too, write manifestos.
  - *Q: What is the full symptom sentence?* A: "There is a proliferation of research programmes, and little agreement about the values for which we should design, the appropriate methods for doing so, standards for evaluation or agreed forms of output" (p. 942).
- **Sources:** paper p. 938 (manifestos, Reflective Design, ludic design, Product Ecology), p. 941 ("dozens", the three named manifestos, "ability to inspire new designs"), p. 942 (symptom), p. 946 (refs 10, 13, 16, 30, 36). `../slides/04-Theory.md` slide 04.1; `../slides/05-Convergence.md` slide 05.1.

## Slide 5: The reaction, 2007 to 2011: a call for standards.

**Time:** 1:15 · **Visual:** a horizontal timeline with four dots, 2007, 2008, 2010, 2011; under each dot the authors in small type and the critics' words on a quote card. Gaver's reading as the pull quote in a strip across the bottom, in the second colour.
**Images:** none
**Build:** the four dots one per click, left to right, then the bottom strip.

**On the slide**
- **2007** Zimmerman, Forlizzi, Evenson: no agreed standard yet
- **2008** Zimmerman, Forlizzi: theory with "a level of extensibility and verifiability"
- **2010** Zimmerman, Stolterman, Forlizzi: "develop protocols, descriptions, and guidelines"
- **2011** CHI panel "Quality control": "lacks clear expectations and standards"; "actionable metrics"
- Quote: conformance to standards "may be a route towards disciplinary legitimacy within HCI" (Gaver, p. 937)

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** Here is the reaction, in the critics' own words, as Gaver quotes them. [click] It starts in 2007. Zimmerman, Forlizzi and Evenson wrote that there was no agreed standard of what research through design means, nor of what a high quality contribution should be. Their four criteria come on slide 7. [click] In 2008 Zimmerman and Forlizzi asked for theory with "a level of extensibility and verifiability." [click] In 2010 Zimmerman, Stolterman and Forlizzi asked the community to "develop protocols, descriptions, and guidelines." [click] And in 2011 a CHI panel called "Quality control" said research through design "lacks clear expectations and standards," and asked for "actionable metrics." Notice what they ask for: standards, protocols, criteria. They do not ask design to become a science. [click] Why want standards at all? Gaver offers one reading, and hedges it: conformance to standards "may be a route towards disciplinary legitimacy within HCI." That is his guess, hedged, not their stated aim. (about 155 words)
- **Transition:** Before Gaver answers, look at what these standards would have judged: how the work was actually being done.
- **Watch out:**
  - Pitfall P2. The critics asked for standards and criteria, not for design to become a science. Do not give them a motive beyond Gaver's "may be a route towards disciplinary legitimacy" (p. 937).
  - Pitfall P3. "A level of extensibility and verifiability" is the critics' wording (p. 937, quoting [33]). "Extensible and verifiable" (p. 938) is Gaver's paraphrase; do not mix them.
  - Attribution is fixed: [34] Zimmerman, Forlizzi and Evenson 2007; [33] Zimmerman and Forlizzi 2008; [35] Zimmerman, Stolterman and Forlizzi, DIS 2010; [9] the CHI 2011 panel, Forlizzi, DiSalvo, Bardzell, Koskinen and Wensveen (p. 946).
  - The 2007 sentence ("no agreed upon standard ...") is from the 2007 paper itself (p. 499), not from Gaver's essay; it is spoken, not on the slide.
  - The panel's full line is "lacks clear expectations and standards for what constitutes "good" design research" ([9] p824, quoted p. 937). The slide truncates at "standards".
  - Stolterman is on both sides: co-author of [35], and Gaver's source for the "ultimate particular" (p. 939, p. 944). It is one small community arguing with itself.
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: Did the critics want design to be a science?* A: No. The 2011 panel's abstract says design knowledge develops with "fundamental differences" from scientific knowledge, and the 2007 paper replaces validity with relevance (Zimmerman et al. 2007, p. 499). Gaver's worry, on slide 8, is about how standards may be interpreted.
  - *Q: What else does the 2010 paper ask?* A: Gaver quotes it for "a proper research methodology that can produce relevant and rigorous theory" ([35] p316) and for doing this "like any other research approach" ([35] p317), both on p. 937. Its abstract reports interviews with 12 leading HCI design researchers.
  - *Q: Is "Paper 37" a real reference?* A: [33] is listed as "Human-Computer Interaction Institute. Paper 37" (p. 946): the Carnegie Mellon repository copy of an article in the journal Artifact, vol. 2, no. 1, 2008, pp. 41 to 45.
  - *Q: What does the 2007 sentence say exactly?* A: "there is no agreed upon standard of what research through design means nor what a high quality contribution should be" (Zimmerman et al. 2007, p. 499).
- **Sources:** paper p. 937 (all critics' quotations with [9], [33], [35]; "disciplinary legitimacy"), p. 946 (refs 9, 33, 34, 35). `../slides/01-Background.md` slide 01.2, If asked and log; `../slides2/02-Fight.md` slide 02.1. Zimmerman et al. 2007 p. 499: https://courses.ischool.berkeley.edu/i262/s13/readings_pdf/Zimmerman_Research_Through_Design_as_a_Method_for_IaD_in_HCI_0.pdf.
