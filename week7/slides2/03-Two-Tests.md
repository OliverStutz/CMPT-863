## Slide 03.1: Test 1, Popper: could you prove a design rule wrong?

**Time:** 2:00 · **Visual:** a scorecard in three panels. Left panel: a design rule as one sentence with the word "sometimes" highlighted. Middle panel, headed "To refute it you would need": two identical system icons differing only by a question mark. Right panel: a large stamp reading "Fails Popper." Beneath, one line in small type with the two-way underspecification.

**On the slide**
- Every design rule hides a word: "X will *sometimes* lead to successful outcomes."
- Gaver's test case is his own 2003 claim: ambiguity in a design makes people engage more deeply.
- To refute it you would need two systems identical except for ambiguity. "Simply tweaking" one misrepresents how designers work.
- **Verdict: fails Popper.** Theory underspecifies design, and design underspecifies theory.

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** Take any design rule. "Make it ambiguous and people will engage more deeply." Gaver points out that every such rule carries an invisible word: sometimes. Ambiguity will sometimes lead to engagement. A sometimes claim cannot be proven wrong, because any failure is just one of the other times. That is the in-principle problem. Then the in-practice problem, and here he picks on his own work, which I think is fair of him. His 2003 paper with Beaver and Benford claimed that ambiguity encourages people to grapple conceptually with a system. In principle, falsifiable. But to test it you would need an ambiguous system and an unambiguous one that are otherwise identical. Simply tweaking a finished design to add ambiguity would not represent how designers actually work. So the test is either unfair or unreal. Verdict: by Popper's rule, design theory fails. Gaver's phrase is that theory underspecifies design: a rule leaves a thousand decisions open. And the reverse holds: design underspecifies theory, because most of what makes a good design work is never in the rule. We argue one thing here. His logical point only covers a bare "sometimes" claim. A stronger claim, "ambiguity works more often than not," could be tested. His practical point, that a fair comparison is nearly impossible, is the one that carries the weight. (about 225 words)
- **Transition:** If you cannot refute it, what is it good for? Test two.
- **Watch out:**
  - The "implicit sometimes" sentence is p. 940. The ambiguity claim is quoted from Gaver, Beaver and Benford, CHI 2003 [14], on p. 940. "Simply tweaking an unambiguous system to be ambiguous" is p. 940. "tests which are simultaneously unambiguous and ecologically valid are inconceivable" is p. 940 and is the line Shin et al. (slide 10) will push against.
  - "Theory is underspecified by design" is on p. 944, in the annotation section, not in the Popper section. The slide pairs the two halves; the notes keep the pages apart.
  - Do not say Gaver thinks design theory is worthless under Popper. He says Popper's mirror is "potentially unflattering" and that the failure "reveals two important characteristics" of design theory (p. 940).
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: Is a "sometimes" claim really untestable? You could test whether X raises the success rate.* A: Fair. Gaver's logical point is about the bare form "X will sometimes lead to successful outcomes" (p. 940). A claim that X works more often than not-X is stronger and could be tested. His answer is the practical argument: "Underspecification makes falsification difficult in practice as well as in principle" (p. 940).
  - *Q: What were the five kinds of design theory he sorts?* A: Articulations of practice (Djajadiningrat and colleagues on embodied movement), borrowed theory (affordances becoming "irresistables," as the paper's text spells it), manifestos (Reflective Design, ludic design), frameworks (Forlizzi's Product Ecology) and meta-theory, including this essay (p. 938 to 939). Version 1 of the deck has a full slide on these.
  - *Q: Does Popper himself say science works by refutation alone?* A: Gaver notes Popper's account "is widely criticised both in Philosophy of Science and by practicing scientists" (p. 940), and quotes Kuhn: when a puzzle fails it "is considered the fault of the scientist using the theory, not the fault of the theory itself" (p. 939).
  - *Q: What is in the ambiguity paper?* A: Three kinds of ambiguity: of information (in the artefact), of context (in the cultural discourses used to read it) and of relationship (in the person's own stance). CHI 2003, pp. 233 to 240.
- **Sources:** paper p. 939 (Popper's criterion; Kuhn quotation), p. 940 ("implicit sometimes"; ambiguity claim; "Simply tweaking"; "inconceivable"; "unflattering mirror"; "reveals two important characteristics"), p. 944 ("theory is underspecified by design"). Ambiguity paper contents: `../slides/04-Theory.md`, If asked.

## Slide 03.2: Test 2, Lakatos: does it breed new work?

**Time:** 1:30 · **Visual:** three small cards, "Reflective Design," "Supple Interfaces," "Designing for the Self," each with "refutable" struck through and "inspires designs" ticked. A stamp reading "Passes Lakatos." Beneath, the three adjectives in large type. Bottom line in our colour: the pushback.

**On the slide**
- Reflective Design, Supple Interfaces, Designing for the Self: none can be refuted. What counts is whether they inspire new designs.
- What Popper would reject as "'merely' confirmatory" is, seen Lakatos's way, "testimony to the fertility of the overarching theory."
- **Verdict: passes Lakatos.** Design theory tends to be "provisional, contingent, and aspirational": theory that is "sometimes right."
- We argue: "productive" is also a standard. Who judges it?

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** Lakatos asks a different question. Not "can you refute it" but "does it keep producing?" Gaver lines up three design manifestos: Sengers's Reflective Design, Isbister and Höök's Supple Interfaces, and Zimmerman's Designing for the Self. We would add his own ludic design, which fits the same pattern. None is falsifiable. What matters, he says, is their ability to inspire new designs. Under Popper, the designs that follow a manifesto are merely confirmatory, weak as evidence. Read Lakatos's way, Gaver says, the same designs are testimony to the fertility of the theory. So the inability to refute design theory is not a flaw, as long as the theory leads to productive work. Both tests together give Gaver his three adjectives: design theory tends to be provisional, contingent and aspirational. It is theory that is sometimes right. He hedges: these two philosophers are not representative of the field, and whether design is a science depends on which account you pick. We argue one thing back. "Productive" is itself a standard. Gaver names Lakatos's criterion, the relative productivity of competing programmes, and never says who applies it in design, or when. Hold that thought for the end. (about 190 words)
- **Transition:** Two tests, two verdicts. Now the puzzle the critics built their case on.
- **Watch out:**
  - The three manifestos are named together on p. 941: "'Reflective Design' [30] or 'Supple Interfaces' [16] or 'Designing for the Self' [36]." Ludic design is Gaver's own manifesto on p. 938 [13]; the Say adds it as a fourth that fits the same pattern. Designing for the Self is by Zimmerman, one of the critics, so Gaver credits a critic's manifesto with fertility.
  - "theories that are sometimes right" is p. 941 (top of the page, continuing from p. 940). "provisional, contingent, and aspirational" is p. 937 (abstract) and p. 941; p. 938 and p. 945 have it without the serial comma.
  - Gaver's hedges: "not ... particularly representative" and "depends on the account of science one adopts," both p. 941. Say them; they matter for slide 11.
  - "relative productivity of competing programmes" is Gaver's wording for Lakatos's criterion, p. 941. The limitation is that nobody is named to apply it, not that it is absent.
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: What does Lakatos's test look like in science?* A: A programme is progressive if it predicts "novel facts" that rivals did not (quoted p. 940), and degenerating if it only patches itself after the fact. Gaver does not use "degenerating"; v1 took it from the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy.
  - *Q: Does "sometimes right" theory deserve the name theory?* A: Gaver's position is yes, and he cites Stolterman: designers "can be prepared-for-action but not guided-in-action by detailed prescriptive procedures" (p. 941). The discussion anchor "is sometimes-right theory worth publishing" takes this up.
  - *Q: Could an empirical programme pass the same test?* A: Yes, and slide 10 uses one: the gesture-elicitation method used by Wobbrock, Morris and Wilson 2009 dates from 2005, and a 2020 review traces it through 216 studies. Fertility does not separate design from science; it only rescues design theory from Popper.
- **Sources:** paper p. 937 and p. 941 ("provisional, contingent, and aspirational"; p. 938 and p. 945 without the serial comma), p. 940 ("predict novel facts"), p. 941 (three manifestos; "testimony to the fertility of the overarching theory"; "theories that are sometimes right"; Stolterman quotation; "relative productivity"; both hedges). Ludic design [13] on p. 938.
