## Slide 04.1: Why can't designers agree? Pick a diagnosis.

**Time:** 2:30 · **Visual:** three cards across the slide, A, B, C, each with a two-word name in large type, one plain sentence, the thinker in small type, and a coloured tag at the bottom: "Fix: standardise," "Fix: nothing," "Fix: let it multiply." Under the cards, the iPad sentence as the one full-sentence quotation. The three "Fix:" tags are built after the vote is counted, since they give the answer away. Footer in large type: "Vote: which is HCI as you have met it?"

**On the slide**
- Dozens of manifestos, no agreed methods. Why?
- **A. Baby science.** No shared paradigm yet; it "must founder." Fix: standardise.
- **B. Messier up close.** Science may be just as contested day to day; "most of us" seem to share core values. Fix: nothing.
- **C. Makes worlds, not maps.** After the iPad, you design "for a different world." Fix: let it multiply.
- **Vote: which is design research in HCI, as you have met it?**

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** Here is the observation Gaver starts from, and the one the critics' case rests on. There must be dozens of design manifestos, each with its own goals, methods and showcase projects, and no agreement on how to evaluate any of them. Why? Gaver offers three diagnoses, and I want you to pick one. A: design research is a baby science. Kuhn's pre-paradigm stage: everyone argues from first principles, findings do not add up, and in Gaver's words such research must founder. If you believe A, the critics are right: write standards, grow up. B: it only looks messy because you are standing close. Latour's point: real science is full of controversy day to day. Gaver adds that the unity we see may come from popular accounts and hindsight. He also says most of us seem to share values: contact with the people we design for, a wide exploration of options, craft, and making as a route to discovery. If you believe B, there is no problem to fix. C: design does not describe one world, it makes new ones. His example: once the iPad exists, everybody designing tablets is designing for a different world. Science presumes one world to be right about, and that, Gaver says, seems to drive convergence. Design has as many worlds as designers build. If you believe C, calls for shared standards are, in his phrase, potentially repressive acts of ontological politics. Which of these is design research in HCI, as you have met it? One hand each. Hands up for A. For B. For C. Notice: only A supports the critics. Gaver leans to B and C. And the person next to you may have just disagreed with you about what field you are in. (about 280 words)
- **Transition:** So if the field will not converge, what is theory for?
- **Watch out:**
  - "Baby science," "messier up close" and "makes worlds, not maps" are our labels. Gaver's are "pre-paradigmatic" (p. 941), the Latour reading (p. 942), and the "performative" or many-worlds reading from Law and Urry (p. 943).
  - "must founder" is Gaver's wording (p. 942), stronger than Kuhn's own; say "in Gaver's words."
  - The "most of us" list (p. 942) is hedged twice: "most of us ... seem to share," and "Perhaps we do not all agree with all of these assumptions all of the time." Say at least the first hedge.
  - The iPad quotation is p. 943: "anybody seeking to research or develop tablet computers ... is designing for a different world, one in which the iPad exists."
  - "potentially repressive acts of ontological politics" is p. 943, and it is Gaver's phrase, hedged with "potentially."
  - Gaver's own verdict is on p. 938: one reading implies working toward shared understandings, "the other two imply either that there is no problem to solve or that progress in this area will be marked by proliferation rather than agreement." Say "leans," not "chooses."
  - Count the vote and note the split; discussion anchor 3 uses it. If the room is mostly A, ask a B or C voter to defend before moving on; if mostly C, ask what their own project assumes about the world.
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: What does Kuhn actually say about pre-paradigm fields?* A: That early inquiry is marked by competing schools, each with its own view of the field, and that progress comes once one paradigm wins. Gaver summarises this on p. 941 to 942 and adds that without agreed standards of importance "there is little sense of priority or order to the findings that are amassed." Kuhn's two senses of paradigm, an exemplar and a set of shared understandings, are both on p. 941.
  - *Q: Isn't B just saying "science is messy too"? How does that answer the critics?* A: It answers them in two ways. If scientific discourse "may well exhibit just as much uncertainty, interpretation and debate as does research through design" (p. 942), then disagreement is not proof of immaturity. And if designers already share the values on the list, there may be more consensus than the call for standards assumes.
  - *Q: Where does the "many worlds" idea come from?* A: Law and Urry 2004, "Enacting the social," quoted on p. 943: "different research practices might be making multiple worlds, and that such worlds might be equally valid, equally true, but simply unlike one another." Osborne and Rose 1999 on public opinion research is the second source: the social sciences create the phenomena they study.
  - *Q: Which reading does Gaver believe?* A: He does not pick one outright. On p. 938 he says the first implies the community should work toward shared understandings and the other two imply no problem or proliferation. His closing advice, to be "wary of impulses towards convergence and standardisation" (p. 937), fits B and C.
  - *Q: Can natural science ever be "many worlds"?* A: Gaver says natural science works under "a strong presumption that the object of study is a single, unitary world that pre-exists and is independent of its observers" (p. 943), and that this belief "seems naturally to lead to convergence."
- **Sources:** paper p. 938 (three perspectives and verdict), p. 941 ("dozens of different 'manifestos'"; "Why is there not more convergence"; Kuhn's two senses; "three possibilities"), p. 942 (pre-paradigmatic; "must founder"; Latour; "most of us" list and both hedges; "just as much uncertainty"), p. 943 (Law and Urry quotation; Osborne and Rose; iPad; "single, unitary world"; "potentially repressive acts of ontological politics"), p. 937 ("wary of impulses").
