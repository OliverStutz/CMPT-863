## Slide 5 of 21: Gaver's judo move: you want us scientific? Which science?

**Layout:** two cards side by side, "Popper" and "Lakatos," each with its one-line test. A short line between them. The pull quote across the bottom.
**Images:** none.
**Build:** first line and the two cards first; the line between them on click 1; the pull quote on click 2.

**Text:**
- Gaver never says design is science. He asks: which science?
- **Popper:** scientific if you could prove it wrong.
- **Lakatos:** scientific if it keeps producing new findings.

## Slide 7 of 21: Test 1, Popper: could you prove a design rule wrong?

**Layout:** a scorecard in three panels. Left panel: the design rule as one sentence, with "sometimes" highlighted (the highlight is ours). Middle panel, headed "To refute it you would need": two identical system icons, one marked with a question mark. Right panel: a large stamp reading "Fails Popper." One line in small type beneath the panels.
**Images:** none.
**Build:** left panel first; middle panel on click 1; stamp on click 2; the small line on click 3.

**Text:**
- Every design rule hides a word: "X will *sometimes* lead to successful outcomes."
- Gaver's test case is his own 2003 claim, with Beaver and Benford: ambiguity in a design makes people engage more deeply.
- To refute it you would need two systems identical except for ambiguity. "Simply tweaking" one misrepresents how designers work.
- **Verdict: fails Popper.** Theory underspecifies design, and design underspecifies theory.

## Slide 8 of 21: Test 2, Lakatos: does it breed new work?

**Layout:** three small cards across the top, "Reflective Design," "Supple Interfaces," "Designing for the Self," each with "refutable" struck through and "inspires designs" ticked. A stamp reading "Passes Lakatos." The three adjectives in large type beneath. The last line in a second colour at the bottom.
**Images:** none.
**Build:** cards first; the Popper-versus-Lakatos line on click 1; stamp and adjectives on click 2; the last line on click 3.

**Text:**
- Reflective Design, Supple Interfaces, Designing for the Self: none can be refuted. What counts is whether they inspire new designs.
- What Popper would reject as "'merely' confirmatory" is, seen Lakatos's way, "testimony to the fertility of the overarching theory."
- **Verdict: passes Lakatos.** Design theory tends to be "provisional, contingent, and aspirational": theory that is "sometimes right."
- We argue: "productive" is also a standard. Who judges it?

## Slide 9 of 21: Why can't designers agree? Three diagnoses

**Layout:** one line across the top. Three cards across the slide, A, B, C, each with a two-word name in large type, one sentence, and the thinker in small type. The pull quote beneath the cards.
**Images:** none.
**Build:** top line first; cards A, B, C on clicks 1 to 3; the pull quote on click 4.

**Text:**
- Dozens of manifestos, no agreed methods. Why?
- **A. Baby science.** No shared paradigm yet; in Gaver's words, such research "must founder." (after Kuhn)
- **B. Messier up close.** Science may be just as contested day to day (Latour); and, Gaver says, "most of us" seem to share core values.
- **C. Makes worlds, not maps.** Design makes new worlds rather than describing one. (Law and Urry)

**Quote:** "anybody seeking to research or develop tablet computers ... is designing for a different world, one in which the iPad exists." (Gaver, p. 943)

**Images:** none.
**Build:** top row first; bottom row on click 1; the third line on click 2.

**Text:**
- Usual order: theory leads, the system illustrates.
- Gaver's order: the artefacts are the "definite facts". Theory is the annotation (our word: the caption).
- Objects embody theory but do not encode it. The annotation makes the choices readable.
