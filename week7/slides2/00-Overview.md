# 00 Overview: Week 7 presentation, version 2 (story-led, simpler)

Presentation on William Gaver, "What Should We Expect From Research Through Design?", CHI 2012, Austin, session Critical Perspectives on Design, pp. 937 to 946. CMPT 863 Human-Computer Interaction, Week 7, 22 October 2026, presented by Oliver Stutz alone. 30-minute talk, then a 30-minute discussion led by the presenter.

This folder is a second cut of the deck in `../slides/`. Oliver found version 1 complete but dull: seventeen slides built as an argument map, every line a page-cited quotation. Version 2 tells the same paper as a story with one question, in twelve slides, with the room asked to do something three times. Every fact and quotation is reused from the version 1 files, which were written and cross-checked by independent reviewers on 2026-10-19 and 2026-10-20, so the fact-checking carries over; each file's log names the v1 file it draws on. Nothing new was added except as marked in the logs.

Written 2026-10-21 by the orchestrating session from the paper text, the version 1 section files and `../progress.md`.

## What changed from version 1

- **One question runs through the talk.** "Is this research?" is asked about a real object, the Drift Table, on slide 1, before any theory. The room votes. The same question comes back on the last slide, and the change in hands is the start of the discussion. The twist that makes it land: the critics who asked for standards cite that exact table as the exemplar of design research, so the fight is about the rules, not the work.
- **Twelve slides, not seventeen.** Background and problem became "the fight" (one slide). Method became "the judo move" (one slide). Theory became two scorecards. Strengths, limitations and thesis became "three things we would say to Gaver" (one slide). Connections went from six to four.
- **Headlines are sentences.** "A coffee table that drifts. Is this research?" instead of "Background." "Why can't designers agree? Pick a diagnosis." instead of "Convergence: three readings."
- **Under about 70 words on any slide, usually under 50.** Printed pages, hedges and secondary sources moved to the notes. One quotation per slide at most where it could be managed.
- **Plain words first, Gaver's words second.** Each idea gets a one-line paraphrase on the slide ("Baby science," "Messier up close," "Makes worlds, not maps"); his phrase sits in the notes with its page.
- **Three audience moments inside the thirty minutes.** A show of hands (slide 1), a three-way vote on the three readings of non-convergence (slide 6), and the Rams test (slide 7), where the room tries to "design in the style of Rams" from principles alone, products alone, and both, and so meets the annotated portfolio before it is defined.
- **One visual per slide**, described in each file's Visual line: a photograph, a pair of speech bubbles, a scorecard with a stamp, three cards, three columns, a scoreboard.
- **Depth kept.** Every slide still carries an "If asked" block, shorter than v1's, drawn from v1's verified depth material.

What was dropped from the slides and lives only in v1 or in the notes: the five genres of design theory (v1 slide 5), the method-evidence-reflexivity table (v1 slide 10), the separate strengths slide (v1 slide 11), the full reference-validation asides, two of the six connections, and the five-tensions slide (v1 slide 16), whose questions are now the discussion anchors.

## The arc

1. **An object** (01). The Drift Table. Is this research? Hands. Reveal: the critics cite it as the exemplar. The fight is about rules, not work.
2. **The fight** (02.1). Critics 2007 to 2011 want standards for legitimacy. Gaver fears self-policing and scientism. Why you care: "where is your evaluation?"
3. **The judo move** (02.2). Not "design is science" but "which science?" Two philosophers who disagree. This is also how the essay works: no study, two borrowed tests.
4. **Test 1** (03.1). Popper. Every design rule hides "sometimes." His own ambiguity claim cannot be fairly tested. Fails.
5. **Test 2** (03.2). Lakatos. Manifestos that cannot be refuted still breed designs. Passes. Theory that is "sometimes right." Our pushback planted: who judges "productive"?
6. **The puzzle** (04). Why no agreement? Baby science, messier up close, or makes worlds. Vote. Only A supports the critics.
7. **Rams** (05.1). Could you design like Rams from principles alone, products alone, both? The third column is the annotated portfolio.
8. **The flip** (05.2). Object is the fact, theory is the caption. Point, area, invariants. Opposite of patterns.
9. **Aftermath** (06). Scoreboard, three lanes. Twist, ours: a proposal wary of standards became a named, citable form. Two open questions: CHI 2026's Design subcommittee, generative AI.
10. **Connections** (07). Tangible Bits, Greenberg and Buxton, Wobbrock et al., Shin et al. Every link ours.
11. **Verdict** (08). Three things we would say to Gaver, and credit where due.
12. **Handover** (09). Hands again. Did you move? Plan for the half hour.

Every section's Transition line was checked against the next section's opening.

## Working rules for edits

- Keep each file's format: On the slide, Speaker notes (Say, Transition, Watch out, If asked, Sources), Verification log, Open items.
- No em-dashes or en-dashes used as dashes. Check with `python3 slides2/_check-sections.py`.
- Every quotation carries its printed page in the notes, not on the slide. Outside facts name the v1 file that verified them, or a URL.
- "Gaver says" for his claims, "Gaver quotes" for what he quotes, "we argue" for ours. Never a motive stated as fact. Keep his hedges.
- Spoken scripts at or under 130 words per minute of the slide's time; on-slide text under about 70 words, aiming for 50.
- Our labels ("baby science," "judo move," "the caption") are ours; the notes give Gaver's wording.
