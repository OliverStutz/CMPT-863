## Slide 08.1: Three things we would say to Gaver

**Time:** 2:00 · **Visual:** the verdict sentence large across the top. Three numbered rows beneath, each a short pushback in our colour. A final row in a second colour headed "Credit where due." A **[classmate]** credit beside any row a commentary raised.

**On the slide**
- **Gaver wins against scientism. He concedes more than his conclusion admits.**
- 1. "Productive research programmes" is a standard. Who judges it?
- 2. As we read it, the critics' problem lives in reviewing. How does a committee decide?
- 3. Your "most of us" list already works as criteria. Standards, just not metrics.
- Credit where due: names the elephant, hedges honestly, brings a form, not only a complaint.
- **[classmate]**

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** First, Gaver's own three conclusions, from his last page: expect provisional theory, treat standard-setting as politics to be approached with care, and let theory annotate portfolios. Now ours. Gaver wins the argument against scientism. The judo move works: if philosophers cannot agree what science is, "be more scientific" is not a usable instruction. But he concedes more than his conclusion admits, and there are three things we would say to him. One. Your test for design theory is whether it leads to productive research programmes. That is a standard. You name Lakatos's criterion and never say who applies it in design, or when. Two. As we read it, the critics' problem is practical. It lives in reviewing, funding and examining. They asked for actionable metrics. You grant that integration would make assessment fairer and more rigorous, then never return to it. Your essay does not say how a committee decides on a portfolio without shared criteria. Three. Your own list of what most of us share: contact with the people we design for, wide exploration, craft, making as discovery. That list already works as criteria. A fair reader could say you supplied standards, just not metrics. You even hedge it yourself: perhaps we do not all agree. And credit where due. He names the elephant in a CHI paper, following Sengers. He hedges honestly. And he brings a form to the table, not only a complaint; in our reading, few critiques of rigour do. **[classmate]** (about 255 words)
- **Transition:** Which brings us back to the table.
- **Watch out:**
  - "Wins against scientism" is our verdict; "concedes more than his conclusion admits" rests on p. 941 (he accepts "clearly articulating ... the standards one might want to advocate") and p. 942 (the "most of us" list). Have both pages ready.
  - Pushback 1: "productive research programmes" and "relative productivity of competing programmes" are both p. 941. The limitation is that no judge is named, not that the criterion is absent.
  - Pushback 2: "actionable metrics" is the panel's phrase, quoted on p. 937. Gaver's nearest reply is that norms are "political acts to be approached with care" (p. 945), which states the risk, not a procedure. Gaver himself says integration "would make assessment of new contributions fairer and more rigorous" (p. 941) and never returns to it. "Funding" and "examining" are our extension; the essay names neither.
  - Pushback 3 was "against our own case" in v1 and is now a pushback: the list works as criteria. Say the hedge "Perhaps we do not all agree with all of these assumptions all of the time" (p. 942) if pressed.
  - Credit: he cites Sengers 2006 on scientism (p. 941), so "names the elephant" means names it in a CHI paper, not first. "some sympathy" p. 937; "depends on the account" p. 941; "a serious form of theoretical contribution" p. 945.
  - Gaver's best replies, for fairness: to 1, that fertility is judged over time by what a programme produces, as in Lakatos (p. 941); to 2, that standard-setting is "ontological politics" and should be "approached with care" (p. 943, p. 945); to 3, that shared values are not the same as agreed methods and outputs, which is what the critics asked for (p. 941).
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: Isn't the judo move self-defeating? If nobody can define science, nobody can be asked for rigour.* A: Gaver's answer is that a field should set standards "on its own terms" (p. 945) and that the question whether design is a science is futile (p. 945). Our answer is that this moves the problem rather than solving it: "its own terms" still have to be written by someone.
  - *Q: Are there limitations you left off?* A: Two from v1. The proposal is sketched, not shown: one figure, of Rams's products, not a research through design project (p. 945). And missing voices: Pullin on curating design collections and Schön are in the reference list but never cited; Cross 1982 is absent. Also, we argue, by its own taxonomy the essay is a manifesto with one example, though Gaver calls it "a contribution to meta-theory" (p. 939).
  - *Q: Strengths you left off?* A: Disagreement used as evidence: "It is difficult to call for research to be more scientific if what it means to be scientific is under question" (p. 941). And the balance rule for portfolios, "neither dominates, and neither is subservient" (p. 945), which is a usable test.
  - *Q: Does Gaver say anything about who should judge?* A: Not directly. The closest is that Lakatos's criterion is "the relative productivity of competing programmes as the grounds for scientists' choice amongst them" (p. 941), which puts the judgement with practitioners over time.
