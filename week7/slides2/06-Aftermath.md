## Slide 06.1: Did he win? 2012 to 2026

**Time:** 2:00 · **Visual:** a scoreboard in three columns, "Gaver's way," "The critics' way," "Both," each with three short items and a year. Beneath the board, the twist line in large type. Bottom strip: two open questions, each as one line. No figure.

**On the slide**
- **Gaver's way:** "Annotated Portfolios" with Bowers, 2012. RTD conference from 2013, artefacts exhibited. DIS Pictorials, 2014.
- **The critics' way:** strong concepts 2012, bridging concepts 2014, documenting the RtD process 2016.
- **Both:** Löwgren 2013: portfolios as one kind of "intermediate-level knowledge."
- **Twist (ours):** a proposal wary of standards became a named, citable form.
- **Open:** CHI 2026's Design subcommittee invites "alternatives to scientistic ways of knowing." Generative AI makes examples cheap. What is one object worth now?

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** Partly. Right after CHI, Gaver and John Bowers published a short piece called "Annotated Portfolios," and the idea got a name. A Research Through Design conference started in 2013 that exhibits the artefacts and discusses them at round tables. DIS added Pictorials in 2014, papers where the images carry the argument. We argue: the form found venues, not a rule. But the other lane, toward knowledge above single designs, kept moving too. Two ideas that sit above single designs: strong concepts, from Höök and Löwgren, and bridging concepts, from Dalsgaard and Dindler. Then a 2016 framework, by Bardzell and colleagues, for documenting the research through design process, which speaks to the 2010 call for protocols. And in the middle, Löwgren in 2013 filed annotated portfolios as one kind of intermediate-level knowledge, next to patterns and strong concepts. One 2014 book prints a Zimmerman and Forlizzi chapter and a Gaver chapter side by side. Here is the twist, as we read it. The man who was wary of standardisation proposed a form, and the form got a name, a citable paper, and venues that exhibit artefacts. Two open questions. The CHI 2026 Design subcommittee now explicitly invites work exploring alternatives to scientistic ways of knowing. That is a scope statement, not reviewing practice, but it is close to his word. And generative AI can produce hundreds of design variants in an afternoon. Gaver's proposal rests on the definite, particular object, and, we would add, a costly one. What is one object worth when examples are nearly free? (about 245 words)
- **Transition:** Before our verdict, four places where this paper touches what we already read.
- **Watch out:**
  - The RTD conference ran 2013 (Gateshead), 2015 (Cambridge), 2017 (Edinburgh), 2019 (Delft and Rotterdam), paused after 2019, and returns in 2027 as "RTD Roams" in York, Melbourne and Tokyo. If someone says it stopped, concede the pause.
  - "The critics' way" is our lane label, not a claim that these authors opposed Gaver; Höök and Löwgren 2012 cite the essay.
  - Gaver and Bowers 2012 and Bowers 2012 come from Gaver's own studio: continuation, not uptake. The independent evidence in his lane is the RTD conference, DIS Pictorials and Pierce 2014.
  - Zimmerman and Forlizzi 2014 do not cite the 2012 essay; they say they "worked to formalize" the approach (p. 178). Neither rebuttal nor concession.
  - "his word": the CHI 2026 Design subcommittee text says "alternatives to scientistic ways of knowing." Gaver's word on p. 938 is "scientism." Close, not identical; say "his word" loosely or "his diagnosis."
  - The generative AI link is ours. The two CHI 2024 papers (Suh et al., Luminate; Davis et al.) are not about research through design.
  - "hundreds of design variants": Suh et al. say language models can produce "hundreds and thousands" of outputs. Keep the number vague.
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: Did these later works cite Gaver?* A: Höök and Löwgren 2012, Bowers 2012, Löwgren 2013, Pierce 2014 and Bardzell et al. 2016 all list the essay. Dalsgaard and Dindler 2014, Frauenberger 2019 and Beaudouin-Lafon, Bødker and Mackay 2021 do not, per their Crossref reference lists. OpenAlex counted about 1,100 citations to the essay in October 2026.
  - *Q: What is a strong concept?* A: Höök and Löwgren's intermediate-level knowledge: generative, carrying a core design idea across use situations, about interactive behaviour rather than appearance, above particular instances. Examples: social navigation, seamfulness. Judged as "contestable, defensible, and substantive." Close to Gaver's "discursiveness and elaboration" (p. 946) but it seeks knowledge above single designs.
  - *Q: What is a Pictorial?* A: A DIS paper format from 2014 in which visual material carries the argument, with limited text. Later adopted by TEI and Creativity and Cognition.
  - *Q: What did the 2014 book say?* A: Ways of Knowing in HCI (Olson and Kellogg, eds.) prints Zimmerman and Forlizzi's chapter, with five steps for research through design (select, design, evaluate, reflect and disseminate, repeat, p. 184), and Gaver's chapter, which says annotated portfolios "may capture best what we can know through design."
  - *Q: Any numbers on CHI reviewing?* A: CHI 2026 had eighteen subcommittees and 6,730 paper submissions; Design had 485, with 37.9% going to revise and resubmit against 38.7% overall. A scope statement is not reviewing practice; keep the question open.
  - *Q: What do the AI papers actually find?* A: Suh et al. (Luminate, CHI 2024): current interfaces push users "towards rapid convergence on a limited set of ideas" despite the models' output volume. Davis et al. (CHI 2024): graphical design-space interfaces supported ideation better than text prompts. The link to Gaver's "definite nature of designed artefacts" (p. 943) is our question.
  - *Q: Do portfolios favour rich labs?* A: Open. The essay was funded by a European Research Council Advanced Investigator Award (p. 946). A portfolio of built artefacts costs more than a study. We raise it; the paper does not.
