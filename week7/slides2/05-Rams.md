# 05 Rams: the annotated portfolio as an experience

**Purpose:** Teach the annotated portfolio by having the room try it on Dieter Rams before defining it, then show what the idea flips in the usual order of theory and object in HCI. **Slides:** 2. **Time:** 4:30. **Speaker:** Oliver. **Previous section:** 04 The puzzle. **Next section:** 06 Aftermath.

## Slide 05.1: The test you can do right now: Dieter Rams

**Time:** 3:00 · **Visual:** three columns under one headline question. Column 1, "Principles only": two of Rams's principles in large type on a plain background. Column 2, "Products only": `../assets/rams-fig1-products-only.png`, the seven photographs from Figure 1 with the notes, principles and credits masked. Column 3, "Both": `../assets/rams-fig1-annotated.png`, Figure 1 as printed with its notes, the four principles and the caption. Reveal the columns one at a time, and build the bullets with them; the line "The third column is an annotated portfolio" appears only after column 3. Caption under column 3: "Figure 1 of the paper, Gaver's sketch; images Flickr Creative Commons."

**On the slide**
- **Could you design "in the style of Dieter Rams" from...**
- ...the principles alone? "Good design is unobtrusive." "Good design is thorough down to the last detail."
- ...the products alone? Seven pieces for Braun and Vitsoe.
- ...both together?
- The third column is an **annotated portfolio**.

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** Gaver's proposal is best met as an experience, so try this. Dieter Rams designed for Braun and Vitsoe and wrote ten principles of good design. [click] Column one: two of the principles. "Good design is unobtrusive." "Good design is thorough down to the last detail." Could you design in the style of Rams from those sentences alone? Hands if yes. [pause] Probably not. Gaver grants they may seem too terse to stand on their own. [click] Column two: the products alone. A radio, a shelving system, a coffee maker, a shaver. Could you design in his style from these? Hands if yes. [pause] You would get the surface, maybe. You would not know which features matter and which are accidents of the period. [click] Column three: the products with the principles beside them, each note pointing at a detail. Hands if yes now. [pause] Now, Gaver says, one can imagine designing in the style of Rams without any detailed formal theory. That third column is an annotated portfolio: a set of related designs, plus short conceptual notes that point at what ties them together, with neither part in charge. The products ground the words. The words tell you where to look. This is his answer to the critics who wanted extensible, verifiable theory. As we read it, you can extend this form by designing the next Rams product. You can verify it by checking a note against the object. And nobody has to pretend the principles are laws. Two cautions. Gaver calls the figure a sketch and says the form is not defined by how it is laid out. And we would add: Rams's portfolio is a product designer's commercial work, not a research through design project. (about 300 words)
- **Transition:** What this quietly flips is the order of theory and object in HCI.
- **Watch out:**
  - Figure 1 shows seven photographs and four of the ten principles (numbers 2, 4, 5 and 8 in Vitsoe's order). The text names two (p. 944). Never say the figure shows all ten (pitfall 8 in v1).
  - The notes in Figure 1 are Gaver's sketch, not Rams's words; the caption says "Sketch design for an annotated portfolio." Say "the notes" or "Gaver's notes."
  - Products "for Braun and Vitsoe," not "commercial products": one item in the figure, a portable TV, was a prototype never produced.
  - "Accidents of the period" is our phrase, not Gaver's. Vitsoe dates the principles to the late 1970s; v1 gives 1957 for the shelving system and 1959 for Vitsoe's founding.
  - Let the room answer each column before the scripted answer; the [click] and [pause] cues mark the spots. If time is short, run only column three as a question.
  - The sentence "one can imagine 'designing in the style of Dieter Rams' without the need for a detailed formal theory" is p. 944. "too terse to serve as substantial conceptual contributions by themselves" is p. 944. "neither dominates, and neither is subservient" is p. 945. "annotated portfolios are not defined by their graphic presentation" is the Figure 1 caption, p. 945; "not a formal one" and "not how materials are presented" are p. 944.
  - "extensibility and verifiability": Gaver claims portfolios allow this "for which some of the HCI design community have called" (p. 945). Our gloss "design the next product / check the note against the object" is ours.
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: What are the ten principles?* A: As Vitsoe lists them: good design is innovative; makes a product useful; is aesthetic; makes a product understandable; is unobtrusive; is honest; is long-lasting; is thorough down to the last detail; is environmentally friendly; is as little design as possible.
  - *Q: Why Rams and not a design research project?* A: Gaver does not say. We argue it is the limitation that matters most: the one worked example is a product designer's commercial portfolio, not research through design, so the form is shown, not demonstrated. Gaver and Bowers 2012 and Bowers 2012 later apply it to the studio's own work.
  - *Q: Who annotates, the designer or someone else?* A: Gaver's example has Rams annotating Rams, via the principles. Figure 1 is annotated by an unnamed hand (Gaver's sketch). The paper does not settle who should annotate; the discussion can.
  - *Q: Is the current Vitsoe site an annotated portfolio?* A: Roughly. It pairs each principle with one product, which Gaver notes "emphasises his design principles over the portfolio of products themselves" (p. 945). As archived in 2026, principle 8 is illustrated by a calculator by Dietrich Lubs, not Rams.
- **Sources:** paper p. 944 (Rams, Braun and Vitsoe; "ten concise principles"; two principles named; "too terse"; "designing in the style of Dieter Rams"; "not a formal one"; "not how materials are presented"), p. 945 (Figure 1 and caption; "neither dominates"; "extensibility and verifiability"; Vitsoe site). Ten principles, Figure 1 contents, prototype TV: `../slides/06-Annotation.md`, If asked and background.

## Slide 05.2: What that flips: the object is the fact, theory is the caption

**Time:** 1:30 · **Visual:** two rows. Top row, labelled "Usual order": the word THEORY in large type, a small system icon beneath it captioned "illustration." Bottom row, labelled "Gaver's order": a large artefact icon, the word "theory" in small type beside it captioned "annotation." Right third of the slide: a scatter of seven dots inside a loose outline, captioned "one design is a point; a portfolio marks out an area."

**On the slide**
- Usual order: theory leads, the system illustrates.
- Gaver's order: the artefacts are the "definite facts." Theory is the caption.
- Objects embody theory but do not encode it. The caption makes the choices readable.
- One design is a point in design space. A portfolio marks out an area: what varied, what never did.
- In several respects the converse of a pattern language: patterns abstract across examples, portfolios keep each one particular.

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** Normally in HCI the theory comes first and the system illustrates it. Gaver flips that. The artefacts, he says, are the definite facts of research through design. Theories are provisional; a finished design is not. He borrows two phrases: Stolterman's ultimate particular, and Carroll and Kellogg's theory nexus, meaning every object carries its designer's choices about values, function, users and form. But here is the key line: artefacts embody theory, they do not encode it. You cannot read the important dimensions off the object. So theory's job is to annotate, to point at what matters. Then from one to many. A single design is a point in design space. A portfolio of related designs marks out an area, and comparing the pieces shows which dimensions the designer varied and which never changed. Last, the contrast with Alexander's pattern language. Patterns abstract a solution out of many examples. Portfolios keep every piece particular and write about what joins them. Gaver is not throwing out theoretical writing. Its role, he says, is inspiration and annotation, not replacement. (about 185 words)
- **Transition:** That was 2012. Did he win?
- **Watch out:**
  - "the definite facts of research through design" is p. 945. "Theories may be provisional, but designed artefacts (as opposed to demonstrators, for example) are not" is p. 944; the parenthetical matters, since many HCI prototypes are demonstrators.
  - Carroll and Kellogg's "theory nexus" is cited on p. 944 but missing from the reference list (a slip of the paper's, found in v1's reference check). The in-text "[3]" there points at Collins 1994 by mistake.
  - "If artefacts embody theory, however, they do not encode it" is p. 944. "limited to inspiration and annotation" is p. 945.
  - Alexander: "in several respects, the converse of Alexander's [1] design patterns" is p. 945. Gaver's point is that patterns "abstract regularities from repeated attempts" while portfolios "maintain the particularity of individual examples."
  - "Caption" is our word. Gaver insists that in a portfolio "neither dominates, and neither is subservient" (p. 945); if a classmate says "caption" makes theory subservient, agree and give his word, "annotation."
  - "point in design space ... an area in that space" is p. 944; "the invariances among them" is p. 944.
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: What is a "theory nexus"?* A: Carroll and Kellogg's term: a designed artefact is the meeting point of its designers' implicit theories, from the philosophical (what values) to the functional, the social and the aesthetic (p. 944). Gaver uses it to say an object carries judgement in a definite form.
  - *Q: What does "ultimate particular" mean?* A: Stolterman's phrase for the designed thing as a specific, situated whole, which he says has "the same dignity and importance as truth in science" (p. 944, quoting Stolterman 2008 p. 59).
  - *Q: How is this different from a design pattern, concretely?* A: A pattern in Alexander's book is a named recurring problem with the core of a solution usable "a million times over," illustrated by one photograph standing for many cases. A portfolio is one designer's set, kept as a set, with notes on what joins and separates the pieces. Patterns generalise; portfolios particularise.
  - *Q: Does Gaver say portfolios replace theory?* A: No: "I am not proposing here that annotated portfolios subsume all other forms of design theory. Theoretical writing remains important ... particularly for an interdisciplinary audience" (p. 945).
  - *Q: Is the microwave example related?* A: Yes. On p. 944 he says that to understand accounts of controlling several functions at once, "it is useful to consider specific designs such as a rotary controller for a microwave oven" from Djajadiningrat and colleagues. Theory points; the example carries the detail.
- **Sources:** paper p. 943 (designers "turn to existing examples"), p. 944 ("ultimate particular"; "theory nexus"; "embody ... do not encode"; point and area; invariances; microwave; "not proposing ... subsume"), p. 945 ("definite facts"; "converse of Alexander's"; "limited to inspiration and annotation"; "Theoretical writing remains important").
