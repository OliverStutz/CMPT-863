# 01 Hook: a coffee table that drifts

**Purpose:** Open with a real object and one question the room has to answer before any theory appears, then reveal that the object's maker wrote the paper and that his critics hold the object up as the exemplar. **Slides:** 1. **Time:** 2:00. **Speaker:** Oliver. **Previous section:** title slide. **Next section:** 02 The fight.

Written 2026-10-21 for version 2 of the deck. Facts reused from `../slides/01-Background.md` and `../slides/10-What-Followed.md`, verified there on 2026-10-19 and 2026-10-20.

## Slide 01.1: A coffee table that drifts. Is this research?

**Time:** 2:00 · **Visual:** Figure 1 of the CHI 2004 Drift Table paper, `../assets/drift-table-chi2004-fig1.png` (author's version from the Goldsmiths repository; the paper states copyright is held by the authors), filling the slide: a low square table on castors, white top, a round porthole in the centre. The headline question in large type over the image. Bottom right, three answer options as three pills: Yes, No, Depends what counts. Nothing else.

**On the slide**
- The Drift Table, 2004: aerial photographs drift in a porthole as you shift weight. No set task. Lived with for weeks.
- **Is this research?**
- Yes · No · Depends what counts

**Speaker notes**
- **Say:** Before the paper, an object. This is the Drift Table, built in 2004 by William Gaver and colleagues, then at the Royal College of Art. Gaver wrote this week's paper. It is a coffee table with a small round screen set into the top. The screen shows aerial photographs of England and Wales, and they drift. Put a heavy book on one side and the landscape slides that way. There is no task to complete. Volunteers lived with it in their homes for weeks, and the team wrote up what people did with it. Hands up: is this research? Yes. No. Depends what counts. [pause] Keep your answer in mind, because I will ask again at the end. Now the odd part. The researchers who later asked design research to adopt standards cite this exact table, in their 2007 paper, as "a well known example of critical design in HCI." The critics themselves hold the table up as good work. The fight is about what rules it should be judged by, and who gets to write them. That is the heart of the paper. It takes ten pages because the answer is not obvious, and because the person writing it has a stake. (about 195 words)
- **Transition:** So who is fighting whom?
- **Watch out:**
  - The Drift Table is not mentioned in Gaver's paper. The CHI 2004 paper gives Gaver's affiliation as the Royal College of Art, with Bowers at KTH Stockholm and co-authors at Lancaster and UCL; the Goldsmiths repository lists it under the Interaction Research Studio. Say "Gaver and colleagues," not "the studio."
  - Call each option once, one hand per person, and jot the three counts on a card. Slide 12 asks the same question; the change is the room's own data. Ask one "depends" voter what it depends on; that is the room's first definition of research.
  - If someone says "no," ask what is missing. The answer (a hypothesis, a control, a measurement) is the critics' position, and you can point back to it on the next slide.
  - If someone says "yes" at once, ask what makes it research rather than product design. That answer is Gaver's position: the situation was chosen for what it could teach.
- **If asked:**
  - *Q: What did the Drift Table study find?* A: The CHI 2004 paper describes it as a study of ludic activities, "motivated by curiosity, exploration, and reflection rather than externally-defined tasks," lived with in volunteers' homes for several weeks. Nine authors, including Gaver, Bowers and Boucher. The table held "about a terabyte of photography of England and Wales"; shifting weights on it changed the view's "apparent height, direction and speed" (CHI 2004, p. 886).
  - *Q: Who cites it as an exemplar?* A: Zimmerman, Forlizzi and Evenson, CHI 2007, p. 496, call it "a well known example of critical design in HCI." Zimmerman and Forlizzi in 2014 write that it "has been held up as one of the earliest and best examples of RtD published within HCI" (Ways of Knowing in HCI, p. 175).
  - *Q: What else did Gaver's group build?* A: The History Tablecloth (DIS 2006), a cloth that glows under objects left on it, as a halo growing over hours; and the Prayer Companion (CHI 2010), a device that showed news and social media items to a convent of cloistered nuns as possible topics for prayer, used for ten months.
  - *Q: Why open with an object the paper never mentions?* A: Because the paper's claim is that the artefact is the definite fact of design research (p. 945). Opening with an artefact tests that claim on the room before Gaver makes it.
